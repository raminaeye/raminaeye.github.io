MGDA (Multiple Gradient Descent Algorithm)

I take the shortest legal blend of the task gradients and step along it. I land on the Pareto front, but I do not pick where.

The intro left us with a fight: two tasks pulling the shared trunk, the shared part of the model that both tasks train, in different directions, and a plain average that just picks a side. This page is the classical answer to that fight. It needs one new idea first: what "best" even means when two tasks trade wins.

When 'best' stops meaning one thing

With one loss, the one number training tries to push down, comparing two models is easy: lower wins. With 2 losses, model A can beat model B on task 1 while losing on task 2. Neither is "better," full stop. So "optimal" needs a new meaning.

The replacement idea is simple: throw out any model that has a free upgrade available, some other model at least as good on every task and strictly better on one. Why stay where a free upgrade exists? What is left after all the free upgrades are used up is the set of defensible trade-offs.

Two tasks, two knobs, one conflict

The conflict, measured: two tasks share two knobs, so every gradient, each task's uphill pull on its loss, is an arrow with two entries, small enough to draw. These numbers are invented, chosen so we can draw everything. The first arrow points east with length 2. The second points west and slightly north, length about 1.1.

Plain training follows the average of the two arrows, drawn dark below. Notice it leans the first task's way: the first arrow is longer, so it pulls the average toward itself.

One method on this page answers that fight directly. MGDA, the Multiple Gradient Descent Algorithm, takes the shortest legal blend of the task gradients and steps along it. MGDA runs as a loop every training step: collect the pulls, take the shortest blend, step, recompute. The next two diagrams set up the problem it solves, and its full treatment follows in the section on stepping along the shortest blend.

The referee's scoring rule, written as an equation: a step in direction d changes task i's loss in proportion to the score below, so a positive score means the step helps that task and a negative score means it hurts it.

Two lines set up the conflict: the referee's score for a step, and the plain average step training follows by default.
⟨gi, d⟩ > 0 ⇒ task i improves
The referee's score for task i is the dot product of its gradient gi with the candidate step d: a step changes task i's loss in proportion to the score, so a positive score means the step helps that task and a negative score means it hurts it. The angle brackets hold the pair whose dot product is the score, and ⇒ reads as implies: a positive score implies the task improves.

The plain training step is half the sum of the two task gradients, and the half means each task gets an equal vote in the sum. But the longer arrow pulls the average toward itself, so the loud task outvotes the quiet one, and the average is just one arbitrary blend of many.

Now the referee scores the step. Task 1's score is +1.00, so the step helps task 1. Task 2's score is -0.38, so the very same step pushes task 2's loss up.

That is the entire conflict problem: the loud task outvotes the quiet one. And the average is just one arbitrary blend of many. There is a whole line of blends between the two arrows, and nothing about the plain average makes it the right one.

The Pareto set is a whole curve of trade-offs

  1. Pareto-dominated: some other point is at least as good on every task and strictly better on one. There is a free upgrade available, so why stay?
  2. Pareto-optimal: nothing dominates it. You cannot improve any task without hurting another. All the free upgrades are used up. The set of all such points is the Pareto set.
  3. Pareto-stationary: some blend of the task gradients cancels out to zero there. Every Pareto-optimal point is Pareto-stationary, just as every single-task minimum has zero gradient.

Stationary is the target: at a Pareto-stationary point, some legal blend of the task gradients has length exactly zero. The pulls cancel.

That condition, written as an equation:

The Pareto-stationary condition: the pulls can cancel exactly.
minw∈Δ ‖Σi wi·gi‖ = 0
Over every legal blend, weights not negative and adding to one, the smallest combined pull is zero: some legal blend of the task gradients has length exactly zero and the pulls can cancel exactly, so no step helps one task without hurting another. Read the glyphs: min names the smallest value, Δ is every legal blend, ∈ means w is a member of Δ, and Σi is the sum over every task i.

Here is the catch: the Pareto set is not one point. It is a whole curve of trade-offs.

A point that sits off the front is dominated: another point is better on both tasks, so moving to it is a free upgrade. A point on the front has no free upgrade.

Moving along the curve helps one task and hurts the other. Picking between the green and blue points is a choice, not a theorem.

Step along the shortest blend

So how do you actually land on the frontier? The classical answer is MGDA, the Multiple Gradient Descent Algorithm. Instead of the fixed average, search over every legal blend of the task gradients, every blend whose weights are not negative and add to one, and step along the shortest one. If the shortest blend has length zero, the pulls cancel and you are Pareto-stationary: stop.

Find the shortest possible blend of the task gradients: choose weights that make the combined pull as small as it can go.

The MGDA step in three lines: find the shortest blend, stop when it is nearly zero, and, for two tasks, every legal blend has one form.
minw∈Δ ‖Σi wi·gi‖2
Find the shortest possible blend of the task gradients: over every legal blend, choose weights that make the squared length of the weighted sum, each task's pull scaled by its blend weight and added up, as small as it can go. Stepping along the shortest blend is how you land on the frontier; if it has length zero, the pulls cancel and you are Pareto-stationary. Read the glyphs: min names the smallest value, Δ is every legal blend, and w∈Δ reads as w is a member of that set.
stop when ‖d*‖ ≈ 0 (Pareto-stationary)
Stop when the shortest blend d* is nearly zero: no direction helps one task without hurting another, so this point is Pareto-stationary.
blend = w·g1 + (1 − w)·g2
For two tasks, every legal blend has one form: one weight w for task 1 and the rest, 1 − w, for task 2, so the two weights always sum to one. That is why a blend tip rides the line between the two arrowheads. At w = 0 the blend is all of the second task's arrow, and at w = 1 it is all of the first task's arrow.

Sener and Koltun start from a blunt observation: multi-task learning is inherently a multi-objective problem, because tasks conflict and force a trade-off, and the standard compromise, a weighted linear combination of per-task losses, bakes the trade-off into fixed weights that are only right when the tasks do not compete, which is rarely true. The textbook MGDA (Désidéri, 2012) provably reaches a Pareto-stationary point, but it needs explicit per-task gradients and does not scale to deep nets. Their contribution is a Frank-Wolfe solver, a cheap solver that works in rounds, each round picking the single task whose pull best matches the direction the blend still needs and shifting the blend a little toward that task, then repeating until the blend stops improving, applied to an upper bound (a stand-in quantity that sits at or above the real one, so working on it is safe) that is computable in a single backward pass, the reverse run through the network that computes a task's gradients, so the overhead is negligible, and they prove it still reaches a Pareto-stationary point under realistic assumptions. Naive MGDA needs one backward pass per task; their bound makes it cheap.

MGDA picks a Pareto point, but not which one

MGDA is proven to reach some Pareto-stationary point. But "some" hides a problem: which point you reach is decided by where you started, not by anything you chose. The average loss, the thing you actually wanted to minimize, is left to luck.

Think of the Pareto set as a menu of all the fair compromises. MGDA only promises to seat you somewhere on the menu; the kitchen picks the dish. Minimizing the average loss is ordering a specific dish. You want the dish you ordered, plus a guarantee that the kitchen is actually cooking it.

Closing that gap, ordering the dish instead of letting the kitchen pick, is the job of the two variants named in this section: Pareto MTL and CAGrad.

One honest caveat before you go: Pareto-stationary is a weaker promise than Pareto-optimal. A blend can cancel to zero at a point that is only a standoff, not a true optimum, the same way a zero gradient holds at a single-task saddle, a point where the slope is zero in every direction but the point is not a bottom, since the surface falls away in some directions and rises in others. MGDA promises a point where the pulls cancel, not a point where no better trade-off exists.

Variants. Pareto MTL (multi-task learning, Lin et al., 2019) refuses to accept wherever the run lands: it steers toward a preferred trade-off you name up front. CAGrad also protects the worst-off task, but it keeps the average loss in view while doing it.

So the frontier is real, and there is a principled way to land on it. But none of this turns the loud task down: task 1's arrow is still nearly twice as long as task 2's, and it still outvotes the quiet one in every average. Knowing the frontier exists is only half the job. That is the question the GradNorm page took on: how do we fix the loudness first?
