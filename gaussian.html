The Gaussian Distribution

The bell curve in one number and the tilted cloud in many: why noise, losses and filters use it, and when its tails are too thin.

1 One bell, one cloud

A Gaussian, or normal distribution, is fixed by a centre and a spread: for one number a mean \(\mu\) and a standard deviation \(\sigma\), for several numbers a mean and a covariance matrix. It shows up so often because a sum of many small independent effects comes out bell-shaped. Training uses it three ways: as noise it adds, as the loss its minus log gives, and as a belief it updates.

In every use it carries only its mean and spread, or its mean and covariance; nothing else about the data is stored.

2 The bell curve

A diffusion model trains by adding noise to images. Take one pixel of that noise, or one sample of the white noise in a reverberation tail: its values pile up near a centre and thin out evenly on both sides. That pile is the bell.

Measure the distance from the centre in spreads; the height falls with the square of that distance.
\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{r} = \dfrac{x - \htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{\mu}}{\htmlStyle{color:#ca6f1e}{\sigma}}\)
\(\mu\) is the centre, \(\sigma\) the spread (the standard deviation), and \(r\), the standard score, counts how many spreads \(x\) sits from the centre, so every bell shares one scale.
\(\displaystyle p(x) = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi \htmlStyle{color:#ca6f1e}{\sigma}^{2}}}\, \exp\!\left(-\dfrac{\htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{r}^{2}}{2}\right)\)
The exponential of minus half \(r^2\) is flat at the centre and drops fast in the tails. The front factor makes the area one, so a wider bell is lower; \(\pi\) is the circle constant. The height is a density, not a chance. \(\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)\) is shorthand for this bell, and \(\mathcal{N}(x \mid \mu, \sigma^2)\) its height at \(x\), as GMM writes it.

The front factor rescales the height so the area is one.

The area of the bell to the left of a value is written \(\Phi\); GELU multiplies each input by the standard bell’s area to its left.

3 Scaling and adding bells

Each step of a diffusion model’s forward process shrinks the image a little and adds fresh noise (Ho et al. 2020). Attention divides its scores by \(\sqrt{d}\), the square root of the key length, to keep their spread steady; LayerNorm and weight initialization (Glorot and Bengio 2010, He et al. 2015) use the same two rules.

Stretching scales the variance by the square; independent bells add into one bell.
\(\displaystyle \operatorname{Var}(cX) = c^{2}\,\operatorname{Var}(X)\)
Stretching by a number \(c\) stretches the standard deviation by \(c\) and the variance by its square. That is why attention divides its scores by \(\sqrt{d}\): a sum of \(d\) independent products, one query entry times one key entry each, has variance growing with \(d\), and the division brings its spread back to one.
\(\displaystyle \mathbb{E}[X_1 + X_2] = \mu_1 + \mu_2\)
For two independent bells \(X_1\) and \(X_2\), the means add.
\(\displaystyle \operatorname{Var}(X_1 + X_2) = \sigma_1^{2} + \sigma_2^{2}\)
The variances add too, not the standard deviations, because independent values share no covariance, and the sum is still a bell (Figure 4).
\(\displaystyle x_t = \sqrt{1-\htmlStyle{color:#ca6f1e}{\beta_t}}\; x_{t-1} + \sqrt{\htmlStyle{color:#ca6f1e}{\beta_t}}\;\varepsilon\)
One step of the diffusion forward process for one pixel \(x\) at step \(t\), with \(\varepsilon\) a standard draw from \(\mathcal{N}(0, 1)\) (Ho et al. 2020, eq. 2, which writes the whole image at once with one independent bell per pixel). The first term shrinks the pixel by the first rule; the second adds fresh noise of variance \(\beta_t\). By the variance rule they add into one bell, so many small steps collapse into a single bell, the paper’s closed form.

The central limit theorem says this goes further: a sum of many small independent effects of almost any shape comes out close to a bell, which is why ICA looks for outputs that are as far from a bell as possible.

4 Drawing from a bell

Diffusion draws fresh noise at every step, DDPG adds a bell-shaped kick to each action to explore, and a Gaussian policy in continuous control draws its action from a bell. All three start from the same standard draw.

Every bell draw is a standard draw, stretched and slid.
\(\displaystyle x = \htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{\mu} + \htmlStyle{color:#ca6f1e}{\sigma}\,\varepsilon\)
\(\varepsilon\) is a draw from the standard bell \(\mathcal{N}(0, 1)\), centre zero and spread one; the tilde in \(\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\) reads “is drawn from”. Multiplying by \(\sigma\) stretches it (section 3) and adding \(\mu\) slides it. Libraries take the standard deviation here, not the variance. The diffusion step’s second term is this with the centre at zero and spread \(\sqrt{\beta_t}\).

5 The loss a bell gives

Builds on losses as minus log-likelihoods (Bernoulli, Categorical and Uniform Distributions, section 5)

A regression head that predicts depth treats its output as the centre of a bell around the true depth. The multitask model of uncertainty weighting goes one step further and learns one spread per task.

The squared error divided by the noise level, plus a charge for claiming that noise level.
\(\displaystyle \mathcal{L} = \dfrac{(y-\htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{\hat{y}})^{2}}{2\htmlStyle{color:#ca6f1e}{\sigma}^{2}} + \ln \htmlStyle{color:#ca6f1e}{\sigma} + \text{const}\)
\(\hat{y}\) is the prediction, used as the bell’s centre, and \(y\) the target, and \(\mathcal{L}\) is the minus log of the bell’s height at the target, \(-\ln \mathcal{N}(y \mid \hat{y}, \sigma^2)\). With \(\sigma\) fixed this is regression’s squared error. With \(\sigma\) learned, a noisy task gets a large \(\sigma\) so its error counts less, and the \(\ln\sigma\) term stops \(\sigma\) growing forever: uncertainty weighting (Kendall, Gal and Cipolla 2018, eq. 5 and eq. 7, which drop the constant, half of \(\ln 2\pi\)).

6 When the bell’s tails are too thin

A bell’s tails fall so fast that moderate and far distances get squeezed together. On a two-dimensional map there is too little room to keep every moderate distance, and with a bell on the map too, the many small pulls between moderately distant points crush the clusters into the middle: t-SNE’s crowding problem (van der Maaten and Hinton 2008). A heavy tail lets a moderate distance in the original space show as a much larger one on the map, so clusters pull apart, as t-SNE does with the Student-t. In a squared-error fit the same thin tails make far points pull hard, because the bell calls them nearly impossible; robust regression uses a heavier-tailed shape so outliers matter less.

A bump like the bell whose tails fall like a power of the distance, not like an exponential.
\(\displaystyle p(x) = \dfrac{1}{\pi\,(1 + x^2)}\)
The Student-t with one degree of freedom, the setting that controls how heavy its tails are; as that setting grows, the curve becomes the bell. \(\pi\) makes the area one.
\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#148f77}{b_{ij}} = \dfrac{1}{1 + \lVert \mathbf{y}_i - \mathbf{y}_j \rVert^{2}}\)
t-SNE’s closeness of map points \(i\) and \(j\): the same curve without \(\pi\), over the distance between their map positions \(\mathbf{y}\).
\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{a_{j\mid i}} = \exp\!\left(-\dfrac{\lVert \mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_j \rVert^{2}}{2\sigma_i^{2}}\right)\)
t-SNE’s bell-shaped closeness of point \(j\) to point \(i\) in the original space, over the distance between their original vectors \(\mathbf{x}\). \(\sigma_i\) is point \(i\)’s own width, tuned per point through t-SNE’s perplexity setting. It dies off far faster than \(b_{ij}\), which is the whole point (Figure 7).

t-SNE then turns both into neighbour chances: each \(a\) divided by its row total and averaged both ways, and each \(b\) divided by the total over all pairs. It moves the map points until the two sets of chances match (t-SNE).

7 The multivariate Gaussian

Builds on the determinant (Vectors and Matrices, section 7) and inverse and rank (Vectors and Matrices, section 8)

In the Olivetti face photos that PCA uses, 400 photos of 64 by 64 pixels, two neighbouring pixels brighten and darken together, so a scatter of their values is a tilted cloud. Each GMM component is one such cloud, and diffusion’s image noise is a round cloud, \(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, I)\), one independent bell per pixel with the identity matrix \(I\) as covariance.

The same bell over several numbers, with distance measured in the cloud’s own units.
\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#148f77}{\Sigma} = \begin{pmatrix} \sigma_1^{2} & \sigma_{12} \\ \sigma_{12} & \sigma_2^{2} \end{pmatrix}\)
The covariance matrix \(\Sigma\), not a sum: its diagonal holds each pixel’s variance and its off-diagonal \(\sigma_{12}\) how the two move together.
\(\displaystyle r^{2} = (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^{\top}\, \htmlStyle{color:#148f77}{\Sigma}^{-1}\, (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})\)
A squared distance in the cloud’s own units. \(\mathbf{x}\) and the mean \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) are columns of numbers, and the \(\top\) lays the first one flat. \(\Sigma^{-1}\), the inverse, undoes the cloud’s stretch, so a step along the tilt is cheap and a step across it is expensive (Figure 8). With one number this is the \(r^2\) of the bell’s height formula in section 2.
\(\displaystyle p(\mathbf{x}) = Z\, \exp\!\left(-\dfrac{r^{2}}{2}\right)\)
The same bell over \(n\) numbers: the height falls with the cloud distance, scaled by a constant \(Z\).
\(\displaystyle Z = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{n} \det \htmlStyle{color:#148f77}{\Sigma}}}\)
\(Z\) makes the volume one. \(\det\Sigma\) is how much \(\Sigma\) stretches area: a spread-out cloud has a lower peak, a narrow one a higher peak. With one number and \(\Sigma = \sigma^2\) it is the front factor of the bell’s height formula in section 2.

The tool sets one cloud and one test point. Compare the gray circle, which ignores the cloud, with the teal ring, which measures in the cloud’s own units, and read the two bars below.

Figure 8. Drag the orange point or the purple handle: across the tilt is far in the cloud’s units, along it is near.

When \(\Sigma\) is diagonal the cloud is not tilted, the density is a product of one-number bells and the coordinates are independent: the diagonal and spherical options of a GMM. For any \(\Sigma\), its eigenvectors find the cloud’s axes.

8 What one value tells you about another

Builds on section 7 and Bayes rule (Probability and Bayes Rule, section 4)

Fix one coordinate of a tilted cloud and look at the other: its best guess slides along the tilt and its spread shrinks. The Wiener filter uses exactly this to guess clean speech \(S\) from what a microphone hears, \(Y\), the speech plus noise.

Knowing one coordinate of a tilted cloud slides the guess of the other along the tilt and narrows it.
\(\displaystyle \xi = \dfrac{\htmlStyle{color:#148f77}{\sigma_S^{2}}}{\htmlStyle{color:#7f8c8d}{\sigma_D^{2}}}\)
The speech-to-noise power ratio: speech power \(\sigma_S^2\) over noise power \(\sigma_D^2\).
\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#148f77}{\Sigma_{SY}} = \begin{pmatrix} \htmlStyle{color:#148f77}{\sigma_S^{2}} & \htmlStyle{color:#148f77}{\sigma_S^{2}} \\ \htmlStyle{color:#148f77}{\sigma_S^{2}} & \htmlStyle{color:#148f77}{\sigma_S^{2}} + \htmlStyle{color:#7f8c8d}{\sigma_D^{2}} \end{pmatrix}\)
\(\Sigma_{SY}\) is the covariance matrix of speech \(S\) and what is heard, \(Y = S + D\) with noise \(D\), where speech and noise are independent and both centred at zero: together they form a tilted cloud. What is heard carries all the speech power plus the noise’s, and the only thing \(S\) and \(Y\) share is \(S\) itself, so the off-diagonal is the speech power.
\(\displaystyle \mathbb{E}[S \mid Y] = \dfrac{\htmlStyle{color:#148f77}{\sigma_S^{2}}}{\htmlStyle{color:#148f77}{\sigma_S^{2}} + \htmlStyle{color:#7f8c8d}{\sigma_D^{2}}}\; Y\)
The best guess of the speech is the heard value scaled by the share of its power that is speech, the same as \(\xi\) over one plus \(\xi\): the Wiener gain, the dashed slope in Figure 9.
\(\displaystyle \operatorname{Var}(S \mid Y) = \dfrac{\htmlStyle{color:#148f77}{\sigma_S^{2}}\,\htmlStyle{color:#7f8c8d}{\sigma_D^{2}}}{\htmlStyle{color:#148f77}{\sigma_S^{2}} + \htmlStyle{color:#7f8c8d}{\sigma_D^{2}}}\)
What stays uncertain after hearing \(Y\) is always less than before: the narrower purple bell in Figure 9.

10 The Gaussian in PyTorch

One bell as a loss, one tilted cloud from real face pixels, and one diffusion step, written with PyTorch.

import torch
from torch.distributions import Normal, MultivariateNormal
from sklearn.datasets import fetch_olivetti_faces

batch = 32                                              # a setting: examples per step
prediction, target = torch.randn(batch), torch.randn(batch)
log_sigma = torch.zeros((), requires_grad=True)
pred = Normal(loc=prediction, scale=log_sigma.exp())    # scale is σ, not σ²
loss = -pred.log_prob(target).mean()

images = torch.from_numpy(fetch_olivetti_faces().images)   # (n_faces, height, width)
mid = images.shape[1] // 2                                 # the middle row and column
pixels = images[:, mid, mid:mid + 2]                       # two neighbouring pixels
pixel_cloud = MultivariateNormal(pixels.mean(0), covariance_matrix=torch.cov(pixels.T))
pair = pixel_cloud.sample()

betas = torch.linspace(1e-4, 0.02, 1000)                # DDPM's linear schedule (Ho et al. 2020)
def diffusion_step(x_prev, beta):
    return torch.sqrt(1 - beta) * x_prev + torch.sqrt(beta) * torch.randn_like(x_prev)
  1. 1The loss of section 5, where prediction and target stand in for a model’s outputs and labels. The noise level is learned in logs, as Kendall et al. do, so it stays positive, and the loss is averaged over the batch.
  2. 2The cloud of section 7, where the pixel position comes from the photos’ own shape. torch.cov treats rows as variables, hence pixels.T; the result is the tilted two-pixel cloud of Figure 8.
  3. 3One forward step, the diffusion line of section 3. torch.sqrt(beta) is the noise’s standard deviation, never beta itself (section 9).
