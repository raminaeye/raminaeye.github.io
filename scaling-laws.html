Scaling Laws

The exchange rate between compute and loss.

The model trains. Now the question is money. Scaling laws describe how test loss, the error on data the model did not train on, falls as you spend more on data, parameters (the numbers inside the model, so its size), or compute. Where does the next dollar go? More data, a bigger model, or more training? That is what this page prices.

The pattern is a power law, found empirically across many domains: the resource you grow is raised to a power.

The shape every law on this page shares: a floor plus a gap that shrinks as a power of the resource.
L = L∞ + A · X−α
L is the test loss, X the resource (data, parameters or compute), L∞ the floor no training run goes under, A the starting size of the gap above it, and α (alpha) the exponent. The minus sign shrinks the gap as X grows; a small α is the brutal exchange rate between compute and loss.
log(L − L∞) = log A − α · log X
Take the log of the gap and the law becomes a straight line in log X with slope minus α. That is why everyone plots on log-log axes, where each equal step multiplies the value by the same factor.

Here is the most expensive straight line in machine learning. Every point on it is a training run someone paid for. The loss falls slowly. Painfully slowly. With an exponent of 0.05, you can 10x your compute and buy two hundredths of a loss point. On linear axes the line bends into a hockey stick. The straightness is not the phenomenon. The straightness is the paper you wrap the phenomenon in.

An illustrative law, with a compute exponent in the style of Kaplan et al. (2020), the first reference below. The chart and the tool below use it; real coefficients come from fitting your own runs.

The compute law: a floor no budget can beat, plus a gap that shrinks as compute grows.
L = 1.5 + 2.0 · C−0.05
The power law with numbers: floor 1.5 that no budget can beat, starting gap 2.0, compute C as the resource, and α = 0.05.
What multiplying the resource buys: the gap shrinks by a fixed factor.
gap aftergap before = k−α
Grow the resource k times and the gap above the floor is multiplied by k to the minus α, whatever the starting point. So absolute gains shrink as you spend more.
10−0.05 ≈ 0.89, (106)−0.05 ≈ 0.50
Compute at α = 0.05: each 10x keeps 89 percent of the gap, and a million-x only halves it.
2−0.5 ≈ 0.71, 2−0.05 ≈ 0.97
Doubling with a data exponent of 0.5 takes a real chunk off the gap; doubling with 0.05 barely dents it.

But wait, how do you estimate the floor? Train on several dataset sizes, adjust a floor guess until the log-log gap is a straight line, then read minus α off the slope. The next section does exactly that.

The illustrative law drawn on log-log axes, from the law stated in this section.

Find the floor by straightening the curve

If more data still improves the test loss, you have not hit the floor, the irreducible error that no training run removes. Only the gap above the floor follows the power law, so the right floor straightens the curve: guess too low and the right end flattens out early, guess too high and the gap goes negative at large data sizes.

The data form of the law: the same floor plus gap shape, over data size instead of compute.
L = 1.5 + 2√D
Five illustrative runs are generated from this curve. 1.5 is the floor, D is the data size, and the square root is why the data exponent here is 0.5.

The fit in code. The straightness |r| is the correlation between log D and log gap: 1 means the points sit exactly on a line.

import numpy as np
# D: dataset sizes, L: test loss of one run per size
def straightness(floor_guess):
    gap = L - floor_guess
    return abs(np.corrcoef(np.log(D), np.log(gap))[0, 1])

guesses = np.linspace(1.30, L.min(), 200)[:-1]  # every gap stays positive
floor = guesses[np.argmax([straightness(g) for g in guesses])]
slope, log_A = np.polyfit(np.log(D), np.log(L - floor), 1)
alpha = -slope

For fixed compute, grow params and data together

Once the line fits, you know three things: the floor, the slope, and the constant A. A high floor means your modality (the kind of data, such as text, images or sensor signals) has a limit. A large exponent such as 0.5 means doubling the data gives a noticeable gain.

One warning: 0.5 is a data exponent, and compute exponents are much smaller, around 0.05. Do not compare alphas across resources. The doubling line in the ratio card at the top (2−0.5 vs 2−0.05) shows the pair. Same math, very different wallets.

The same law works over model size, where the slope tells you if you need a deeper model, and over compute: on a wearable with a compute limit, it gives the best loss reachable inside that limit, what you're capped at.

Joint scaling laws add a params term beside the data term. The bigger term says what to grow: data term bigger means data-limited, and doubling the model buys you almost nothing until you feed it; params term bigger means model-limited, and doubling the data buys almost nothing until you grow the model.

For years the recipe was simple: fix the data, grow the model. Then Hoffmann et al. (2022) trained Chinchilla and called that recipe wasteful: for a fixed compute budget, model size and training tokens (word-pieces of text, the unit the model reads) should scale together, roughly tens of tokens per parameter. The famous run was 70B (billion) params on 1.4T (trillion) tokens.

Compute follows a rule of thumb: about 2 FLOPs per parameter per token on the forward pass (predictions from inputs) and about 4 on the backward pass (gradients from the loss). Compute is your money.

The joint law and its compute rule: loss from params and tokens together, under one budget.
L = 1.69 + 406.4 · N−0.34 + 410.7 · D−0.28
The Chinchilla fit (Hoffmann et al., 2022): N is the parameter count, D the token count, and each term shrinks as its count grows. 1.69 is the floor that neither term shrinks.
C = 6 · N · D
Compute C is about six times the parameter count times the token count, the 2 plus 4 FLOPs per parameter per token.

Hold compute fixed, trade params for tokens, and read where the loss bottoms out:

import numpy as np
C = 1e23                         # fixed budget in FLOPs, as in the tool below
N = np.logspace(8, 12, 400)      # parameter counts to try
D = C / (6 * N)                  # tokens the budget leaves for each N
L = 1.69 + 406.4 * N**-0.34 + 410.7 * D**-0.28
best = np.argmin(L)
print(D[best] / N[best])         # tokens per parameter at the bottom

The bottom of the U is not at 1:1. It sits data-heavy, tens of tokens per parameter. Toward param-heavy the loss climbs: you bought parameters you cannot feed. Go too data-heavy and it climbs again: the model is too small to use all those tokens. The fit is illustrative; your own runs give your own coefficients.

A final honesty note. Scaling laws price smooth loss, but capabilities can jump: emergence is a capability appearing suddenly as the model grows. A loss curve never told anyone the model would suddenly do arithmetic. Use scaling laws to decide where the next dollar goes, not to prophesy what the model will learn.
