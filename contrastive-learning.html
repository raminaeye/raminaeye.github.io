Contrastive Learning

On this page

In Self-Supervised Learning, one image becomes two random views and a shared encoder (the network that turns an image into a vector, its embedding) learns to match them. Contrastive learning adds other images to push away.

Positives pull, negatives push

Each training step sees a batch of images. The two views of one image are a positive pair; every other image is a negative. Embeddings are L2 normalized (scaled to length 1, so they sit on a sphere) and compared by cosine similarity: 1 for the same direction, −1 for opposite. Pull positives together and push negatives apart, and similar images cluster without labels.

One batch becomes one loss

SimCLR (Chen et al. 2020) is the plainest version. Here is its whole pipeline on a toy batch, with one encoder f and one small projection head g shared by all views.

SimCLR's pipeline as a toy with N = 4 images (the paper's default is N = 4096).

The loss picks the true pair out of the batch

A batch of N images gives 2N views, and each view must pick its twin out of the other 2N−1. The loss is NT-Xent (normalized temperature-scaled cross entropy): cross entropy, the standard loss for picking the right answer from a set, with the twin as the right answer.

The loss: how sure the model is that each view's twin beats every other view.
ℓi,j = −log pi,j
The loss of view i is minus the log of pi,j, the probability given to its twin j: near 0 when the twin wins clearly.
pi,j = exp(sim(zi,zj)τ)Σk≠i exp(sim(zi,zk)τ)
z is a view's embedding and sim is cosine similarity. The top scores view i against its twin; the bottom adds that score for every view k except i itself. The fraction is a softmax (scores turned into probabilities that sum to 1), and the temperature τ sharpens or softens it.
L = 12N Σi=1..2N ℓi,t(i)
Every view takes one turn, so training minimizes the average over all 2N views; t(i) is the twin of view i.

View i is left out of its own sum because its self-similarity is always 1. A low τ focuses the push on hard negatives, the wrong views closest to view i (good values: Self-Supervised Learning Failure Modes and Checks). In code:

def nt_xent_loss(z1, z2, temperature=0.1):  # SimCLR's best on ImageNet at 100 epochs
    # z1, z2: [N, D] projected views, already L2 normalized
    N = z1.size(0)
    z = torch.cat([z1, z2], dim=0)          # [2N, D]
    sim = torch.mm(z, z.t()) / temperature  # [2N, 2N] cosine similarity
    labels = torch.cat([torch.arange(N, 2 * N), torch.arange(N)]).to(z.device)
    sim.fill_diagonal_(-9e15)               # skip k = i: a view never picks itself
    return F.cross_entropy(sim, labels)     # softmax, negative log, average

The twin becomes the nearest neighbor early; the loss keeps falling while the others spread out, then levels off.

Augmentations decide what is learned

SimCLR edits every view with a random crop resized back to full size (plus a random flip), color distortion and Gaussian blur. On ImageNet (about 1.28 million photos, labels unused), scored with a linear probe, no single edit was enough; crop plus color distortion stood out. Crops of one photo share the same mix of colors, so with crops alone the network matches color histograms (how often each color appears) and ignores shapes. Color distortion removes that shortcut:

import torchvision.transforms as T
augment = T.Compose([
    T.RandomResizedCrop(size),                 # random crop, resized back to size
    T.RandomHorizontalFlip(),
    T.RandomApply([T.ColorJitter(0.8*s, 0.8*s, 0.8*s, 0.2*s)], p=0.8),  # s: strength
    T.RandomGrayscale(p=0.2),                  # sometimes drop the color
    T.RandomApply([T.GaussianBlur(k)], p=0.5), # k: odd kernel, about 10% of size
    T.ToTensor(),
])
view1, view2 = augment(img), augment(img)     # two random views of one PIL image

Weak edits teach nothing, because matching pixels is enough. Strong edits force the model to match content, until the crops share no scene and the positive pair is a lie.

A throwaway head protects the features

The projection head g is a small MLP (multilayer perceptron: two linear layers with a ReLU, which zeroes negative values, between them). It maps the encoder's output h to z, the vector the loss compares, and is thrown away after training. The loss makes z ignore what augmentations change, such as color; g absorbs that, so a linear probe on h beats one on z by more than 10%.

A queue replaces the giant batch

SimCLR's negatives come from the batch, 2(N−1) per positive pair: only 510 at a batch of 256. Its default batch of 4096 is too big even for a high-end machine with 8 GPUs (graphics processors), by the MoCo v2 authors' estimate. MoCo (Momentum Contrast, He et al. 2020) keeps recent batches' embeddings as negatives in a queue instead. The trained view is the query, its twin is the positive key, and 65,536 queued keys are the negatives, at a batch of 256. The loss is the NT-Xent loss above, which MoCo calls InfoNCE (after van den Oord et al. 2018), with τ = 0.07.

Queued keys came from older weights, so MoCo computes keys with a momentum encoder that gets no gradients and trails the query encoder as an exponential moving average (EMA):

The momentum update: the key encoder trails the query encoder slowly.
pk ← m·pk + (1−m)·pq
After every step the key encoder's weights pk keep the share m of themselves and take the rest from the query encoder's weights pq. With m = 0.999 the key encoder barely moves, so queued keys stay consistent.

The slow copy matters: m = 0.999 beat m = 0.9, and copying every step (m = 0) did not converge. MoCo v2 (Chen et al. 2020) added SimCLR's MLP head and blur and, at a batch of 256, beat SimCLR's large-batch result (see the results table on Self-Supervised Learning). The same pull and push also matches photos to their captions in From BLIP to the Q-Former.
