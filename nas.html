Neural Architecture Search (NAS)

On this page

1 Search space, search strategy and evaluation 2 How the search works 3 Search cost of each method 4 DARTS in practice 5 Pitfalls 6 Combining NAS with distillation 7 References

Designing architectures by hand is slow, it is biased toward whatever worked last time, and much of it is folklore: a layer made wider here, a skip connection added there (a path that carries a layer's input past other layers and adds it back).

Neural architecture search is the admission that a search can do this job. Instead of hand-picking layers, define what is allowed to change, how to explore the options, and how to score candidates cheaply, then let the search run.

NAS runs once, before the final model is trained. The search is its own job, from about 2,000 GPU-days for NASNet down to a few GPU-days for DARTS (see Search costs), and its output is an architecture, which is then trained from scratch like any hand-designed network. Inside the search, the state is the strategy's memory: a population for evolution, a controller network updated after every scored child for reinforcement learning, or architecture weights updated on every step for differentiable search.

1 Search space, search strategy and evaluation

Every NAS paper is the same three pieces with different details:

  • The search space sets what may change: kernel sizes (a kernel is the small window of weights a convolution slides over the input), depths, widths, block types, skip connections.
  • The search strategy sets how to explore it: random sampling, evolution, reinforcement learning, or gradients.
  • The evaluation strategy sets how to score a candidate without training it to convergence (until its accuracy stops improving), because that costs too much.

Get these wrong and thousands of GPU hours go into rediscovering ResNet (a well known hand-designed network built around skip connections).

2 How the search works

2.1 The search space

Say each of L layers picks one op out of k candidates. Every layer multiplies the possibilities, giving N architectures.

The number of architectures is the choices per layer raised to the number of layers.
N = kL
Each of the L layers chooses independently among k ops, so every added layer multiplies the count by k. Repeated multiplication is a power, which is why N grows exponentially with depth: one more layer is k times more work for brute force.

Eight layers with five ops each already give 58 = 390,625 architectures, so brute force is not a plan.

So the real question is not the size of the space, it is the search strategy.

2.2 Four search strategies

  1. Random search samples architectures and keeps the best. It is the baseline every NAS paper should beat, and sometimes they barely do.
  2. Evolution keeps a population, drops the worst, and adds a mutated copy of the best: no gradients, no controller, and it parallelizes well because every member trains independently. AmoebaNet uses a variant called aging evolution, which removes the oldest member instead of the worst.
  3. Reinforcement learning trains a controller network that proposes architectures and learns from their scores (Learning to propose).
  4. Differentiable search turns the choice into weights that gradient descent can tune (Searching with gradients).

The first two fit in a few lines on a toy space where the answer is known: 200 architectures with fixed true accuracies, so "training" one is a lookup. score stands in for training an architecture briefly, and mutate moves it up to 15 places along the toy space.

import random

N, BUDGET = 200, 100
acc = [random.random() for _ in range(N)]

# stands in for training
def score(a):
    return acc[a]

def mutate(a):
    step = random.randint(-15, 15)
    # stay inside the space
    return min(N - 1, max(0, a + step))

samples = random.choices(range(N), k=BUDGET)
best_random = max(samples, key=score)
  1. 1A toy space of 200 architectures; score looks up a stored accuracy instead of training.
  2. 2mutate moves to a nearby architecture, at most 15 places away.
  3. 3Random search: score 100 random picks and keep the best.

Evolution spends the same budget on a population of 8: each round sorts the members by score and replaces the worst with a mutated copy of the best.

BUDGET is the number of architectures each strategy may train and score. Raising it lets every strategy get closer to the best architecture at proportionally more GPU time; lowering it saves time but leaves every strategy further from the best. The toy uses 100.

pop = random.choices(range(N), k=8)
for _ in range(BUDGET):
    # worst first
    pop.sort(key=score)
    pop[0] = mutate(pop[-1])
best_evo = max(pop, key=score)
  1. 1Starts a population of 8 random architectures.
  2. 2Each round replaces the worst member with a mutated copy of the best.
  3. 3Keeps the best architecture found.

The mutation step, at most 15 places here, sets how far a child can land from its parent. A larger step explores more of the space but often jumps off a good peak; a smaller step refines the current peak but can stay stuck on a small one. 15 is this page's choice, not a published setting.

One more thing: NAS can only find what the search space contains. If the good design is not in the search space, no strategy will invent it. So the designer's choices still shape the result, now through the search space.

2.3 Reinforcement-learning controllers

Reinforcement learning is the NASNet approach. A controller, usually a small RNN (recurrent neural network, which emits its choices one at a time while remembering earlier ones), samples architectures. Each one is trained, and its validation accuracy (accuracy on held-out data the network did not train on) comes back as a reward.

The search itself is one loop: the controller proposes, the child network trains and gets scored, the score scales the update, repeat.

The mapping from RL to NAS is direct:

RL conceptNAS version
AgentThe controller (an RNN)
ActionPicking an architectural choice, e.g. kernel size 3, 5, or 7
EnvironmentTraining and evaluating the sampled network
RewardValidation accuracy, sometimes minus a latency penalty, latency being the time one prediction takes on the target hardware
PolicyHow the controller samples its choices

The controller is a policy: for each choice it gives every option a probability, written π(a) for the option a it actually picked. The update rule is REINFORCE: push up the log-probability of each picked option in proportion to the reward R, the child's validation accuracy. The first NAS compares R with a baseline, a running average of past rewards, so only better-than-usual children get pushed up; NASNet uses PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization, which limits how far one update can move the policy), with the same intuition.

The controller pushes up the log-probability of what it picked, scaled by the reward.
∇J ≈ R · ∇log π(a)
The update ∇J is the reward R times ∇log π(a), the direction that makes the picked action a more likely. Multiplying by R sets how hard to push: a high reward makes the pick much more likely, a low one barely moves it. It is approximate because one sampled child stands in for the average over all architectures; subtracting a baseline, the running average reward, makes only better-than-usual picks gain.
∇J the controller's updateR the reward; ∇log π(a) the chosen action's pull· times; ≈ approximately; ∇ gradient, the direction of steepest change

The listing is that update on a one-layer toy: the controller's logits choose among kernels 3, 5 and 7, the reward is a lookup table perf standing in for real training, and the loss line is REINFORCE (negated, because the optimizer minimizes). It runs without a baseline.

import torch
from torch.distributions import Categorical

# kernels 3, 5, 7
perf = torch.tensor([0.70, 0.90, 0.60])
logits = torch.nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(3))
opt = torch.optim.Adam([logits], lr=0.1)
for step in range(50):
    dist = Categorical(logits=logits)
    action = dist.sample()
    reward = perf[action]
    logp = dist.log_prob(action)
    loss = -logp * reward
    opt.zero_grad()
    loss.backward()
    opt.step()
  1. 1Stored rewards for kernels 3, 5 and 7, and the controller's logits over them.
  2. 2The controller samples a kernel and receives its reward.
  3. 3REINFORCE update: raise the log-probability of the sampled kernel in proportion to its reward.

Across runs the probability mass drifts toward kernel 5, usually within the 50 steps. Without a baseline it is noisy and some runs wander first, the variance problem (results spreading out from run to run) the baseline fixes. Swap the lookup table for real training and the loop becomes NASNet's controller, updated once per scored child.

The first NAS (Zoph and Le 2017) trained every sampled child network for 50 epochs. NASNet (Zoph et al. 2018) cut the cost by searching for small cells instead of whole networks.

An architecture found on a small dataset also did not necessarily scale to a big one. The fix: search for two cells instead of whole networks, on CIFAR-10 (60,000 tiny labeled images), a cheap stand-in for ImageNet (the large standard image-classification dataset). 2.4 Differentiable search (DARTS)

Differentiable search (DARTS) makes the search itself trainable by gradient descent. RL and evolution treat the space as discrete: sample, train, score, repeat, thousands of GPU-days. DARTS makes the search differentiable with a continuous relaxation: every edge between nodes holds all candidate ops at once, and its output ō(x) is their weighted blend, with one learnable architecture weight αo per op o.

Each DARTS edge runs every candidate op at once and blends the outputs by a softmax vote.
ō(x) = Σo eαoΣo′ eαo′ · o(x)
The edge's output is a weighted average of every op's output o(x). The weights come from a softmax over the architecture weights: exp makes each αo positive and dividing by the sum makes the shares add to 1, with a bigger α getting an exponentially bigger share. Because the blend is smooth in α, gradient descent can tune the vote like any other weight.
final op = argmaxo αo
At the end each edge keeps only the op with the biggest vote, which turns the soft blend back into one concrete architecture. Replace a hard choice with a soft average, optimize it with gradients, then harden it.

The listing is one DARTS edge with three candidate ops on c channels: alpha holds the architecture weights α, and forward is the blend line of the card. After the search, the kept op is block.ops[block.alpha.argmax()].

class DartsBlock(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, c):
        super().__init__()
        self.ops = nn.ModuleList([
            nn.Conv2d(c, c, 3, padding=1),
            nn.Conv2d(c, c, 5, padding=2),
            nn.Identity(),
        ])
        a = torch.zeros(3)
        self.alpha = nn.Parameter(a)

    def forward(self, x):
        w = F.softmax(self.alpha, dim=0)
        outs = [op(x) for op in self.ops]
        # weighted sum of all ops
        return sum(wi * o for wi, o in zip(w, outs))
  1. 1Three candidate operations and one architecture weight α per operation.
  2. 2Softmax over α, then the weighted sum of every operation's output.

There are now two things to learn: the architecture weights α and the ordinary network weights w. DARTS nests them as a bilevel optimization, with training loss Ltrain and validation loss Lval.

The architecture is chosen for how well its trained network does on held-out data, and training happens inside that choice.
minα Lval(w*(α), α)
The outer problem picks the architecture weights α whose trained network does best on the validation set. It uses validation data because judging an architecture on the data its weights were fit to would reward memorizing, not a good design.
w*(α) = argminw Ltrain(w, α)
The inner problem trains the network weights w on the training set for a fixed architecture. Its answer w*(α), the trained weights, depends on α, which is what ties the two levels together.
w′ = w − ξ ∇w Ltrain(w, α)
Training to convergence inside every outer step would cost a full training run per step, so DARTS stands in one gradient step with step size ξ (xi) and updates α as if w′ were the fully trained weights. It is approximate, but it searches in days instead of years.
Outer loop: architecture weights α
minimize validation loss over α
↓ fix α, train the weights ↓
Inner loop: network weights w
minimize training loss over w, with α fixed
↑ evaluate on validation, update α ↑

The outer loop tunes α on validation data; the inner loop trains w with α fixed.

DARTS acts on every search step and alternates the two levels, one batch each. The listing is the simplest, first-order form, where α's gradient skips how w′ depends on α: net is a network built from DartsBlocks, its parameters split by name into the architecture weights and the network weights, each with its own optimizer.

alphas, weights = [], []
for n, p in net.named_parameters():
    if n.endswith("alpha"):
        alphas.append(p)
    else:
        weights.append(p)
w_opt = torch.optim.SGD(weights, lr=0.01)
a_opt = torch.optim.Adam(alphas, lr=1e-3)
batches = zip(train_loader, val_loader)
for (x_tr, y_tr), (x_val, y_val) in batches:
    # alpha on validation data
    a_opt.zero_grad()
    val_loss = F.cross_entropy(net(x_val), y_val)
    val_loss.backward()
    a_opt.step()
    # w on training data
    w_opt.zero_grad()
    loss = F.cross_entropy(net(x_tr), y_tr)
    loss.backward()
    w_opt.step()
  1. 1Splits parameters into architecture weights α and network weights w, each with its own optimizer.
  2. 2Updates α on a validation batch.
  3. 3Updates w on a training batch.

2.5 Weight sharing in a supernet

Training every candidate from scratch is how searches reach thousands of GPU-days. Most modern NAS methods rely on weight sharing: one giant supernet contains every candidate as a sub-network, and one subnet is one path through the layers:

green = one subnet, one op per layer
Layer 1
conv 3x3
conv 5x5
skip
Layer 2
conv 3x3
conv 5x5
skip
Layer 3
conv 3x3
conv 5x5
skip

All subnets train on the same shared weights, which is what makes the search affordable.

The supernet trains by sampling one path per step and updating only that path's weights. Every op still gets trained, because over many steps every op gets sampled many times. The listing is a one-layer supernet: three convolutions share the layer, and choice picks the path.

class SuperNet(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__()
        self.ops = nn.ModuleDict({
            "k3": nn.Conv2d(3, 16, 3, padding=1),
            "k5": nn.Conv2d(3, 16, 5, padding=2),
            "k7": nn.Conv2d(3, 16, 7, padding=3),
        })
        self.fc = nn.Linear(16, 10)

    def forward(self, x, choice):
        h = F.relu(self.ops[choice](x))
        # average over height and width
        pooled = h.mean(dim=(2, 3))
        return self.fc(pooled)
  1. 1One supernet holds every candidate kernel, sharing one classifier.
  2. 2The forward pass runs only the chosen path.

Training samples a fresh path on every step, so each step updates one convolution and the shared head.

supernet = SuperNet()
params = supernet.parameters()
opt = torch.optim.SGD(params, lr=0.01)
for x, y in train_loader:
    # one path per batch
    choice = random.choice(["k3", "k5", "k7"])
    out = supernet(x, choice)
    loss = F.cross_entropy(out, y)
    opt.zero_grad()
    loss.backward()
    opt.step()
  1. 1Builds the supernet and one optimizer for all shared weights.
  2. 2Samples one path per batch and computes its loss.
  3. 3Updates only the weights that path used.

Once the supernet is trained, score subnets with the shared weights, pick the winner, and always retrain the winner from scratch: the shared weights are good enough for ranking candidates, not for final accuracy.

Read the search costs as the real story. RL and evolution found great architectures and cost thousands of GPU-days. Then DARTS got comparable results for about 4 GPU-days, and the field never looked back. That cost collapse is why weight sharing won.

The most famous product of this lineage is EfficientNet-B0: 77.1% top-1 on ImageNet with 5.3M params. It came from an RL search over a compact mobile search space whose reward traded accuracy against FLOPs (floating-point operations, the raw count of arithmetic the network does). After the search found B0, they stopped searching and scaled it up with compound scaling:

One coefficient grows depth, width and resolution together.
d = aφ,  w = bφ,  r = cφ
The multipliers d, w and r scale the network's depth, width and input resolution. Each is a fixed ratio (a, b, c, found by a small search on B0) raised to the same power φ (phi), so one coefficient grows all three in a fixed balance instead of stretching one dimension until it stops helping. Turning φ up gives B1 through B7.

4 DARTS in practice

A DARTS search is its own training job, run before the real model exists. The first-order loop is one complete search step: one batch updates the weights w, one held-out batch updates the architecture weights α. After the search, keep the strongest op on each edge and train that network from scratch with an ordinary loop.

  • Each step costs about two forward and backward passes, one per level, and every edge runs all its candidate ops, so memory grows with the number of candidates. That is why the search runs on a small proxy, 8 cells with 16 initial channels, and the final network is larger.
  • The official search script uses 50 epochs at batch size 64, SGD for w at learning rate 0.025 with cosine decay to 0.001, momentum 0.9, weight decay 3e-4 and gradient clipping at 5, and Adam for α at learning rate 3e-4 with weight decay 1e-3. Half of the CIFAR-10 training set trains w and the other half scores α (quark0/darts, cnn/train_search.py).
  • The two learning rates set how fast each level moves. Raising α's lets one op pull ahead before the weights have learned what each op can do; lowering it leaves the op weights close to uniform when the search ends, so the final pick rests on tiny differences. Raising w's trains the shared network faster but makes each step noisier, the usual SGD trade-off. The repository keeps α slow, 3e-4 against 0.025.
  • The search's validation accuracy does not predict the final accuracy; the repository says the chosen cell must be retrained from scratch at full size.
  • Different seeds end in different cells, so the authors repeat the search with several seeds and keep the best cell after a short retraining.

5 Pitfalls

  • Search cost is real. The thousands of GPU-days in Search costs are not typos. Without a cluster, RL and evolution searches are out of reach. This is exactly why the field moved to weight sharing.
  • The supernet ranking is noisy. The supernet's ranking of architectures does not always match the true ranking after retraining from scratch. The shared weights couple everything together: an op can look good because it co-adapted with its neighbors (they adjusted to each other's quirks while sharing weights), not because it is good on its own. Treat the search as a rough filter, not an oracle. And random search with early stopping (judging a candidate after a short training run) is embarrassingly competitive with several published NAS methods.
  • DARTS collapses toward skip connections. Skip connections train fast early in the search, so the architecture weights drift toward them, and you can end up with a "discovered" cell that is mostly skip connections. Such a cell computes almost nothing, so the network is effectively shallow. If you run DARTS and the final cell is all skips, that is not a discovery, that is a bug. Fixes exist: DARTS+ stops the search before the skip collapse sets in, FairDARTS gives each op its own sigmoid weight instead of a share of one softmax (the FairDARTS card after this list), or just penalize skips in the score. Watch for it.
  • Reproducibility is shaky. Run the same search twice with different seeds (a seed is the fixed random starting setting that makes a run reproducible) and you can get two different architectures. Report the seed, report the variance, and be suspicious of papers that report one lucky run.
  • Hand design still wins sometimes. Small budget, weird hardware, a brand new domain: a human with one good idea beats a cluster searching the wrong space. And the search-space limit applies again: NAS cannot invent an op that is not in your search space.
FairDARTS replaces the competing softmax shares with independent sigmoid weights, which takes away the skip connection's head start.
wo = eαoΣo′ eαo′
DARTS weighs each op o by a softmax over the scores α. The shares must add to 1, so the ops compete: a skip connection that trains fast grabs share from every other op.
wo = σ(αo) = 11 + e−αo
FairDARTS uses the sigmoid σ instead, which squashes each score into a value between 0 and 1 on its own. With no shared total, a strong skip takes nothing from the other ops.

6 Combining NAS with distillation

NAS finds the architecture. Knowledge Distillation then teaches that small model to behave like a big one: two halves of the same goal, big-model accuracy on a small-model budget. Pruning and Quantization shrink whatever comes out further. The REINFORCE controller is the policy gradient from RL Basics.
