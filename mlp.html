Multilayer Perceptrons (MLP)

When a line is not enough.

One straight line cannot separate the XOR blobs

Here is a question I love. Four blobs of dots at the corners of a square. Top-left and bottom-right are blue, top-right and bottom-left are orange: the classic XOR layout. Your model gets one straight cut. One line. Can it separate them?

No. And that is not a trick, it is the whole point. Draw any straight line you like: it always strands two same-color blobs on opposite sides. Logistic regression, the straight-line model that turns a weighted sum of the inputs into a yes or no probability, can only draw one straight line, and on this problem it manages 51.8% on the training points and 47.0% on the test points, no better than a coin flip. If your data will not sit still for a straight cut, your model needs bends.

The blobs are not random decoration. Four Gaussian blobs, bell shaped clouds of points clustered around a center, in an XOR layout, 1,200 points, 8% of the labels flipped: the same blob family the notebook for kNN (k-nearest neighbors, the classifier that labels a point by the majority label of its k closest points) builds with make_classification, scikit-learn's synthetic-data maker (labeled Gaussian blobs, here two clusters per class). Everything on this page trains on those blobs.

ReLU adds the bend: negative in, zero out

The fix starts with the humblest possible bend: the ReLU.

Read it: negative in, zero out. Positive in, straight through. It is a hinge. Flat on the left, a ramp on the right, one kink at zero.

Now here is the move. A sum of hinges is a piecewise line, and you get to choose where every kink goes. A plain line stalls with the error stuck high (a line is a line, it cannot bend). Hinges bend the fit down toward the curve.

A sum of hinges is a piecewise line, and you get to choose where every kink goes.
ReLU(x) = max(0, x)
Read it: max(0, x) takes the larger of 0 and x, so negative in, zero out. Positive in, straight through. It is a hinge, flat on the left, a ramp on the right, one kink at zero.
f(x) = a + Σk wk ReLU(x − tk)
The fitted curve f(x) starts at height a, then adds one hinge per k: each hinge is shifted so its kink sits at tk and scaled by wk, how hard that hinge bends. Each added hinge is one more bend the fit is allowed to make, so the sum can bend down toward the curve a plain line cannot follow.

Choose where the kinks go, fit the least-squares polyline for that bend budget, the polyline that makes the average squared gap between the fit and the curve as small as it can go, and read the fit error, the fit MSE (mean squared error), that same average squared gap. Same data, same least-squares fit. Each added hinge is one more bend the fit is allowed to make, so add hinges to lower the error.

How to play

  • The point: A curve is just line pieces with bends. Every ReLU is one bend, and stacking more bends lets the fit hug the dashed parabola tighter.
  • Hinges: Draws the best fit for that bend budget with the hinges at fixed, evenly spaced positions. With 1 hinge that fit still misses badly; with 8 the blue line and the dashed curve are barely distinguishable.

That is the whole idea of a neural network, minus the buzzwords. One hinge bends once. A layer of hinges bends everywhere at once, each in its own direction. Stack layers and the bends compose into curves, curls, and eventually the boxy little fences around our four blobs.

Hinges with learned positions

A one-hidden-layer MLP is just hinges with learned positions and learned weights. One perceptron is a single unit of the kind this page stacks: it mixes its inputs with learned weights, adds a bias, a learned number that shifts the sum up or down, and passes the result through the bend. Working inside out: the first layer draws a pile of straight lines, one per hidden unit. The ReLU bends each one at zero. The second layer mixes the bent pieces into the final answer.

One gotcha before the fun part, and it is the reason the notebook's two-linear-layers model flopped. Two linear layers stacked with no ReLU between them collapse on contact:

It collapses into one matrix and one bias. Linear of linear is still linear, a fancier straight line, still useless on the blobs. The bend is not decoration. It is the entire source of power.

One point's trip through the network

You have seen the idea from the outside. Now walk one point through it from the inside: two inputs, two hidden units, one output.

The hidden layer votes: mix the inputs with learned weights, add a bias, and silence the negative votes with ReLU. The output layer decides: mix the hidden votes again and squash into a probability.

One trip through the net in two lines: the hidden layer votes and bends, then the output layer votes and squashes.
h = ReLU(W₁x+b₁)
The hidden layer votes: mix the inputs x with the learned weights W₁, add the bias b₁, and silence the negative votes with ReLU. That bend is the entire source of power: without it the two layers collapse into one matrix and one bias, still linear, still useless on the blobs.
ŷ = σ(W₂h+b₂)
The output layer decides: the result ŷ (y-hat, the predicted score) is σ (sigma, the sigmoid squash) applied to the hidden votes h mixed by the second layer weights W₂ plus its bias b₂. Squashed to a probability, that number is the point's class call.
  1. One point from a blue blob enters the network.
  2. Each hidden unit casts its vote: the two sums land.
  3. ReLU bends each vote: the negative one goes silent, the positive one passes through.
  4. The output unit adds the surviving votes into one number.
  5. Squashed to a probability: blue wins.

How to play

  • The point: one input point, one trip through the layers: the inputs light up, each hidden unit casts its vote, ReLU silences the negative vote, the output adds the survivors, and the final number picks blue.
  • Each step lights only the nodes and edges carrying the signal so far, ending with the winning class.
  • A toy net with hand-picked weights, not the trained XOR model; the steps are the same for any MLP.

Width and depth control model capacity

Capacity is how complicated a boundary the model can draw, its room to bend. Width and depth set it: more units in a layer and more layers both give the model more room.

I trained all four offline in NumPy, the numeric library the training code ran in, plain Adam, the usual adaptive gradient optimizer that gives each weight its own step size based on recent gradients, no tricks, and froze each model's boundary so you can sweep through them.

The four stops, in dial order: linear: one straight cut. Small MLP (128 to 64): a smooth curve. Overkill (512 down to 32): the crazy boundary. Overkill + L2 (a tax on big weights): tamed.

The dial tells the story in four stops. It opens with a straight cut: it cannot bend, so it fails on the four-blob pattern. A small MLP draws a smooth four-way split: it learns the XOR pattern and mostly ignores the flipped labels. Push capacity further and the overkill net scores even higher on train, the data it learned from, while its score on test, held out data it never trained on, lags behind, but its boundary grows little islands around the mislabeled points. It memorized the noise. That is overfitting you can see.

The last stop keeps the same giant net but taxes the weights with L2, and the boundary relaxes back to the clean answer. Capacity is not the enemy; untaxed capacity is.

How to play

  • The point: Drag the dial and watch the boundary: too simple misses the bend, too flexible memorizes the noise, and the L2 tax irons it back.
  • Capacity: At 3 the boundary wiggles around the mislabeled points; at 4 the L2 tax irons the wiggles out.

Penalize the weights that go wild

So the overkill model memorized the blobs and drew a boundary that wiggles around individual noisy points. That wiggle is the whole disease: the weights grew huge so the model could bend sharply around every flipped label. Regularization is a tax on weights, and there are two classic tax codes: L1, a flat tax per unit of weight, and L2, a progressive tax that punishes big weights quadratically.

L1 is a flat tax per unit of weight. Small weights cost more than they are worth, so they get fired to exactly zero. That is why L1 gives you sparse models, models where most weights sit at exactly zero and only a few do the work.

Dropout is the other classic trick: randomly switch off neurons during training, so the net can never lean on one hero neuron and has to learn redundant paths. Same spirit, different tax.

Pick the bend that fits the job

ReLU is the default hidden-layer bend because it is cheap and trains well. But it is not the only bend, and different jobs want different ones.

ReLU: negative in, zero out. The default hidden bend, but a neuron can die forever on negative inputs.

Leaky ReLU: lets a little through when negative, so neurons never fully die.

Sigmoid: squashes everything to 0 to 1; the classic for yes-or-no outputs.

Tanh: squashes to -1 to 1, centered at zero, so it trains faster than sigmoid.

Pick the bend that fits the job: each bend reads its input its own way.
ReLU(x) = max(0, x)
max(0, x) takes the larger of 0 and x, so negative in, zero out. The default hidden bend, but a neuron can die forever on negative inputs.
Leaky ReLU(x) = x when x ≥ 0, 0.1x when x < 0
The line passes x unchanged when x ≥ 0 (at least zero) and passes 0.1x when x < 0 (below zero), so it lets a little through when negative and neurons never fully die. The 0.1 is the leak slope the figure itself uses.
sigmoid(x) = 11 + e−x
The value is 1 divided by 1 + e−x, so it squashes everything to 0 to 1, the classic for yes-or-no outputs. As x grows the e−x term fades and the value nears 1; as x falls it nears 0. Here x is the input to the bend and e is the exponential base inside the sigmoid code.

Tanh squashes to -1 to 1, centered at zero.

Outputs get their own bends: sigmoid for yes-or-no, softmax, which turns the scores for all the classes into probabilities that add up to 1, when the classes are many, and the transformer crowd mostly uses GELU (Gaussian error linear unit, a smoother bend that lets small negative values leak through slightly instead of cutting them to zero, and passes large positive values through almost unchanged) now. Same idea every time: pick the bend that fits the job.

What this shows

  • The point: four classic bends, side by side. Every one is flat or kinked somewhere, and that kink is what lets a network bend its boundary.
