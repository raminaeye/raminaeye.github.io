Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG)

DQN (Deep Q-Network) picks buttons. DDPG turns dials.

DQN's world is buttons. The banana agent had four: forward, back, left, right. Score every move, pick the biggest, done. That works beautifully when the menu is short.

But here is the question that breaks it: how hard do you twist the shoulder joint? Not which button. How much. A torque is any real number between -1 and 1, and there are infinitely many of them. You cannot argmax over infinity (picking the move with the biggest score). I hit this exact wall in my RF-DDPG repo (my DDPG solution for the Unity Reacher task), where a double-jointed arm has to hold its hand on a floating target in a Unity ML-Agents task. The fix: an actor that outputs the move directly, and a critic that judges it.

The interactive toys on this page are from-scratch JavaScript re-enactments at toy scale. The environment facts, network sizes, hyperparameters (settings chosen before training, not learned) and training curve come straight from my RF-DDPG repo.

The arm must output continuous torques

The Reacher environment is a double-jointed arm bolted to a table, one segment driven by the shoulder and one by the elbow. The job is to keep the hand on a small floating ball for a whole episode, not to touch it once.

Every step the agent gets thirty-three numbers: how bent each joint is, how fast it swings, where the segments are, and where the target sits. It never sees the arm, only those numbers.

It answers with four torques, one dial per joint axis, each any real number from -1 to 1. That is a continuous action space: infinitely many settings, recomputed every step.

Episodes run a fixed number of steps. A step with the hand on the target pays +0.1, any other step pays 0, and the episode score is the total.

I trained twenty identical arms in parallel in one Unity build. Unity runs the physics; Python runs the networks and collects all twenty streams at once. Here env is the Unity build and agent holds the networks:

env_info = env.reset(train_mode=True)[brain_name]
states = env_info.vector_observations       # shape (20, 33): 20 arms, 33 numbers each
scores = np.zeros(20)
while True:
    actions = agent.act(states)               # shape (20, 4): 4 torques per arm, in [-1, 1]
    env_info = env.step(actions)[brain_name]  # Unity moves all twenty arms
    scores += env_info.rewards                # 0.1 on target, else 0
    states = env_info.vector_observations
    if np.any(env_info.local_done):
        break
The bar for solved: the twenty-arm average, held over a hundred episodes.
average score ≥ 30
That is the solved bar. Each episode score is averaged over the twenty arms, then over the last 100 episodes.
on-target steps ≈ 300.1 = 300
At +0.1 a step, 30 points means holding the target for roughly 300 steps per episode, on average.

Three things make it hard. Most steps pay 0, so the agent must work out which of hundreds of past torques earned each +0.1: the credit assignment problem. A slightly wrong torque fails quietly, drifting the hand a millimeter off. And the arm never starts in the same pose twice, so the policy must work from any 33-number state.

Early in training the hand scribbles circles and scores under 1; late in training it parks on the ball and scores in the high 30s. The only difference is the numbers inside two neural networks.

DQN cannot do continuous actions

DQN's whole trick is one line: pick the action with the biggest Q value. That needs a finite menu.

Two ways to choose an action: score a menu, or output the move.
a = argmaxa Q(s, a)
This is DQN's choice. Q(s, a) is the score of move a in state s, and argmax picks the move with the biggest score. Four buttons means four scores to compare; four real-valued dials give infinitely many moves and no biggest one to pick.
104 = 10,000 combos
Chopping each of the 4 dials into 10 notches does not save it. Every added dial multiplies the count by 10 again (the curse of dimensionality), and the notches throw away precision: 0.61 versus 0.62 on the elbow can decide whether the hand holds the ball.
a = μ(s)
This is DDPG's choice. The actor μ maps the state straight to the four torques. No menu, no search.

The DDPG paper (Lillicrap et al., 2016) keeps DQN's machinery, the Q-network, the replay buffer (a memory of past steps that training samples from at random) and the target network (a slow copy whose prices hold still), and adds an actor trained with the deterministic policy gradient: the actor improves by following the gradient the critic hands it.

It is actor-critic and model-free (it learns from rewards alone, with no model of how the world responds). One algorithm, architecture and set of hyperparameters solved more than 20 simulated physics tasks, among them cartpole swing-up, dexterous manipulation, legged locomotion and car driving. On many it matched a planner that had full access to the dynamics, and it could learn straight from raw pixels.

The actor also amortizes the search: it pays the cost once, during training, so at test time choosing torques is one fast forward pass. The policy is the search, distilled.

Argmax needs a menu; a dial has none, so the actor outputs the move itself.

Two networks, two jobs

DDPG splits the job in two. The actor is a policy network: state in, four torques out. The critic is a Q-network: a state-action pair in, one score out. The actor proposes, the critic judges.

The layer sizes come straight from my repo. The actor ends in a tanh, which squashes every output into [-1, 1], so it cannot ask for an impossible torque:

actor = nn.Sequential(                 # ReLU: max(0, x), between layers
    nn.Linear(33, 256), nn.ReLU(),
    nn.Linear(256, 128), nn.ReLU(),
    nn.Linear(128, 64), nn.ReLU(),
    nn.Linear(64, 4), nn.Tanh())       # 4 torques, each in [-1, 1]

The critic wires the action in late: the state goes through a 256-unit layer first, then the 4 torques join:

class Critic(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__()
        self.state_layer = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(33, 256), nn.ReLU())
        self.head = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Linear(256 + 4, 128), nn.ReLU(),   # the 4 torques join here
            nn.Linear(128, 64), nn.ReLU(),
            nn.Linear(64, 1))                     # one Q value
    def forward(self, state, action):
        return self.head(torch.cat([self.state_layer(state), action], dim=1))

State alone feeds the actor. State first, action after, feeds the critic. That asymmetry is the whole architecture: one network moves, the other evaluates the move.

Same layer sizes, opposite jobs: the actor proposes torques, the critic only judges them.

Deterministic means the actor outputs the same torques for the same state, with no sampling. A stochastic policy samples from a distribution and explores for free, but it jitters, and an arm should hold still. DDPG buys its exploration separately, with noise.

Why tanh instead of clipping? Gradients die at a clipping wall; tanh is smooth, so gradients keep flowing and the network learns to stay inside the range.

Each network has roughly 50,000 parameters, just over 100,000 together, plus their slow-copy twins.

The critic learns from the Bellman backup

The critic learns like DQN's Q-network, by a Bellman backup: a move's price should equal the reward it just earned plus the discounted price of where it leaves the arm. Gamma, the discount factor, shrinks that future part. One recorded step (state, torques, reward, next state) is a transition, and the next price comes from the slow actor and slow critic, not from a search.

Written out, that backup is two lines:

One critic update in two lines: the slow twin sets the target price, then the live critic measures its miss.
y = r + γ·Q′(s′, μ′(s′))
Read it inside out: from the next state s′, the slow actor μ′ picks the next move, then the slow critic Q′ prices that pair. The target price y is the reward r just seen, plus the discount γ times that price. The primes mark the slow twins, so the price holds still.
loss = E[(y − Q(s,a))2]
This is the number the critic's step pushes down: the average, over the batch, of the squared gap between the target price and the live critic's guess Q(s,a) for the move actually taken. The E is that batch average, so one weird transition cannot yank the networks around. Squaring means big mistakes hurt more than small ones.

In PyTorch, over a minibatch (a small random handful of transitions from the replay buffer, held as tensors s, a, r, s_next, with r of shape (B, 1) for a batch of B):

with torch.no_grad():                        # the target holds still
    a_next = actor_target(s_next)            # slow actor picks the next move
    y = r + gamma * critic_target(s_next, a_next)   # r: (B, 1), like the critic's output
loss = F.mse_loss(critic(s, a), y)           # mean of (y - Q(s,a))^2 over the batch
critic_opt.zero_grad()
loss.backward()
critic_opt.step()

The critic never picks an action; it only judges pairs. Choosing is the actor's job.

One update does almost nothing; training is hundreds of thousands of minibatches. Learning here is erosion, not lightning.

The actor follows the critic's gradient uphill

Here is the trick this page exists for. The actor never sees a target action. Instead, ask the critic which way the torques should move to score higher. The critic is differentiable, so the gradient of Q with respect to the action answers exactly, and the actor's weights shift so its torques slide that way.

For one fixed state the critic is a landscape over torque space, and the actor's output is a dot on it; each update steps the dot uphill. Climb the critic.

The chain rule turns that into one line, the deterministic policy gradient theorem, the second big idea on this page after the Bellman backup:

The actor's uphill step in one line: two sensitivities multiplied, two links, one chain.
∇θJ ≈ ∇aQ·∇θμ
Read it as a sentence: move the weights in the direction that moves the torques in the direction that raises the score. ∇ is the gradient, the direction of steepest change. ∇aQ is how the critic's score changes when the torques change; ∇θμ is how the live actor μ's torques change when its weights θ change; the · multiplies them. J is the actor's objective, and ≈ is there because the climb follows the critic, not the real return.

In code, the actor minimizes minus Q, and backpropagation through the critic multiplies the two factors:

actor_loss = -critic(s, actor(s)).mean()     # raise Q by lowering -Q
actor_opt.zero_grad()
actor_loss.backward()                        # chain rule through the critic into the actor
actor_opt.step()                             # only the actor's weights move

Why go through the critic? The Unity physics is a black box: you can run it, but not take its gradient. The critic is a learned, differentiable model of how good a move is, trained on real rewards and kept honest by the Bellman backup. No argmax, no search: the actor follows the slope.

The catch: early on the critic's landscape is random, and the actor climbs it confidently anyway. A bad judge makes a confident fool. That is why the stabilizers in Three stabilizers: slow targets, replay buffer, smooth noise are not optional.

Three stabilizers: slow targets, replay buffer, smooth noise

DDPG stands on three pieces of scaffolding. Skip any one and you get an arm that spins in circles. The repo taught me each of these the hard way.

One stabilizer per way training dies: oscillation, overfitting to the recent past, stagnation.

Slow target networks. The critic's target is built from its own predictions, so a fast-moving target makes it chase its own tail. The target copies creep toward the live networks instead:

Slow-copy the live weights into the twins, a tiny fraction at a time, so the targets stay stable.
θ′ ← τθ + (1−τ)θ′
The arrow means gets replaced by: the slow weights θ′ become a blend of a share τ of the live weights θ and the rest, 1−τ, their own. With τ a tenth of a percent, every target weight moves only a hair per update.
tau = 1e-3                                   # a tenth of a percent per update
for p_t, p in zip(critic_target.parameters(), critic.parameters()):
    p_t.data.copy_(tau * p.data + (1 - tau) * p_t.data)
# the same loop runs for actor_target and actor

DQN hard-copied its targets every few thousand steps; DDPG makes the copy continuous and gentle. Hard-copy every step and the training curve turns into a seismograph.

Ornstein-Uhlenbeck noise. A deterministic actor left alone repeats its first guess forever, so during training I add OU noise to the torques. Gaussian (white) noise is independent every step, so torques stutter; OU noise remembers its last value and is pulled back toward zero, so it wanders smoothly:

OU noise in two lines: a smooth wander around zero, added to the torques during training.
x ← x − κ·x + σ·ξ
x is the noise, one value per torque. The term κ·x pulls it back toward zero, σ sets the size of each fresh kick, and ξ is a new standard normal random number every step. Because x carries over, consecutive steps drift together instead of twitching.
a = μ(s) + x
The torques sent to the arm are the actor's output μ(s) plus the noise. Noise is on during training and typically off at test time, so the arm can hold still.

For an arm with inertia (a moving part keeps moving), smooth noise tries coherent strategies ("push left for a while, see what happens") instead of vibrating in place. The original DDPG paper used OU noise for this reason, and my repo kept it.

The replay buffer. Every transition from every arm goes into one big memory, and training samples random minibatches from it. Consecutive steps are correlated (step 100 looks like step 99), and random sampling breaks that. Each transition is also reused many times, and twenty arms fill the buffer twenty times faster than one:

from collections import deque
import random

buffer = deque(maxlen=buffer_size)           # a big, fixed-size memory
buffer.append((s, a, r, s_next))             # every step, from every arm
batch = random.sample(buffer, batch_size)    # a random minibatch

Each stabilizer fixes a different way training dies: the targets fix oscillation, the noise fixes stagnation, the buffer fixes overfitting to the recent past. DDPG without stabilizers is not a simpler algorithm. It is a broken one.

One training update, walked through one move at a time

One training update is five moves: sample, price, judge, climb, creep. The update trains on old experience, never on the step that just happened. That is off-policy learning: the networks learn from experience gathered by older versions of the actor, which is what makes reusing the replay buffer legal.

One DDPG training update in four lines: the slow twin sets the target price, the critic measures its miss, the actor climbs the critic's slope, and the twins creep toward the live nets.
y = r + γ·Q′(s′, μ′(s′))
Read it inside out: from the next state, the slow actor picks the next move, then the slow critic prices that pair. The target price is the reward just seen, plus the discounted value of that pair. The price comes from the slow twins so it holds still: if those predictions move fast, the target moves fast, and the critic chases its own tail.
loss = E[(y − Q(s,a))²]
This is the number the critic's step pushes down: the average, over the batch, of the squared gap between the target price and the live critic's guess for the move actually taken. The E is that batch average, so one weird transition cannot yank the networks around, and squaring means big mistakes hurt more than small ones.
∇θJ ≈ ∇aQ·∇θμ
Read it as a sentence: move the weights in the direction that moves the torques in the direction that raises the score. Two sensitivities multiplied: how the score changes when the torques change, times how the torques change when the weights change. The step is approximate because the actor climbs the critic, a learned model, not the real return: the environment is not differentiable, and the critic fixes that.

The slow weights get replaced by a blend: τ of the live weights and the rest, the 1−τ share, their own. Each update moves them just a tenth of a percent of the way toward the live values, a nearly stationary bullseye, so the targets stay stable.

One pass through the actor-critic training loop

The whole algorithm as one circle: state in, torques out, world moves, reward lands, critic judges, gradient flows home. The critic can only judge state and torques together, and the gradient is what carries that judgment back to the actor.

Two pieces hide inside it: the replay buffer sits between the world and the critic, and the critic in step five is really two, the live one and its slow target copy. Training is millions of laps.

The whole algorithm is one loop, not a stack of parts.

The actual Reacher training log

That was the toy. Here is what actually happened in my RF-DDPG repo, on the real Unity ML-Agents Reacher task.

The setup is the one above, with Ornstein-Uhlenbeck noise exploring and +0.1 per step on target. The settings that matter:

state_size, action_size = 33, 4        # observation numbers, torque dials in [-1, 1]
num_agents = 20                        # identical arms, scores averaged
gamma = 0.99                           # discount factor
tau = 1e-3                             # target creep per update, from the DDPG paper
actor_sizes = (33, 256, 128, 64, 4)    # tanh on the output
critic_sizes = (33, 256, 128, 64, 1)   # the 4 torques join after the 256 layer

Gamma at 0.99 says the future matters but the present matters more, so the agent plans ahead without obsessing over the far future. Each number is an opinion about the world, written as a hyperparameter.

The early episodes are noise near zero. Then the average bends upward, wobbling because twenty arms are twenty opinions and the critic is still learning, and once it crosses 30 it stays above. That crossing is the whole project succeeding.

Why twenty arms? A single arm's score bounces wildly from episode to episode, and learning from it is like grading essays during an earthquake. Twenty arms, each exploring with its own random noise, average that away and fill the buffer with diverse experience. One arm alone takes far longer to cross 30.

The payoff is the deterministic actor at test time, with no noise. The hand glides to the ball, overshoots by a hair, corrects and settles, emitting four fresh torques dozens of times per second. Control is not the absence of error. It is error, corrected fast.

What can still go wrong: noise, slow targets, action space, parallelism

  • Deterministic policies are brittle without noise. Ornstein-Uhlenbeck noise during training, typically none at test time. Skip the noise and the arm learns exactly one pose: its first guess.
  • The target networks are load-bearing. The targets creep at a tenth of a percent per update; hard-copy them every step and the targets move as fast as the critic does.
  • Continuous is not always better. On a discrete problem DQN is simpler, faster, and often wins. Match the algorithm to the action space, not to the hype.
  • Twenty parallel arms do a lot of heavy lifting. One arm alone takes far longer to solve this. Parallelism is a compute cheat code, and this repo uses it.

What came after DDPG

TD3 (Twin Delayed DDPG) trains two critics and builds the target from the lower score, which keeps an inflated score out of the target and fixes the critic overestimating how good a move is. It also updates the actor less often than the critics, and adds a little noise to the target action so the critic cannot memorize sharp peaks:

TD3's target price: the more cautious of two critics, at a slightly noisy next move.
y = r + γ·mini=1,2 Q′i(s′, μ′(s′) + ε)
Q′1 and Q′2 are the two slow target critics, and min takes the lower of their prices. ε is the small noise added to the slow actor's next move μ′(s′). The rest is DDPG's target.

SAC (Soft Actor-Critic) drops determinism. The policy stays stochastic and earns an entropy bonus (entropy measures how spread out its choices are), so exploration lives in the objective instead of being bolted on as OU noise. It tends to learn from fewer steps, which matters when each step is expensive.

Multi-agent DDPG is the tennis page's family. My repo runs one shared actor-critic pair in self-play, one brain driving both rackets; the MADDPG paper gives each agent its own actor and its own centralized critic, which watches everybody's moves. See the tennis page.

When DDPG is the right call: continuous dials, a cheap simulator, and a controller that should hold still at test time. Discrete menus belong to DQN; expensive real-world steps often favor SAC.
