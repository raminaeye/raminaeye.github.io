Math for Multitask Learning

Vectors, dots, and projections: the three moves every method is built from.

On this page

The kit Arrow Norm Agreement Pull Shadow Blend Reading equations

Every method in this series does one of three things to gradient arrows: it measures how loud they are, it checks how much they agree, or it cuts out the part that fights. So the entire math kit is six ideas, and every one of them is simple once seen the right way. If vectors and gradients are old friends, skip ahead. Otherwise, start with the toolkit below: four draggable pictures. Drag the arrowheads and the dot, watch the numbers respond, then read the six ideas with these pictures in hand.

1. A vector is an arrow

Before the name, the picture. A vector is just a list of numbers, like (3, 1). I picture it as an arrow on a map: start at the origin, walk 3 steps east and 1 step north, and the arrow from start to finish is the vector. Panel (a) above is exactly this arrow, with its east and north parts drawn as dashed lines.

A model's parameters θ are a vector too, a list with millions of entries, so the "map" has millions of directions. I cannot draw that map, but every fact about flat 2-D arrows stays true in it, which is why every picture on this page gets away with drawing flat arrows.

2. The norm is the arrow's length

The name: the norm, written ‖v‖. The plain idea: it is the arrow's length. The recipe: square every entry, add them up, take the square root. That is Pythagoras wearing a hat. For the arrow in panel (a):

$${\lVert (3,1) \rVert} = \sqrt{3^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{10} \approx 3.16$$
  1. \(3^2 = 9\) and \(1^2 = 1\)
  2. \(9 + 1 = 10\)
  3. \(\sqrt{10} \approx 3.16\)

The payoff for this series: whenever I call a task's gradient "loud", I mean exactly this number is large. Loudness is a norm. Drag v in panel (a) to a short stub and a long spear and watch the number track the length.

3. The dot product is an agreement meter

Two arrows can point the same way, cross at an angle, or fight. I want one number that says which. The name: the dot product, written ⟨u, v⟩. The plain idea: an agreement meter. Multiply matching entries and add:

$$\langle (3,1),\, (-1,2) \rangle = 3\cdot(-1) + 1\cdot 2 = -3 + 2 = -1$$

The dot product also equals the two lengths times the cosine of the angle between the arrows, so its sign is the verdict: positive means roughly the same direction, zero means exactly perpendicular, negative means they partly oppose each other.

$$\langle u,v \rangle = {\lVert u \rVert}{\lVert v \rVert}\cdot\cos\theta \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \cos\angle(u,v) = \frac{\langle u,v \rangle}{{\lVert u \rVert}{\lVert v \rVert}}$$

Dividing by the lengths gives the cosine similarity, a scale-free agreement score between -1 and 1. Scale-free because a loud arrow and a quiet arrow pointing the same way score the same. For the pair in panel (b), one computation per line:

  1. \(\lVert (3,1) \rVert = \sqrt{10} \approx 3.16\)
  2. \(\lVert (-1,2) \rVert = \sqrt{5} \approx 2.24\)
  3. \(\cos \angle = -1 / (3.16 \times 2.24) = -1 / 7.07 \approx -0.14\)

Negative, so (3, 1) and (-1, 2) partly oppose. In panel (b), drag v until the meter reads about -0.14 and you will find yourself near (-1, 2). Drag it to point along u and watch the meter climb toward 1: full agreement.

4. A gradient is a pull

A loss L(θ) assigns a "badness height" to every setting of the knobs. Picture a hilly landscape, like the contour hill in panel (d). From where I stand I want to know: which way is up, and how steep? The name: the gradient, written ∇L(θ). The plain idea: the arrow pointing straight uphill from where I stand, with length equal to the steepness.

I treat each task's gradient as a pull on the shared parameters: direction ∇L, strength ‖∇L‖. Training follows the opposite pull, -∇L, straight downhill. In panel (d) the red arrow is the uphill pull and the gold arrow is the step training actually takes.

Each task has its own landscape and therefore its own pull, and those pulls need not point the same way. That sentence is the seed of every problem in this series.

One worked example on the panel (d) hill, L = x² + 2y², at the starting dot (1.4, -0.8). First the height:

  1. \(L = 1.4^2 + 2 \cdot (-0.8)^2\)
  2. \(= 1.96 + 2 \cdot 0.64\)
  3. \(= 1.96 + 1.28 = 3.24\)

Then the pull, ∇L = (2x, 4y):

  1. \(2 \cdot 1.4 = 2.8\) and \(4 \cdot (-0.8) = -3.2\)
  2. so \(\nabla L(1.4, -0.8) = (2.8, -3.2)\)

And the steepness, which is the pull's strength:

  1. \(\lVert \nabla L \rVert = \sqrt{2.8^2 + (-3.2)^2}\)
  2. \(= \sqrt{7.84 + 10.24} = \sqrt{18.08} \approx 4.25\)

Drag the dot in panel (d) toward the center and watch the red arrow shrink: flatter ground, weaker pull. Drag it outward and the pull grows.

5. Projection is casting a shadow

I have g1's pull and g2's pull, and I want to know: how much of g1 is really just pushing along g2's line? The name: the projection of g1 onto g2. The plain idea: the shadow g1 casts on g2's line. The recipe: take the agreement score, divide by g2's squared length, and scale g2 itself by that:

$${\text{proj}_{g_2}(g_1)} = \left( \frac{\langle g_1,g_2 \rangle}{{\lVert g_2 \rVert}^2} \right) \cdot g_2$$

For the pair in panel (c), g1 = (3, 1) and g2 = (-1, 2), one computation per line:

  1. \(\langle g_1, g_2 \rangle = -1\), computed above
  2. \(\lVert g_2 \rVert^2 = (-1)^2 + 2^2 = 1 + 4 = 5\)
  3. \(t = -1 / 5 = -0.2\)
  4. shadow = \(-0.2 \cdot (-1, 2) = (0.2, -0.4)\)
  5. leftover = \((3, 1) - (0.2, -0.4) = (2.8, 1.4)\)
  6. dot check: \(2.8 \cdot (-1) + 1.4 \cdot 2 = -2.8 + 2.8 = 0\)

Subtracting the shadow from g1 leaves the part perpendicular to g2, and the dot check reading 0 is the proof: perpendicular means dot product zero. When the dot product is negative, the shadow points against g2, so the leftover is g1 with its anti-g2 part removed. This one subtraction is all of PCGrad: cut the shadow, keep the rest.

Where does the formula come from? The shadow lies on g2's line, so it must be some scaled copy t·g2. A shadow meets the line at a right angle, and "right angle" means dot product zero: ⟨g1 - t·g2, g2⟩ = 0. Expand: ⟨g1,g2⟩ - t·‖g2‖² = 0, so t = ⟨g1,g2⟩ / ‖g2‖². One line of algebra and the formula pops out.

6. A convex blend is a way to split 100%

Two tasks pull in different directions. The cheapest compromise is to split my step between them: some percent of pull one, some percent of pull two. The name: a convex combination. The plain idea: a blend where every weight is at least zero and the weights add to one, so each task gets a percentage of the mix. The set of all legal weight lists is the simplex, written Δ:

$$w_1 g_1 + w_2 g_2 + \cdots + w_K g_K, \quad w_i \ge 0, \quad \sum_i w_i = 1$$

Two more bits of vocabulary I will use everywhere: minx f(x) is the lowest value f can reach; argminx f(x) is the setting of x that reaches it: the best knob position, not the best score.

A worked blend with real numbers, g1 = (2, 0), g2 = (-1, 1.6), weights (0.5, 0.5):

  1. \(0.5 \cdot (2, 0) = (1.0, 0)\)
  2. \(0.5 \cdot (-1, 1.6) = (-0.5, 0.8)\)
  3. sum = \((1.0 - 0.5, 0 + 0.8) = (0.5, 0.8)\)

Why blends matter so much: blending is the cheapest way for tasks to compromise, and one fact pays off twice later: a blend can never do worse than its worst ingredient. If every task's pull likes a step, every blend likes it too. So "search over all blends" often turns out to be the same problem as "protect the worst task", only much cheaper to solve. How to read any equation

A habit that pays off across this whole series: read every equation aloud in words before reading it in symbols. The left side of = names the thing; the right side is the recipe for computing it. Every worked example in this series redoes the key computations with real numbers, one computation per line, so you can check my arithmetic instead of trusting it.
