Aliasing and Anti-Aliasing Filters

Why fast things look slow once sampled, and the filter that stops it.

On ADC Fundamentals the converter rounded each sample to the nearest stair, the amplitude axis. This page is the time axis, how often to take a sample, and what goes wrong when you take them too slowly. The running example is still a brain recording sampled 1,000 times per second.

Sampling keeps one value at fixed intervals and throws away everything in between. A tone faster than half the sampling rate then leaves exactly the same samples as a slower tone, so it shows up at the wrong frequency, and nothing after the ADC can undo that. The cure is to remove those fast tones before they are sampled, with an analog low-pass filter, or to sample fast and filter digitally before slowing the rate down.

1 A wheel that spins backward on film

Film takes 24 pictures per second and shows you nothing in between, so your eye joins each spoke to the nearest spoke in the next picture. If the wheel turns a little less than one spoke gap per picture, it seems to creep backward (Figure 1).

A sampled sine has the same problem. Between two samples a fast sine can turn almost a full cycle, and the dots then trace a slow wave instead. Sample a 600 Hz tone 1,000 times per second and the dots fall exactly on a 400 Hz tone. That false, slower tone is called an alias.

2 The Nyquist limit

A spectrum shows how much of each frequency a signal holds; Fourier Transform and the FFT builds it from scratch.

The wheel looked right as long as it turned less than half a spoke gap per picture. For a sine the limit is half a cycle per sample, which puts the highest safe frequency at half the sampling rate fs.

The highest frequency the samples can describe.
fNyquist = fs2
A tone needs more than two samples per cycle to be told apart from a slower one, so the highest frequency the samples can describe is half the sampling rate. Doubling fs doubles this limit; anything faster folds back below it.

Harry Nyquist showed in 1928 that a channel can carry at most twice as many independent pulses per second as its bandwidth in hertz. Claude Shannon stated and proved the sampling theorem in 1949: a signal with nothing above fs/2 is fully pinned down by its samples.

The edge itself is not safe. A sine exactly at fs/2, sampled at its zero crossings, reads as all zeros. So sample strictly faster than twice the highest frequency.

3 Sampling copies the spectrum

Why does a fast tone land at a specific slower one? Taking one value every 1/fs seconds is like multiplying the signal by a row of very narrow spikes. In frequency, that multiplication makes copies of the whole spectrum, one centred on every multiple of fs.

The spectrum after sampling.
Xs(f) = fs Σk X(f − k fs)
Sampling multiplies the signal by a row of spikes one sample period apart, and in frequency that makes copies of X(f), the continuous signal's spectrum, shifted by every multiple k fs (k counts copies here, not DFT bins). The factor fs only scales the height. Where a copy overlaps the original band, the two can no longer be pulled apart, and that overlap is aliasing.

The copy that lands between 0 and fs/2 is the one you see. That gives the fold rule.

Where a tone at frequency f shows up after sampling.
falias = |f − k fs|
A tone at f also sits in every copy, at f − k fs, and the samples show whichever copy lands between 0 and fs/2. The whole number k picks that copy, and the absolute value folds a negative frequency back to positive.
falias = fs − f
This is the case k = 1, for f between fs/2 and fs. As f rises past Nyquist the alias falls by the same amount, so a tone just above fs/2 shows up just below it.

Twenty samples of a 600 Hz tone and of its 400 Hz alias at fs = 1 kHz. The last line prints True, because the two sample lists are the same.

import numpy as np

FS, F_TONE = 1000, 600
F_ALIAS = abs(F_TONE - FS)          # 400 Hz
n = np.arange(20)                   # sample index n
tone = np.cos(2 * np.pi * F_TONE * n / FS)
alias = np.cos(2 * np.pi * F_ALIAS * n / FS)
print(np.allclose(tone, alias))
  1. 1Puts the 600 Hz tone's alias at fs − f = 400 Hz (eq card in Section 3, line 2).
  2. 2Samples both tones 20 times and checks that the dots are the same.

A perfect digital-to-analog converter rebuilds the one smooth wave below Nyquist that passes through every dot. Past Nyquist, that rebuilt wave is the alias, so the playback is wrong even though every sample was right.

4 Filter before you sample

Once a 600 Hz interferer has folded to 400 Hz, it looks exactly like a real 400 Hz signal. No digital filter after the ADC can tell them apart, so the cleanup has to happen before the samples are taken. An analog low-pass anti-aliasing filter in front of the ADC removes what lies above Nyquist, so nothing is left to fold.

analog signal low-pass filter ADC digital samples

|H(f)| is how much of frequency f a filter lets through; How FIR and IIR Filters Shape Magnitude and Phase builds it from scratch. Real filters roll off gradually. The cutoff fc is where half the power gets through, −3 dB. Past it the response falls by a fixed number of dB per decade (a decade is ten times the frequency), and each step of filter order p adds 20 dB per decade. The Butterworth filter is the one that stays as flat as possible below the cutoff.

How much power a Butterworth low-pass of order p lets through.
|H(f)|2 = 11 + (ffc)2p
At f = fc the ratio is 1, so the bottom is 2 and half the power passes. Below the cutoff the ratio raised to 2p stays near 0 and almost everything passes; above it the term grows fast, and a higher order p makes the fall steeper, 20p dB per decade.

Back to the brain recording, sampled at 1 kHz. A 500 Hz cutoff is only 3 dB down at Nyquist, so a 600 Hz interferer still gets through and becomes a 400 Hz alias.

The Butterworth card in decibels, with arguments f, fc and p. The five calls print 0.0, −7.2, −14.3, −28.2 and −42.3 dB, so a lower cutoff and a higher order both push the 600 Hz interferer down.

import numpy as np

def butter_db(f, f_c, p):
    return -10 * np.log10(1 + (f / f_c) ** (2 * p))

print(butter_db(150, 400, 4))       # the wanted band
print(butter_db(600, 500, 4))
print(butter_db(600, 400, 4))
print(butter_db(600, 400, 8))
print(butter_db(600, 400, 12))
  1. 1Gain in dB of a Butterworth low-pass (eq card in Section 4, line 1).
  2. 2The wanted 150 Hz band passes almost untouched.
  3. 3The 600 Hz interferer at both cutoffs and orders 4, 8 and 12, the points of Figure 7.

Higher order has a price. Butterworth is an IIR filter, and its group delay, how long each frequency is held back, changes with frequency, more so at higher order. How FIR and IIR Filters Shape Magnitude and Phase shows why that bends a waveform.

5 Sample fast, then slow down in software

A steep analog filter is expensive, and its phase gets worse as it gets steeper. The way out is to sample much faster than you need. Now the analog filter only has to remove what lies far above your band, which a gentle filter can do. Then a digital low-pass, cheap and as steep as you like, cuts at the new Nyquist, and you keep one sample in Q.

The sampling rate after keeping one sample in Q.
fs,new = fsQ
Keeping one sample in Q divides the rate by Q, and with it the Nyquist limit, which drops to fs,new/2. Anything between the new and the old limit would fold, so the digital low-pass removes it first.

By default, decimate uses an order 8 Chebyshev low-pass and runs it forward and back, so it adds no delay. The polyphase resampler in the snippet uses a windowed FIR low-pass.

6 In practice

Anti-aliasing sits in two places, an analog low-pass right before the ADC and a digital low-pass before every downsampling step in software.

A rising tone downsampled by 4 in two ways with SciPy. bad keeps every 4th sample and folds; good and good2 low-pass first, so nothing above the new Nyquist is left.

import numpy as np
from scipy import signal

FS, FACTOR, DUR_S = 8000, 4, 2
t = np.arange(DUR_S * FS) / FS
sweep = signal.chirp(t, f0=0, t1=DUR_S, f1=3800)

bad = sweep[::FACTOR]                     # no filter, folds
good = signal.decimate(sweep, FACTOR)
good2 = signal.resample_poly(sweep, up=1, down=FACTOR)
  1. 1Makes a 2-second sweep from 0 to 3.8 kHz at 8 kHz.
  2. 2Keeps every 4th sample with no filter, so the sweep folds (eq card in Section 5, line 1).
  3. 3Low-passes first, then keeps every 4th sample, so nothing folds.

6.1 Gotchas

  • Slicing x[::4] keeps every 4th sample with no filter, so it folds.
  • For factors above 13, SciPy recommends calling decimate several times.
  • Zero phase needs the whole signal; on a live stream set zero_phase=False.
