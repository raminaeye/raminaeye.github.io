1 Why one big step can wreck a good paddle

A Pong paddle that already hits back most balls can be ruined by one update that changes its move chances too much. This page shows how to learn from each stretch of play several times without that happening.

The agent is the learner that picks the moves (RL Basics).

A state is the agent’s situation right now; a move, or action, is one choice the agent can make in a state; a reward is the number the world hands back after each move, good or bad (RL Basics).

The agent sees a state, makes a move and gets a reward, over and over.

An episode is one run from the start until it ends, and a policy is the agent’s rule for choosing a move in each state; it may always pick the same move or pick by chance.

The running example is Atari Pong. Each player moves a paddle up and down to hit the ball back past the other. The state is the screen, given as a stack of the last few frames so the ball’s direction shows. The moves are up, stay and down. The paddle moves once every few frames of the game; one such move is one step. Each step shifts the paddle only a little, so it has to start moving before the ball arrives. The reward is \(+1\) for a point won, \(-1\) for a point lost and \(0\) on every other step. One episode is one full game. A point ends only when a player misses the ball, so most steps of a game pass with nothing won or lost. \(J\) is the average points per game, the quantity the policy’s weights are moved to raise (Policy Gradient Methods); it is points won minus points lost.

Figure 1 shows Atari Pong, the running example for the whole page: one paddle, three moves, and a point for every ball the other side misses. The state is the screen, the moves are up, stay and down, and the reward is plus one for a point won, minus one for a point lost, zero otherwise. Take a rally: the paddle must start moving before the ball arrives, because each step shifts it only a little.

A policy network is a network that looks at the state and outputs the chance of each move (Policy Gradient Methods).

The policy gradient pushes each move the agent played by a score \(\Psi_t\) of how well it turned out, up when the score is positive and down when it is negative (Policy Gradient Methods).

A batch is the group of steps an update learns from; PPO collects each batch from fresh play, a stretch of steps from several games played at once.

PPO is the policy-gradient method that makes small, limited updates so one batch cannot wreck the policy.

Playing a batch takes a long stretch of steps, so it pays to learn from each batch more than once. Yet many gradient steps on the same batch keep pushing the same moves, and the paddle’s chances can swing far past what the batch supports. These are destructively large policy updates.

A smaller step for every weight does not fix this, because it cannot see how far each move’s chance has moved, so it must suit the worst case and wastes most of the batch.

Figure 2 shows one too-big update: before it, up takes the largest share, and after it, up nearly vanishes while stay and down grow. Below, each mark is one rally, serve to point: the first seven are green hits, the last three red misses, so the paddle that won most rallies now loses them. Take the up bar: it shrinks from the longest to a sliver in one update.

The return is all the reward still to come, with later rewards counted a little less (RL Basics).

A state’s value \(V(s)\) is the return the agent expects from state \(s\) (RL Basics).

In Pong, a screen with the ball heading past the other paddle has a high value, and one with the ball slipping past this paddle a low one.

On-policy means learning the values of the same policy that is playing (SARSA).

Policy gradients are on-policy, because each push counts a move as often as the current policy plays it, so play by an older or different policy would push by the wrong amounts (Actor-Critic: A2C and A3C).

The batch in hand was played by the policy as it was before the update.

In Section 2, PPO corrects for this with a ratio of new to old chances, so the batch can be used a few times.

\(\theta_{\text{old}}\) is the frozen copy of the weights that played the batch; \(\theta\) is the copy being updated.

\(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s)\) means the whole set of move chances on screen \(s\), up, stay and down together, rather than the chance of one move.

So while the paddle learns, the batch still shows the chances it played with, and the update shows the chances it has now.

1.1 An earlier hard limit and its cost

Trust Region Policy Optimization (TRPO), an earlier method, keeps each update inside a trust region, a limit on how far the new chances may move from the old ones in one update.

PPO keeps the same aim for the Pong paddle, chances that stay near the ones that played the batch, but reaches it another way.

How far the chances move is measured by the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence, how different two sets of chances are.

TRPO’s limit needs an expensive extra calculation at every update, and it is awkward with networks that share one body between the policy and other outputs, or that use dropout, switching off units at random while training.

PPO uses only ordinary gradient steps, the same kind that train any network.

Equation 1. TRPO kept each update inside a hard limit, but that limit cost an extra calculation at every update, so PPO needs a cheaper way to keep the paddle near the chances that played the batch. A driver who may only turn the wheel a little per correction faces the same limit.
1\(\displaystyle \begin{aligned}&\hat{\mathbb{E}}_t\Big[\mathrm{KL}\big(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#1f4e9c}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}}(\cdot\mid s_t)\,\Vert\,\\&\quad\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#1f4e9c}{\pi_\theta}}(\cdot\mid s_t)\big)\Big]\le\Delta\end{aligned}\)
Intuition. One update must not move the chances far in a single step, or a good paddle can be ruined at once.
The mathematics. This line says the batch average of the KL between the old chances and the new chances on each screen is at most Delta. \( \hat{\mathbb{E}}_t \) is the batch average. On one screen, the KL adds over the three moves the old chance times the log of the old chance over the new chance, and the double bar reads as between. \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#1f4e9c}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}}(\cdot\mid s_t) \) is the old chances on screen \( s_t \), from the frozen weights; \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#1f4e9c}{\pi_\theta}}(\cdot\mid s_t) \) is the new chances, from the weights being updated. The dot stands for all three moves at once, up, stay and down. The sign says the average must stay at or below \( \Delta \), the allowed size of the change. So the whole equation says this: average how far the new chances moved, and keep it at or below Delta.
Example. Take a screen where the ball is far away and both chance sets give up the same chance: the KL on that screen is zero, so it adds nothing to the average.
Take a screen where the ball is coming in high and the old chances favour up. After one pass the new chances favour up a little more, so the KL on that screen is small and the batch average stays small too. If a later pass pushed up much higher, the KL on that screen would grow and the batch average would grow with it, so the limit would stop the update once the average passed Delta.

Inside the limit, TRPO raises a stand-in objective, the surrogate, that rewards making good moves more likely, weighted by how good they were.

A version with a penalty in place of the limit needs a penalty weight that is hard to choose.

PPO replaces both the limit and the penalty with a cap on how far each move’s chance may change.

2 Capping how far each move’s chance can move

Each move in the batch needs a score of how well it turned out.

A move’s advantage is how much better it is expected to do than the usual result from that state (Dueling DQN).

\(\hat A_t\) is an estimate of the advantage of the move at step \(t\), built from real rewards and a guess of the usual result (REINFORCE and the Baseline).

That usual result has to be guessed.

A critic is a second network that guesses each state’s value, the return expected from it (Actor-Critic: A2C and A3C).

In PPO the critic’s guess of a screen’s value is the usual result each move is compared with.

The network being trained is the actor.

An actor is the policy network that picks the action; the critic’s guesses are used to score how good it was (Actor-Critic: A2C and A3C).

Actor-Critic’s actor push raises moves the critic scored above usual and lowers the rest.

PPO reuses each batch for a few passes, so it must track how far each move’s chance has moved since the batch was played. Each update works on a minibatch, a small slice of the batch.

The ratio \(\rho_t\) compares the new policy’s chance of the move taken with the old policy’s chance; at one, nothing has changed.

Clipping holds the ratio between two clip edges set by \(\epsilon_{\text{clip}}\), so an update stops rewarding a helpful change once it passes an edge.

For a move that scored well, once the new policy already makes it noticeably more likely than the old one did, stop rewarding further increases. For a move that scored badly, once it is noticeably less likely, stop pushing it down. A change in the harmful direction, a bad move becoming more likely or a good move becoming less likely, always keeps its full penalty.

\(\epsilon_{\text{clip}}\) is unrelated to the \(\epsilon\) of epsilon-greedy exploration on the value-based pages (RL Basics).

Figure 3 shows one PPO round: workers collect a batch, the critic scores every move, a few capped passes run, and the new policy becomes the old one. The arrow back to the first step says the round repeats, so each batch feeds the next. Take step 3: the cap sits under the passes, because every pass is capped.

  1. Workers, copies of the agent each playing its own game, collect a batch with the old policy (Actor-Critic: A2C and A3C).
  2. The critic scores every move: the critic’s guesses and the real rewards are blended into each move’s score.
  3. A few passes of minibatch updates, each on a small slice of the batch, on the clipped objective.
  4. The new policy becomes the old one.

One round runs after each batch, with a few passes over it in minibatches.

GAE (generalized advantage estimation) builds the advantage estimate \(\hat A_t\) from the real rewards and the critic’s guesses, blending short and long stretches of real reward into one (Actor-Critic: A2C and A3C).

\(\lambda\) sets how quickly the long stretches fade.

So in Pong a point won many steps later still raises the scores of the moves that led to it, less the further back they are.

Figure 4 shows one term of the clipped objective against the ratio, for a move that scored well above and one that scored badly below. On the helpful side the line rises to the clip edge, marked 2, then goes flat, so no more push comes past the edge; on the harmful side the line keeps falling, never flattened, marked 4. Take marker 1: the dot sits on the rising line below the edge, where the push still runs.

3 The ratio, the surrogate and the clipped objective

Equation 2. PPO replaces the hard limit with a cap on each move's chance, so one batch can be reused for a few passes without the paddle swinging far past what the batch supports. A teacher who lets a student redo one exercise a few times, but never lets one redo change the mark by much, faces the same need.
1\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#6c3483}{\rho_t}}(\theta)=\frac{\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#1f4e9c}{\pi_\theta}}(a_t\mid s_t)}{\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#1f4e9c}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}}(a_t\mid s_t)}\)
Intuition. Each move needs a number saying how far its chance has moved since the batch was played.
The mathematics. This line says \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#6c3483}{\rho_t}}(\theta) \) equals \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#1f4e9c}{\pi_\theta}}(a_t\mid s_t) \) over \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#1f4e9c}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}}(a_t\mid s_t) \). \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#6c3483}{\rho_t}}(\theta) \) is the ratio: as the weights change, the numerator changes and the ratio moves. The numerator, \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#1f4e9c}{\pi_\theta}}(a_t\mid s_t) \), is the chance the policy now gives the move actually taken, \( a_t \), on this screen \( s_t \). The denominator, \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#1f4e9c}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}}(a_t\mid s_t) \), is the chance the frozen old policy gave that same move. The divide compares them: at one nothing has changed, above one the move is more likely now, below one less likely. \( \theta \) is the weights being updated; \( \theta_{\text{old}} \) is the frozen weights that played the batch. It compares the same two policies as Equation 1, old and new, but on the one move taken. So this line says: divide the new chance of the move taken by its old chance.
Example. Take a move that scored well: on the first pass its new chance is barely above its old one, so the ratio sits near one.
2\(\displaystyle L^{\text{CPI}}(\theta)=\hat{\mathbb{E}}_t\big[\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#6c3483}{\rho_t}}\,\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#196f3d}{\hat{A}_t}}\big]\)
Intuition. The push should reward making good moves more likely, weighted by how good they were.
The mathematics. This line says \( L^{\text{CPI}}(\theta) \), the surrogate objective, equals \( \hat{\mathbb{E}}_t \) of \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#6c3483}{\rho_t}} \) times \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#196f3d}{\hat{A}_t}} \). \( \hat{\mathbb{E}}_t \) is the batch average over the batch's steps. \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#6c3483}{\rho_t}} \) times \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#196f3d}{\hat{A}_t}} \) pairs each move's score with how much more or less often the new policy would play it. \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#196f3d}{\hat{A}_t}} \) is the move's score, built by GAE from real rewards and the critic's guesses; the hat means estimate. The product rewards making good moves more likely: a positive score with the ratio above one raises the objective. With no cap it grows without limit as the ratio grows, which is why the clip is needed. CPI stands for conservative policy iteration, the earlier method behind this line. At ratio one it gives the same push as the actor loss. So this line says: average the score times the ratio over the batch.
Example. Take a move that scored well: its score is positive, so raising its chance raises the objective, and the push rewards it.
3\(\displaystyle \begin{aligned}\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#6c3483}{\bar{\rho}_t}}&=\operatorname{clip}\big(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#6c3483}{\rho_t}},\\&\quad 1-\epsilon_{\text{clip}},\;1+\epsilon_{\text{clip}}\big)\end{aligned}\)
Intuition. The ratio must stop counting once it has moved far enough: the cap is a band around one.
The mathematics. This line says \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#6c3483}{\bar{\rho}_t}} \) equals clip of \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#6c3483}{\rho_t}} \), one minus \( \epsilon_{\text{clip}} \), one plus \( \epsilon_{\text{clip}} \). On the left, \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#6c3483}{\bar{\rho}_t}} \) is the clipped ratio, and the bar marks the clipped version. On the right, clip holds \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#6c3483}{\rho_t}} \) between one minus \( \epsilon_{\text{clip}} \) and one plus \( \epsilon_{\text{clip}} \); \( \epsilon_{\text{clip}} \) is 0.1 on the Atari run, so the band is 0.9 to 1.1, annealed to zero over training. The two edges are the clip edges of Figure 4. Inside the band the clipped ratio equals the ratio; past an edge it sticks at the edge. The real ratio can still end up past an edge, and past it the ratio simply stops counting. So this line says: hold the ratio inside the band, and let it stick at the edge past it.
Example. Take a ratio just above one on the first pass: inside the band, so the clipped ratio equals it.
4\(\displaystyle L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta)=\hat{\mathbb{E}}_t\big[\min\big(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#6c3483}{\rho_t}}\,\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#196f3d}{\hat{A}_t}},\;\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#6c3483}{\bar{\rho}_t}}\,\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#196f3d}{\hat{A}_t}}\big)\big]\)
Intuition. Of the plain push and the capped push, only the smaller one counts, so the push stops at the edge.
The mathematics. This line says \( L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta) \) equals \( \hat{\mathbb{E}}_t \) of the min of \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#6c3483}{\rho_t}} \) times \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#196f3d}{\hat{A}_t}} \) and \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#6c3483}{\bar{\rho}_t}} \) times \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#196f3d}{\hat{A}_t}} \). On the left, \( L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta) \) is the clipped objective. Min takes the smaller of the two terms: the plain term and the clipped term. For a good move, \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#196f3d}{\hat{A}_t}} \) positive, once \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#6c3483}{\rho_t}} \) passes the right edge the clipped term is the smaller one; past it, it stops changing with the weights, so it gives no push. For a bad move, \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#196f3d}{\hat{A}_t}} \) negative, multiplying by a negative flips the smaller term, so the cap is on the left edge, and a bad move grown more likely keeps its full penalty. So the whole equation says this: take the smaller term at every step, and the push stops at the edge.
Example. Take a move that scored well and grew more likely over the passes: once the ratio passes the right edge, the push stops.
Take a move that scored well, up on a screen where the ball is coming in high. On the first pass the ratio sits inside the band, so the plain term counts. On a later pass the ratio passes the right edge, so the clipped term is the smaller one and the push stops, while a move that scored badly and grew more likely keeps its full penalty.

The full objective adds two more terms to the clipped push, one for the critic and one for staying undecided.

Entropy \(\mathcal{H}\) is how undecided the policy is; it is high when the moves are about equally likely and zero when one move is certain (REINFORCE and the Baseline).

The entropy bonus is a small extra term that rewards the policy for staying undecided between moves, so it keeps trying the others for longer (REINFORCE and the Baseline).

In PPO that bonus is the third term of the full objective, and it keeps the paddle from settling on one move while the passes run.

Equation 3. The clipped push alone is not a whole training step. A coach who must cover the whole game in one session faces the same job.
1\(\displaystyle \begin{aligned}&L^{\text{PPO}}(\theta,\phi)=\hat{\mathbb{E}}_t\big[L^{\text{CLIP}}_t(\theta)\\&\quad -\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{c_V}}\big(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{V_\phi(s_t)}}-\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#196f3d}{\hat{R}_t}}\big)^2\\&\quad +\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6d80}{c_H}}\,\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6d80}{\mathcal{H}}}\big(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#1f4e9c}{\pi_\theta}}(\cdot\mid s_t)\big)\big]\end{aligned}\)
Intuition. One step must do three jobs at once: push the moves, fit the critic, and stay undecided.
The mathematics. This line says \( L^{\text{PPO}}(\theta,\phi) \), the full objective for the policy weights \( \theta \) and the value weights \( \phi \), equals \( \hat{\mathbb{E}}_t \) of \( L^{\text{CLIP}}_t(\theta) \) minus \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{c_V}} \) times \( (\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{V_\phi(s_t)}} - \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#196f3d}{\hat{R}_t}}) \) squared plus \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6d80}{c_H}} \) times \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6d80}{\mathcal{H}}} \) of \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#1f4e9c}{\pi_\theta}}(\cdot\mid s_t) \). \( L^{\text{CLIP}}_t(\theta) \) is the clipped term for step t, before averaging. \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{V_\phi(s_t)}} \) is the critic's guess of the return from screen \( s_t \); \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#196f3d}{\hat{R}_t}} \) is the return it is trained to predict; the square makes the gap always positive. The minus subtracts the critic term, so maximising the objective minimises the gap. \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{c_V}} \) sets how much the critic's fit counts. The plus means the entropy bonus is added, raising \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6d80}{\mathcal{H}}} \), how undecided the policy is. \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6d80}{c_H}} \) sets how much staying undecided counts. So the whole equation says this: cap the push, fit the critic, stay undecided.
Example. Take a screen where the ball is slipping past the paddle: the critic's guess sits too high, so the squared gap pulls it down toward the returns.
Take a screen where the ball is slipping past the paddle and the critic's guess sits above the return the moves there keep earning. The clipped term pushes the good moves up, the squared gap pulls the critic's guess down toward the returns, and the entropy bonus keeps a sliver of chance on the moves the paddle rarely tries, so one update trains the push, the critic and the undecidedness together.

The code minimises the negative of this objective, so the signs flip in code.

5 PPO’s settings and runs with and without the clip

5.1 Settings

The discount \(\gamma\) sets how much a reward one step later still counts (RL Basics).

Bias here means the push is off from the true uphill direction of \(J\) in the same way on average, not just noisy from one update to the next, as in the bias-variance trade-off; it is not the offset weight inside a network layer (Policy Gradient Methods).

Counting later points a little less makes the push less noisy, because far-off points hang on many later chance moves and add the most noise; the price is a small bias.

MuJoCo is a physics simulator for robot bodies.

Spread is how much results vary from one training run to the next.

5.2 Practical tips

  • Normalize the advantages in each minibatch: shift and scale them so each minibatch averages zero with a similar size.
  • Anneal the learning rate and the clip range, lowering them step by step over training.
  • Watch how many moves hit a clip edge in each update, and the KL between the old and new policy.
  • Stop the passes early if the KL grows.
  • Scale the rewards in robot tasks.

5.3 Runs with and without the clip

A fixed-weight penalty on the KL was tried, and an adaptive one whose weight is halved when the KL stays small and doubled when it grows too large.

The clip won that comparison. On seven MuJoCo robot tasks, scored so a random policy scores 0 and the best scores 1 and averaged over the last 100 episodes, the clipped version scored 0.82, against 0.74 for the best adaptive KL penalty, 0.72 for the best fixed one, and minus 0.39 with neither a clip nor a penalty, where the unclipped run fell below a random policy.

Replay keeps a memory of past steps, and training draws random batches from it (Deep Q-Networks (DQN)).

PPO was run beside two other actor-critic methods, advantage actor-critic (A2C) and actor-critic with experience replay (ACER), an actor-critic that reuses old play by reweighting it with a ratio of new to old chances, much like PPO’s ratio, so play by an older policy no longer pushes by the wrong amounts.

On 49 Atari games PPO won 30 games to ACER’s 18 and A2C’s 1 when scores were averaged over all of training, so it learned faster; on the last 100 episodes alone ACER won 28 to PPO’s 19, so ACER finished ahead and PPO’s edge was speed of learning, not the final score.

6 PPO training in PyTorch

A toy PPO training loop for Pong in PyTorch, to read rather than run; each block’s comment names the card line it computes. Its loop follows the structure of the Atari PPO script in CleanRL, a library of short single-file training scripts; the settings values are the Atari settings in the settings table. Pong has six buttons; we keep the three that move the paddle. The code uses gymnasium, the library that runs the games, and ale_py, the Atari game set it loads. The network outputs three logits, the raw outputs \(z\) of the softmax line on Policy Gradient Methods.

import gymnasium as gym, ale_py
import torch
from torch import nn
from torch.distributions import Categorical

gym.register_envs(ale_py)
MOVES = [2, 0, 3]   # Atari action numbers: 2 moves the paddle up, 0 stays, 3 moves it down
n_envs, n_steps = N_ENVS, N_STEPS   # workers, each plays a batch of steps
# network shapes: 4 stacked frames, 32 then 64 channels, 512 hidden
FRAMES, C1, C2, HIDDEN, N_MOVES = 4, 32, 64, 512, 3
FLAT = C2 * 9 * 9   # 64 channels on a 9 by 9 map: 5184
MINIBATCH_SIZE = 256   # the batch of 1024 steps cut into 4 minibatches
EPS_GUARD = 1e-8   # a tiny guard so a flat minibatch never divides by zero

def make_pong():
    env = gym.make("ALE/Pong-v5", frameskip=1)   # the emulator itself skips no frames
    env = gym.wrappers.AtariPreprocessing(env, frame_skip=4)   # each chosen move repeats for 4 frames
    return gym.wrappers.FrameStackObservation(env, 4)
envs = gym.vector.SyncVectorEnv([make_pong] * n_envs,
    autoreset_mode=gym.vector.AutoresetMode.SAME_STEP)

class ActorCritic(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__()
        self.body = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Conv2d(FRAMES, C1, 8, stride=4), nn.ReLU(),
            nn.Conv2d(C1, C2, 4, stride=2), nn.ReLU(),
            nn.Flatten(), nn.Linear(FLAT, HIDDEN), nn.ReLU())
        self.policy_head = nn.Linear(HIDDEN, N_MOVES)   # actor: up, stay, down
        self.value_head = nn.Linear(HIDDEN, 1)    # critic: V_phi(s)
    def forward(self, x):
        h = self.body(x)
        return self.policy_head(h), self.value_head(h).squeeze(-1)   # squeeze drops the extra size-one axis, so each screen gets one guess

settings = {"clip_eps": CLIP_EPS, "n_epochs": N_EPOCHS, "minibatch_size": MINIBATCH_SIZE,
            "c_v": C_VALUE, "c_h": C_ENTROPY, "lr": LEARNING_RATE, "max_grad_norm": GRAD_CAP}
clip_eps, n_epochs, minibatch_size = settings["clip_eps"], settings["n_epochs"], settings["minibatch_size"]
c_v, c_h, max_grad_norm = settings["c_v"], settings["c_h"], settings["max_grad_norm"]

agent = ActorCritic()
opt = torch.optim.Adam(agent.parameters(), lr=settings["lr"])

obs, _ = envs.reset()
while True:
    obs_b, act_b, logp_b, rew_b, done_b, val_b = [], [], [], [], [], []
    for _ in range(n_steps):                          # each worker plays a batch of steps
        x = torch.as_tensor(obs, dtype=torch.float32) / 255
        logits, v = agent(x)
        dist = Categorical(logits=logits)   # turns the raw outputs into the move chances
        a = dist.sample()
        obs, r, term, trunc, _ = envs.step([MOVES[i] for i in a.tolist()])
        obs_b.append(x); act_b.append(a); logp_b.append(dist.log_prob(a)); val_b.append(v)
        rew_b.append(torch.as_tensor(r, dtype=torch.float32))   # r_{t+1}: the reward after move t
        done_b.append(torch.as_tensor(term | trunc, dtype=torch.float32))   # dones[t]: the game ended right after move t
    with torch.no_grad():
        _, v_last = agent(torch.as_tensor(obs, dtype=torch.float32) / 255)   # the rollout's last step bootstraps from V of the next screen
    values = torch.stack(val_b).detach()
    # GAE, built backward from the rewards, the critic's guesses and the done flags
    advantages = torch.zeros_like(values)
    gae = 0.0
    for t in reversed(range(n_steps)):
        next_v = v_last if t == n_steps - 1 else values[t + 1]
        delta = rew_b[t] + DISCOUNT * next_v * (1 - done_b[t]) - values[t]
        gae = delta + DISCOUNT * LAMBDA * (1 - done_b[t]) * gae
        advantages[t] = gae
    returns = advantages + values.detach()
    old_logp = torch.stack(logp_b).detach()
    actions = torch.stack(act_b)
    obs_f = torch.cat(obs_b)
    # flatten the batch, so each minibatch index picks one step
    advantages = advantages.reshape(-1)
    returns = returns.reshape(-1)
    old_logp = old_logp.reshape(-1)
    actions = actions.reshape(-1)
    for epoch in range(n_epochs):
        for mb in torch.randperm(len(obs_f)).split(minibatch_size):
            adv_mb = (advantages[mb] - advantages[mb].mean()) / (advantages[mb].std() + EPS_GUARD)   # shift and scale the advantages so each minibatch averages zero with a similar size
            new_logits, new_value = agent(obs_f[mb])
            dist_mb = Categorical(logits=new_logits)
            new_logp = dist_mb.log_prob(actions[mb])
            ratio = (new_logp - old_logp[mb]).exp()   # line 1 of the clip card: the ratio
            clipped = ratio.clamp(1 - clip_eps, 1 + clip_eps)   # line 3 of the clip card: the clipped ratio
            pg_loss = -torch.min(ratio * adv_mb, clipped * adv_mb).mean()   # line 4 of the clip card: the clipped objective
            v_loss = (new_value - returns[mb]).pow(2).mean()
            entropy = dist_mb.entropy().mean()
            loss = pg_loss + c_v * v_loss - c_h * entropy   # the full objective card: the clipped push, the critic and the entropy bonus
            opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward()
            nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(agent.parameters(), max_grad_norm)   # a separate cap on the size of each gradient step, unrelated to the ratio clip
            opt.step()
  1. 1Copies of Pong in one batch, each played by the same network for a batch of steps; a finished game restarts within the same step, so every step is a real move. Done marks the step at which a game ended, so nothing after it is counted.
  2. 2GAE, from Actor-Critic: A2C and A3C: the advantage estimate, built backward from the rewards, the critic’s guesses and the done flags. When a game ends, the next guess is dropped, so a game that ended contributes nothing after it; do not use CleanRL’s shifted done index.
  3. 3The ratio, line 1 of the clip card: the new chance of each move taken, divided by its old chance.
  4. 4The clipped ratio and the clipped objective, lines 3 and 4 of the clip card: the ratio held between the two edges, and the smaller of the plain and clipped terms, averaged over the minibatch. The minus sign turns the objective the code maximises into a loss.
  5. 5The full objective card: the critic’s loss, the squared gap between its guess and the return it is trained to predict, plus the entropy bonus. The gradient-norm cap is a separate cap on the size of each gradient step, unrelated to the ratio clip.
  6. 6Epochs and minibatches: the batch tensors are flattened first, so each minibatch index picks one step; the batch is shuffled and cut into minibatches, and the update runs over each minibatch several times. The advantages are shifted and scaled per minibatch so each averages zero with a similar size.

7 Where the clip stops the push

The learning rate \(\eta\) sets how big a step each update takes (RL Basics).

Figure 11 shows where the clip stops the push: step size is the learning rate, one batch is reused for several passes, and the toggle picks the move's score. At rest, the open circle on the dashed line marks where the ghost ends after ten passes at the current step size, so dragging the slider moves that circle before any pass runs. Over the passes, larger steps reach the clip edge in fewer passes; with a badly scored move the picture mirrors: pushing the move down stops at the edge, but a change that made it more likely keeps its full penalty. Take the largest step: one pass overshoots the edge, so the dot sits past it, but the clip only stops further pushing.

Intuition. Figure 12 shows the clip tool with a move that scored badly: the picture mirrors, but the cap does not treat the two sides alike.
The mathematics. The solid line is flat to the left of the clip edge and then keeps falling to the right, while the dashed line falls throughout, so a change that made the bad move more likely keeps its full penalty.
Example. Take the paddle pushing down on a screen where down loses the point: the tool pushes down only to the edge, yet the penalty for playing down more stays whole.

8 When PPO fits

PPO is a robust default for games, robots and language-model fine-tuning, when fresh batches are cheap enough to collect and one set of settings should work across many tasks.

Prefer deep Q-network (DQN) family methods when steps are costly and replay is needed. For smooth moves, amounts from a range such as joint angles or steering, in robot control with few samples, see PPO for smooth moves and for language models.

9 What breaks PPO

  • The clip range or the number of passes is too large: the KL blows up.
  • The advantages are not normalized: one batch’s pushes dwarf another’s.
  • The critic is weak: its guesses are wrong, so \(\hat A_t\) is wrong too.
  • The paddle stops being undecided too early: raise \(c_H\).
  • Clipping does not strictly bound the KL: one large step can still overshoot the edge. Add an early stop on the KL.
