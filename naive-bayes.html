Naive Bayes

Classifying by counting words.

Sorting 15,000 forum posts without reading them

You know me, I love a question that sounds impossible and turns out to be bookkeeping. Here is one: how do you sort 15,076 forum posts into 20 newsgroups without reading a single one? You do not read. You count. The post in front of you says "windows" twice and "motif" twice, and you ask: which pile uses those words the most? That is naive Bayes. It is a classifier that treats every document as a bag of words, counts the bag, and bets on the class whose counts look most like it.

Documents are bags of words

The first move is to throw away word order. "I need to port several applications" becomes a bag: port x1, applications x1, need x1. The notebook in this series does exactly this with a custom CountVectorizer over the 20 Newsgroups corpus: 15,076 training posts become rows of 10,000 word counts. Order gone, counts kept. It feels lossy, and it is, but for topic sorting the counts carry almost all the signal. A comp.windows.x post says "windows" a lot. A rec.sport.hockey post never does. The bag knows.

Bayes rule, then the naive part

Here is the whole classifier in one line.
P(c | d) ∝ P(c) · Πw P(w | c)
The left side is the answer we want: how likely class c is, given document d. The bar reads "given", so P(w | c) is the probability of word w given the class. The prior P(c) is how common the class is before we read anything. The likelihood multiplies, with the big Π, each word's frequency in the class's posts over every word w in the document. The ∝ sign reads "is proportional to": the missing factor is the same for every class, so the ranking is right. The product is the naive part: we pretend words appear independently, which is obviously false ("san" and "francisco" are not independent events) and works anyway.

In the notebook, comp.windows.x posts are 0.0513 of the training set, so every document starts with that as its comp.windows.x score before a single word is counted. The word "naive" is doing a lot of heavy lifting in this algorithm's name.

Each word's likelihood is a count with Laplace smoothing, the add-a-little-to-everything trick.

A word's likelihood in a class is its smoothed share of the class's words.
P(w | c) = count(w, c) + αNc + α·V
count(w, c) is how many times word w appears in class c's training posts, and Nc is the class's total word count. α is the smoothing constant, added to every word's count. V is the vocabulary size, 10,000 here, so the bottom grows by α times V.

With the notebook's α of 0.1, every word starts as if seen a tenth of a time. Without it, one word the class never saw gets probability zero, and the whole product dies. It is stupid easy and it saves the entire classifier.

The code never multiplies thousands of tiny probabilities, because that underflows to zero:

import numpy as np
p = np.full(1000, 1e-4)  # 1,000 word probs
print(np.prod(p))        # 0.0, underflow
print(np.log(p).sum())   # -9210.34, fine
So the notebook scores each class in log space: same math, sums instead of products.
score(c) = log P(c) + Σw nw·log P(w | c)
nw is how many times word w appears in the document, and Σ adds over the vocabulary (words not in the document have nw = 0). The class with the largest score wins, the same winner as the product.

At α = 0 an unseen word would give log 0, negative infinity, so this page's tool floors the score at a large negative number and the class counts as zeroed out.

The tool in 'Every word moves all 20 bars' replays that moment on a toy corpus written for this page: 62 short posts, three for each of 19 newsgroups and five for comp.windows.x, with that exact post in the comp.windows.x pile. So its bars at rest show the toy priors, 3 or 5 posts out of 62, not the notebook's. The math is the notebook's, with α defaulting to 0.1.

Here is the notebook's classifier, stripped to the parts that matter. It is the multinomial version, the one that scores a document from how many times each word appears rather than just whether it appears:

import numpy as np

class MultinomialNB:
    def __init__(self, alpha=1.0):
        self.alpha = alpha  # Laplace smoothing parameter

    def fit(self, X, y):
        # P(word|class) = (count of word in class + alpha) /
        #                (total words in class + alpha * vocab size)
        unique_classes = np.unique(y)
        num_features = X.shape[1]
        self.feature_log_prob_ = np.zeros((len(unique_classes), num_features))
        # P(class) = documents in the class / total documents
        self.class_prior_ = np.array([np.sum(y == cls) for cls in unique_classes]) / len(y)
        for i, cls in enumerate(unique_classes):
            X_cls = X[y == cls]
            total = X_cls.sum() + num_features * self.alpha
            self.feature_log_prob_[i, :] = np.log((X_cls.sum(axis=0) + self.alpha) / total)

    def predict_log_proba(self, X):
        # log joint score = log prior + doc . log P(word|class), proportional to log P(class|doc)
        return np.log(self.class_prior_) + X @ self.feature_log_prob_.T

Next: k-means finds clumps with no labels at all

Naive Bayes needed labeled piles: 15,076 posts, each already stamped with its newsgroup. But what if nobody labeled anything? What if you just have the points and want to find the clumps? That is k-means: no labels, no counting words, just points picking the nearest center until nobody moves. It is the unsupervised mirror of everything on this page.
