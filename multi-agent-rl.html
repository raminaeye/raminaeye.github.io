Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning

Two rackets share one DDPG actor and critic, so each trains against an opponent that keeps improving.

Single-agent RL, as on the DQN page (a banana-collecting agent) and the DDPG page (the Reacher arm), assumes the world's rules stay fixed while the agent learns. In tennis each racket's world contains the other racket, which keeps learning.

The running example is the TennisGym repo, the collaboration and competition project from Udacity's deep RL nanodegree. A gym, in RL usage, is the environment an agent trains in: it receives actions and returns observations and rewards. This one is built with Unity ML-Agents.

In self-play one DDPG actor and critic play both sides of the net. Self-play gives an opponent that gets harder exactly as fast as the learner improves, but it also makes old experience go out of date: a lesson learned against yesterday's opponent may not hold today.

Both rackets act every step, but learning runs in bursts (Figure 1).

1 The tennis environment

A rally runs until a racket misses: the ball hits the ground or goes out. Each racket sees 8 numbers, the position and velocity of the ball and of itself, and answers with 2 continuous actions, scoot toward or away from the net and jump. The gym stacks three consecutive snapshots, so the actor gets 24 numbers per step and sees how the ball's motion changes across three steps (Figure 3).

The snippet runs one step from Python. brain_name is the key of the rackets in the output of env, states is (2, 24), one row per racket, and the step returns one reward and one done flag per racket.

import numpy as np

N_RACKETS, N_ACTIONS = 2, 2
out = env.reset(train_mode=True)
info = out[brain_name]
states = info.vector_observations
shape = (N_RACKETS, N_ACTIONS)
rand = np.random.uniform
actions = rand(-1, 1, shape)
out = env.step(actions)
info = out[brain_name]
rewards = info.rewards
dones = info.local_done
  1. 1Resets the court and reads both rackets' states.
  2. 2Sends one random action per racket and reads rewards and done flags.

The other racket is not in the observation; the ball is the only signal from it. This is partial observability: the agent cannot see what it most needs to predict. The actions are continuous, so the actor-critic method of the DDPG page applies.

Each racket's episode score adds 0.1 for every clear over the net and subtracts 0.01 for every drop.
score = 0.1·clears − 0.01·drops
Every clear of the net pays 0.1, and every drop, a ball let fall or hit out of bounds, costs 0.01. A racket earns 0.1 only for balls it hits over the net itself. In the repo's loop an episode does not stop at the first miss. It counts every step on which a racket is done and stops after the 11th such step, or after 2,500 steps.
The tennis task counts as solved when the better racket's score, averaged over the last 100 episodes, reaches 0.5.
best = max(scoreA, scoreB)
scoreA and scoreB are the two rackets' episode scores from the card above. The max keeps the better racket in each episode, because one good player is enough and the other is the sparring partner.
1100Σepisodes best ≥ 0.5
The fraction and the sum average best over the last 100 episodes, so one lucky episode cannot pass the bar. At 0.1 a clear, 0.5 means about five of its own clears per episode for the better racket.

Rewards come often, since every return over the net pays 0.1.

2 Self-play DDPG

One actor and one critic are shared by both rackets. Both rackets' transitions go into one replay buffer. Each racket treats its opponent as part of the environment, even though the same networks control the opponent. This simple setup solved the task (Figure 8).

The two sides improve together: one side returns a few more balls, rallies get longer, the other side learns to return more, and so on. No hand-tuned opponent schedule is needed.

Figure 4. One training step; both rackets share one actor and one critic.

The recipe is DDPG from the DDPG page, with the settings in Figure 5. The actor's tanh keeps both actions in [−1, 1]; the critic takes the 24 observations plus the 2 actions. Both networks use batch norm, which keeps each layer's inputs on a steady scale, and gradient clipping, which caps each update; the critic also uses weight decay, a small pull of every weight toward zero. Both learn against target networks, slow copies of the actor and critic.

After each update, the target networks move a small share of the way toward the live ones.
θ′ ← τθ + (1 − τ)θ′
θ are the live network weights, θ′ the target copy, and τ the small share. The arrow means "is replaced by": the target becomes τ parts live and 1 − τ parts its old self. A small τ makes the target trail the live network slowly, so the targets the critic learns toward change slowly; τ = 1 would copy the live network every step.

Exploration uses the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) noise of the DDPG page, so a jump lasts a few steps instead of one. Its κ pulls the noise back toward zero, σ sets the size of each kick, and the repo divides each sample by 5. In the loop, s holds both rackets' states, agent is the shared DDPG agent, noise its exploration noise and buf the one replay buffer both rackets fill, with add and sample(n); learn is the update in Section 8.

import numpy as np

BATCH, LEARN_EVERY, N_UPDATES = 128, 64, 128
max_steps, BREAK_AFTER = 2500, 10
count = 0

for t in range(max_steps):
    a = agent.act(s)
    a = a + noise.sample() / 5
    a = np.clip(a, -1, 1)
    out = env.step(a)
    info = out[brain_name]
    s2 = info.vector_observations
    r = info.rewards
    d = info.local_done
    rows = zip(s, a, r, s2, d)
    for row in rows:
        buf.add(*row)
    s = s2
    ready = len(buf) >= BATCH
    due = t % LEARN_EVERY == 0
    if due and ready:
        for _ in range(N_UPDATES):
            b = buf.sample(BATCH)
            learn(b)
    if np.any(d):
        count += 1
    if count > BREAK_AFTER:
        break
  1. 1Acts with exploration noise, clips to [−1, 1], and steps the court; a is (2, 2), one row per racket. The repo clips first and adds the noise after, so its actions can leave [−1, 1].
  2. 2Stores one transition per racket in the shared buffer.
  3. 3Every LEARN_EVERY steps, runs N_UPDATES updates on random batches.
  4. 4Counts the done steps and ends the episode after more than 10 of them, or after 2,500 steps.

The buffer holds the last million transitions and gains only two per step, so its contents change slowly and most samples come from older versions of the opponent. That steadies learning when the world is fixed and slows adaptation when the opponent changes (Section 3).

3 Non-stationarity from a learning opponent

A replay buffer assumes past experience previews the future, which held for the DQN banana agent and the DDPG Reacher arm. In tennis, what happens after each shot depends on the other racket, and that racket's policy changes with every update. This is non-stationarity: the world changes while the agent learns it. A lesson such as "hit the ball deep" stops working once the opponent learns to stand deep.

Training only on recent transitions would give up the large replay buffer that keeps DDPG stable. Multi-agent RL has other fixes: opponent modeling (predicting the opponent's next move), centralized critics (a critic that sees both rackets' observations and actions during training), and curricula (opponents or tasks that get harder step by step). The repo uses the simplest setup: one shared actor and critic.

6 How the reward shapes the curve

Once rallies start, the score rises fast, because of the reward asymmetry.

In one rally the racket that dropped the ball earns its clears and pays for one drop; the other racket only earns its clears.
rally score = 0.1 × n − 0.01
n is the number of clears by one racket, about half the hits in the rally, and the single 0.01 is the drop that ends it. A clear is worth ten drops, so the score rises in a straight line with n: one clear barely beats the drop, and 6 clears by the racket that dropped, a rally of 12 or 13 hits, pass 0.5. The other racket pays no drop, so its 0.1 × n reaches 0.5 at 5 clears. Longer rallies, not fewer mistakes, drive the score, and failed early attempts cost little.

7 The trained rackets

At test time the replay buffer and the critic are not used, and the repo turns the OU noise off (add_noise=False in Tennis.ipynb). Only the actor runs, in eval mode, with no gradients; each action comes out in [−1, 1].

8 Self-play DDPG in practice

The shared agent is a DDPG agent whose buffer receives two transitions per step, with the settings in Figure 5 (TennisGym, scripts/agent.py). Each update costs two target forward passes, a critic forward and backward pass, an actor pass through the critic, and two weight blends; 128 updates every 64 steps is 2 per environment step.

learn is one shared update on a batch from the common buffer. It is the DDPG update with the repo's gradient clipping on both networks.

import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F
import torch.nn.utils as U

GAMMA, TAU, CLIP = 0.99, 1e-3, 1.5
tgts = actor_target, critic_target
nets = actor, critic

def soft_update(tgt, live):
    dst = list(tgt.parameters())
    src = list(live.parameters())
    with torch.no_grad():
        for t, p in zip(dst, src):
            t.lerp_(p, TAU)

@torch.no_grad()
def td_target(r, s_next, done):
    a2 = actor_target(s_next)
    q2 = critic_target(s_next, a2)
    keep = 1 - done
    return r + GAMMA * q2 * keep

def learn(batch):
    s, a, r, s_next, done = batch
    y = td_target(r, s_next, done)
    q = critic(s, a)
    critic_loss = F.mse_loss(q, y)
    critic_opt.zero_grad()
    critic_loss.backward()
    w = critic.parameters()
    U.clip_grad_norm_(w, CLIP)
    critic_opt.step()
    q = critic(s, actor(s))
    actor_loss = -q.mean()
    actor_opt.zero_grad()
    actor_loss.backward()
    w = actor.parameters()
    U.clip_grad_norm_(w, CLIP)
    actor_opt.step()
    for t, n in zip(tgts, nets):
        soft_update(t, n)
  1. 1Sets the constants, pairs each target network with its live network, and defines soft_update.
  2. 2Builds the target y from the target networks; keep is 0 at the end of an episode (the DDPG critic target).
  3. 3Critic step, with gradient clipping.
  4. 4Actor step, with gradient clipping.
  5. 5Soft-updates both target networks (soft target update card in Section 2, line 1).

Two mistakes to avoid.

  • Store each racket's transition from its own side of the court; mixing up which observation goes with which action trains the shared actor on wrong pairs.
  • Judge progress by the 100-episode average of the better racket's score, the environment's own measure, not by one racket or one episode.

9 MADDPG

MADDPG (Lowe et al., 2017) gives each agent its own actor and its own critic. During training each critic is centralized: it sees every agent's observations and actions, so it can judge one agent's action knowing what the others did. At test time each actor sees only its own observations. This is called centralized training with decentralized execution. The authors' code is openai/maddpg.

The paper argues that Q-learning struggles here because the environment is non-stationary, and that the variance of policy gradients (how much the actor's learning signal varies from batch to batch) grows with the number of agents. It trains each agent as an ensemble, several policies with one picked per episode, so it does not overfit one opponent, and shows coordination on particle-world tasks such as cooperative navigation and predator-prey.

The TennisGym setup is simpler than MADDPG: its critic sees one racket's 24 numbers and 2 actions at a time.

10 DQN, DDPG and self-play compared

DQN scores a list of actions, DDPG outputs the move, and self-play outputs the move for two players with one shared actor.
a = argmaxa Q(s, a)
DQN's network Q scores every move a in state s, and argmax plays the highest score. That only works while the list is short, like the four moves of the DQN banana agent.
a = μ(s)
DDPG's actor μ outputs the continuous actions directly from the state, and the critic Q(s, a) only scores the action during training. No argmax anywhere. That is the Reacher arm on the DDPG page.
ai = μ(oi)
In self-play the same μ drives both rackets, each from its own observations oi, with i naming racket A or racket B. The formula is DDPG's with an index added: each racket's world contains the other, and the shared networks keep rewriting the opponent it plays against.

In the DQN, DDPG and self-play setups the reward comes from the environment. What if the reward has to come from people judging which answer is better? The Aligning Language Models with RL page answers it.
