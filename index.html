The classics that still run the world. How much can you learn without a single GPU (graphics processing unit, the chip that trains large models fast)?

  • Linear RegressionFitting a line by walking downhill. Gradient descent versus the closed-form answer (the exact formula that jumps straight to the best line), and what learning rate (the size of each step the walk takes) does to the walk.
  • Logistic RegressionA line that says yes or no. The sigmoid, an S-shaped curve that squeezes any score into the range from 0 to 1, turns a score into a probability, and 0.5 draws the boundary.
  • Multilayer Perceptrons (MLP)When a line is not enough. Its layers each mix the numbers they receive with learned weights and bend the mix, so stacked layers can bend the boundary too. One dial grows the model from a straight boundary to a smooth curve to a crazy overfit, where the model learns the training examples so closely that it does worse on new ones.
  • Decision Trees, Random Forests, and BoostingTwenty questions, then a forest. One tree memorizes, many vote, and boosting fits the last round's mistakes.
  • Support Vector Machines (SVM)Maximum margin (the widest empty gap you can leave between the boundary and the nearest points on each side). Hinge loss (the penalty for points on the wrong side of the margin), support vectors (the borderline points that hold the margin up), and the C knob that trades margin width against violations.
  • k-Nearest Neighbors (k-NN)Ask the neighbors. No training, just voting, and one slider that shows bias (oversmoothing the pattern away) versus variance (twitching at every noisy neighbor) live.
  • Naive BayesClassifying by counting. Each word moves the class bars, and smoothing (adding a small count to everything) stops zero counts from zeroing everything.
  • k-Means ClusteringFind the clumps with no labels. Two steps repeated: points pick the nearest center, centers jump to the middle.
  • Principal Component Analysis (PCA)Faces from parts. The notebook's eigenfaces, the ghostly basic face patterns that get mixed in different amounts to build a face, rebuilt in your browser: one slider from ghost to person.
  • t-SNEThe name expands to t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding. PCA's nonlinear follow-up. Neighbor probabilities, the heavy-tailed t (which lets far pairs stay vaguely far, so clusters get room to separate), and the perplexity knob (how many effective neighbors each point gets) that remakes the map.
  • Recommender SystemsPeople who liked this. Taste as geometry: same-taste users versus same-content movies, side by side.

CNNs (convolutional neural networks, networks that slide small pattern detectors over an image), RNNs (recurrent neural networks, networks that read a sequence step by step and carry memory forward), loss functions (the scores that measure how wrong a prediction is and guide training), scaling (how models change as they grow in data, size, and compute). What does a kernel (one of those small pattern detectors, a little grid of numbers slid over the input) actually see?

  • Convolutional Neural NetworksHow convolutions actually work, what the receptive field (the patch of the image one neuron can see) is doing, and why stacked small kernels (the little pattern detectors that slide over the image) beat one big one.
  • Recurrent Neural Networks: RNN, GRU, and LSTMSequences and memory, why gradients (the signals that tell each weight which way to move to reduce the loss) vanish on the way back, and the gates (learned doors that decide what memory passes and what is forgotten) that fix it. The GRU (gated recurrent unit) is a linear highway for memory, and the LSTM (long short-term memory) guards a separate memory cell with gates like those.
  • Cross-Entropy LossHow a model scores one prediction. BCE (binary cross entropy, how you score a coin flip prediction, where the model states a probability and the loss measures how wrong that probability was), cross entropy, and why confident-and-wrong is ruinous.
  • Gradient DescentThe loss (a score of how wrong the model's predictions are) over all the weights is a landscape. Walk it downhill, then race three optimizers (the methods that decide how the weights actually move downhill at each step) on one steep valley.
  • CTC LossWhat if you don't know which frame said what? Blank tokens (a no-label token that soaks up extra frames), dynamic programming (summing all the possible alignments by reusing partial sums), and beam search (keeping only the best few candidate outputs at each step), so the alignments get searched without trying every one.
  • Understanding Model ErrorsBias (the error from a fit that is too simple and oversmooths the pattern away), variance (the error from a fit that twitches at noise and changes with the sample), and the errors you cannot train away. Know your floor before you blame your model.
  • Regularization and Data AugmentationYour model memorized the training set. Dropout (randomly switching neurons off during training), weight penalties (a cost on large weights), and fake data that actually works.
  • OptimizersSGD (stochastic gradient descent, stepping on one small batch at a time) zigzags, momentum (carrying some of the last step's movement forward) glides, Adam (adapting each parameter's step size) adapts. What each optimizer is actually fixing, and the one demo that shows it.
  • Scaling LawsHow much loss (how wrong the model is, as one score) does a dollar buy? The brutal exchange rate between compute and loss, and how to split a fixed budget.
  • Self-Supervised LearningTeach a model without labels. SimCLR (pull two views of one image together, push the others apart), BYOL (the same pull with no negatives at all), and what the temperature, the knob that sharpens or softens the comparison, is actually doing to the loss.

No labels, no dataset, just a scoreboard. Learning from rewards: the agent acts, the world answers with a score. How does anything learn to win?

  • Reinforcement Learning (RL)Learning from rewards: the agent acts, the world answers with a score. Bananas included.
  • Deep Q-Networks (DQN)Teach an agent the Q value of every move (Q for quality, how good that move is), then it just picks the best one. Watch it stop eating blue bananas.
  • Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG)DQN picks buttons; DDPG turns dials. Continuous control with an actor that moves and a critic that judges.
  • Multi-Agent Reinforcement LearningTwo rackets, both learning at once. Your opponent never sits still.

Transformers are models built by stacking attention blocks. Attention is weighted averaging: each position looks at the others, weighs how much each one matters, and averages their information with those weights. So why does the bill come out quadratic (growing with the square of the length, so doubling the input makes the work four times as large)?

  • TransformersAttention is just weighted averaging with learned weights. Masks (hiding the parts a token, one chunk of the input the model reads as a single unit, may not look at), multihead (several attention heads running in parallel, each learning a different relationship), KV caches (saving each token's key vector, the part a query scores against to decide how much to listen to that token, and value vector, the content that gets mixed in when a token is listened to, so they are not recomputed at every step), and the quadratic bill, all on toy examples.
  • AdaptersFine-tune a giant model while training almost nothing. Rank, the size of the tiny trainable update, is a dial, not a magic number.
  • ConformerThe speech model that hears both the phoneme (one distinct sound of a language) and the sentence. Convolution plus attention, macaron style (attention and convolution sandwiched between two feed-forward layers, small networks that process each position on its own).

LLMs (large language models, giant models trained on text) in practice: watching what they understand (reading the lists of numbers inside the model that describe what it has understood so far) and catching misuse before they answer.

  • Activation ProbesTiny classifiers that read a model's internal activations (the lists of numbers inside the model that describe what it has understood so far) to flag harmful prompts, before it answers.

Half the bits, same model. Which weights (the learned numbers inside the model) can you cut and get away with it?

  • QuantizationThe lazy version of quantization, and that is not a bad thing. Fewer bits, same model, almost free.
  • Neural Architecture Search (NAS)Let the machine design the model. The search space (the set of candidate model designs the search can choose among) matters more than the search, so build it carefully.
  • Knowledge DistillationShrink a giant model into a small one without much performance loss. The teacher's soft labels, its full probability distribution over the classes rather than one hard answer, do the teaching.
  • PruningCut the weights that do not matter and keep the accuracy. Sparsity, the fraction of weights cut away, is free compression if you do it right.

If analog (a continuous signal that can take any value) is a ramp, an analog-to-digital converter (ADC) is a staircase. Where do the ghosts (the fake low frequencies a high frequency folds into when it is sampled too slowly) come from?

  • ADC FundamentalsIf analog input is a ramp, ADC is a staircase. Bits, noise, and the 6 dB rule, each extra bit buys about 6 dB of signal-to-noise ratio, made playable.

One model, many tasks. When the tasks fight, whose gradient (the signal that tells each weight which way to move to reduce its task's loss) wins?

  • Introduction to Multitask LearningOne model, many tasks: the diseases, the geometry, the fixes, and how to pick one.
  • The Multitask Field GuideDiagnose which disease you have, avoid the five mistakes, batch jointly, and admit only the losses that must shape the trunk.
  • MGDA (Multiple Gradient Descent Algorithm)Blend the task gradients into the shortest legal mix (a weighted mix whose weights are not negative and add to one, chosen to make the combined gradient as short as possible) and step along it.
  • GradNormLearn one volume knob per task so every task trains at the same pace.
  • Uncertainty WeightingSet each task's volume from the data's noise.
  • PCGradWhen gradients fight, cut only the part that fights back.
  • GradVacNudge each pair of task gradients back to its own healthy level of cooperation (how much that pair's gradients usually agree, measured by their cosine similarity, a score for how far two vectors point the same way, near 1 when they point together and lower as they point apart).
  • CAGrad (Conflict-Averse Gradient Descent)Help the worst-off task without losing sight of the average.

About

Currently: AI research scientist at Meta, working on multitask models for sensory data.

I studied at the University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign and started in audio ML (machine learning, ML for short, applied to audio), interning at several industries, including the Advanced Digital Sciences Center in Singapore and Adobe. I later joined Dolby Labs, working on deep learning (neural networks with many layers, trained on data) and audio codecs (the technology that encodes audio into a compressed form and decodes it back for playback). Later, as the first hire at CurieAI, I helped develop models that detect respiratory symptoms from noisy audio, scaling the technology to hundreds of patients.

Looking to push myself into unfamiliar territory, I pivoted to NLP (natural language processing, working with text and language). At UnitedHealth Group, I was again the first ML lead on the team. I built natural language search for health benefits that became the foundation for an enterprise-wide semantic search platform (semantic search matches by meaning, not only by the exact words typed). As the ML lead for the enterprise team, I trained language models from scratch, scaled the team to over 50 people, and reshaped how ML was applied to search through my experiences with sensory signals.

At Precision Neuroscience, I applied cutting-edge signal processing (finding and pulling useful patterns out of signals like brain waves and audio) and machine learning to brain-computer interfaces. Basically, translating brain signals into real-time actions. Currently, I work at Meta as an AI research scientist on multitask models for sensory data.
