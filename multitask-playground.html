The setup

I keep one sandbox where every method has to show its work. Two gradient arrows, g1 and g2, one pull per task on the shared model. You drag them wherever you want, pick a combination rule, and the sandbox draws the update that rule would take: the gold arrow d. Same fight, four philosophies, and the scores say who gets helped.

This is a toy, and it says so everywhere it matters. Two arrows stand in for whole task gradients, and the four rules are the methods' actual math: the plain sum, PCGrad's surgery, MGDA's shortest blend, CAGrad's worst-task tilt. Nothing else lives here.

The score

Every verdict on this page comes from one number, the score $\langle d, g_i \rangle$, my referee. Take the update arrow d and dot it with task i's gradient $g_i$. If the score is positive, the step pushes task i's loss down. If it is negative, the step pushes the loss up, and that task is betrayed.

Read it aloud with real numbers, one computation per line. Take d = (2, 3) and g1 = (3, 1). Multiply the x parts: 2 times 3 is 6. Multiply the y parts: 3 times 1 is 3. Add: 6 + 3 = 9. Positive, so task 1's loss goes down. Now the other fight: d = (1.5, 1.5) against g2 = (-1.5, 0.5). x parts: 1.5 times -1.5 is -2.25. y parts: 1.5 times 0.5 is 0.75. Add: -2.25 + 0.75 = -1.5. Negative: the step pushes task 2's loss up. That second pair is not random. It is the loud-vs-quiet fight, and it gets its own section below.

The shaded half-planes on the canvas are the betrayal zones: every direction that would hurt a task, drawn as a region. Any update arrow ending inside the purple zone betrays task 1; inside the green zone it betrays task 2. A good method's gold arrow never ends in a shaded zone.

The four methods

Four methods, four answers to the same question: given two pulls, which single arrow do we actually take? Each one below goes idea first, then the equation, then real numbers.

Plain sum (GD)

The naive answer: add the pulls. $d = g_1 + g_2$. No questions asked, no conflict detected. It is the baseline every other method has to beat.

Loud vs quiet, plain sum. g1 = (3, 1), g2 = (-1.5, 0.5). Add them: (3 + -1.5, 1 + 0.5) = (1.5, 1.5). Score for task 1: 1.5 times 3 plus 1.5 times 1 = 4.5 + 1.5 = 6.0, helped. Score for task 2: 1.5 times -1.5 plus 1.5 times 0.5 = -2.25 + 0.75 = -1.5, HURT. The big gradient drags the sum, the gold arrow lands in the green betrayal zone, and task 2 pays for it.

One labeling note, kept plain: on this page GD and PCGrad use the gradient sum. The CAGrad page's racer averages instead. Each page is internally consistent.

PCGrad

Surgery. When the gradients fight, cut the conflicting shadow: remove from each gradient the part that points against the other, then add what is left. When they do not fight, meaning their dot product is at least zero, surgery does nothing and PCGrad is the plain sum. Cooperation costs nothing.

When $\langle g_1, g_2 \rangle < 0$:

$$g_1' = g_1 - \frac{\langle g_1, g_2 \rangle}{\|g_2\|^2} g_2, \qquad g_2' = g_2 - \frac{\langle g_2, g_1 \rangle}{\|g_1\|^2} g_1, \qquad d = g_1' + g_2'$$

The fractions are the shadows: each gradient's projection onto the other. Subtracting them cuts exactly the conflicting part. Both shadows are cut at once, from the original arrows. The paper's own algorithm cuts them one at a time in shuffled order; this sandbox shows the simultaneous version.

Now loud vs quiet, surgery, one computation per line. First check the fight: $\langle g_1, g_2 \rangle$ = 3 times -1.5 plus 1 times 0.5 = -4.5 + 0.5 = -4.0. Negative, so cut. g2's squared length: $(-1.5)^2 + 0.5^2$ = 2.25 + 0.25 = 2.5. The shadow of g1 on g2: (-4.0 / 2.5) times (-1.5, 0.5) = -1.6 times (-1.5, 0.5) = (2.4, -0.8). Cut it: g1' = (3, 1) - (2.4, -0.8) = (0.6, 1.8). g1's squared length: $3^2 + 1^2$ = 10. The shadow of g2 on g1: (-4.0 / 10) times (3, 1) = -0.4 times (3, 1) = (-1.2, -0.4). Cut it: g2' = (-1.5, 0.5) - (-1.2, -0.4) = (-0.3, 0.9). The update: d = (0.6, 1.8) + (-0.3, 0.9) = (0.3, 2.7). Scores: $\langle d, g_1 \rangle$ = 0.3 times 3 + 2.7 times 1 = 0.9 + 2.7 = 3.6, helped. $\langle d, g_2 \rangle$ = 0.3 times -1.5 + 2.7 times 0.5 = -0.45 + 1.35 = 0.9, helped. Both positive: the fight is over. And the dashed edited gradients land at exact right angles to the task they were cut against: $\langle g_1', g_2 \rangle$ = 0.6 times -1.5 + 1.8 times 0.5 = -0.9 + 0.9 = 0.

MGDA

The shortest legal blend. Of all the ways to average the two gradients, take the shortest arrow. If that shortest blend has length near zero, the tasks are in a standoff: no direction helps both at once, a Pareto-stationary point.

$$w^* = \frac{\langle g_2, g_2 - g_1 \rangle}{\|g_1 - g_2\|^2}, \quad \text{clamped to } [0, 1], \qquad d = w^* g_1 + (1 - w^*) g_2$$

Read w* as the fraction of task 1 in the blend. The numerator measures how much g2 points along the difference of the two arrows; the denominator normalizes by the squared distance between them. The clamp keeps the blend between the two gradients instead of overshooting past one of them.

Check value, walked by hand so you can trust the sandbox's readout: g1 = (2, 0), g2 = (-1, 1.6). First g2 - g1 = (-1 - 2, 1.6 - 0) = (-3, 1.6). Numerator: $\langle g_2, g_2 - g_1 \rangle$ = (-1) times (-3) + 1.6 times 1.6 = 3 + 2.56 = 5.56. Denominator: $\|g_1 - g_2\|^2$ = $(2 - (-1))^2 + (0 - 1.6)^2$ = 9 + 2.56 = 11.56. w* = 5.56 / 11.56, about 0.481. No clamping needed. Drag the handles to (2, 0) and (-1, 1.6) and the blend-weight readout should read 0.48.

MGDA's paper

Ozan Sener, Vladlen Koltun

Multi-Task Learning as Multi-Objective Optimization.

NeurIPS 2018 · arXiv:1810.04650

Sener and Koltun's starting point: multi-task learning is inherently a multi-objective problem, because tasks conflict and force a trade-off. The standard compromise, a weighted linear combination of per-task losses, is only valid when the tasks do not compete, which is rarely true. Their move was to aim for Pareto-optimal solutions directly. The textbook MGDA (Désidéri, 2012) provably reaches a Pareto-stationary point, but it needs explicit per-task gradients and does not scale to deep nets. Their contribution is a Frank-Wolfe optimizer with an upper bound computable in a single backward pass, so the overhead is negligible, and they prove it still yields a Pareto-optimal solution under realistic assumptions.

CAGrad

Worst-task first, on a leash. Start from the average, then tilt the update toward whichever task is worst off. The leash length c sets how far the update may stray from the average.

$$g_0 = \frac{g_1 + g_2}{2}$$

The dashed circle on the canvas is centered on g0 with radius c times $\|g_0\|$. Inside the sandbox, CAGrad searches the blends $w g_1 + (1 - w) g_2$ on a fine grid for the one that most improves the worst-off task, then applies the paper's reference code rescale 1/(1+c), which shortens the arrow without changing its direction or any helped/HURT verdict.

Slide c from 0 to 1.5. At 0 the update collapses onto the plain average: same direction as the plain sum, half the length, so the helped/HURT verdicts do not change. As c grows, the dashed leash circle widens and the update tilts toward the worst-off task.

How to play
  • The point: Every multitask optimizer is a combination rule: two gradients in, one update out. This sandbox runs the actual math of four of them on arrows you place yourself, and the scores say which tasks each rule actually helps. Drag the arrows; same fight, four philosophies, read the scores.
  • What you'll see: two numbered handles, purple for g1 and green for g2: drag either anywhere inside the canvas. The gold arrow is the update d for the selected method. The shaded half-planes are the betrayal zones. Below, each task gets a live score box with a helped or HURT verdict. There is no Play button: dragging is the whole interaction.
  • Try this: load "Paper section 5" and flip between Plain sum and PCGrad; load "Loud vs quiet" and watch task 2's score go negative under the plain sum while PCGrad, MGDA, and CAGrad each fix it differently. Drag the handles yourself and find a configuration where MGDA's arrow nearly vanishes: a Pareto-stationary point.
  • Reset: there is no Reset button because there is no Play button. Dragging is live, and the four preset buttons snap the arrows back to named configurations any time.

Loud vs quiet, by hand

The fight, named: g1 = (3, 1) is loud, g2 = (-1.5, 0.5) is quiet. Their dot product: 3 times -1.5 + 1 times 0.5 = -4.0. They disagree, and the loud one shouts. Here is what each method does with it, exactly as the sandbox computes it:

Plain sum: d = (1.5, 1.5), scores 6.0 and -1.5. The loud task thrives; the quiet one is betrayed.

PCGrad: surgery gives d = (0.3, 2.7), walked step by step above. Scores 3.6 and 0.9. Both helped; the quiet task survives.

MGDA: the shortest blend uses w* of about 0.317, so d = 0.317 times (3, 1) + 0.683 times (-1.5, 0.5), about (-0.073, 0.659). Both scores land at about 0.44. That is MGDA's fingerprint: it equalizes the scores.

CAGrad, c = 0.5: d comes out about (0.165, 0.612), scores about 1.11 and 0.06. The tilt drags the quiet task's score just above zero; widen c toward 1.5 and both scores climb past 0.5. Same fight, four answers.

Load the "Loud vs quiet" preset and flip through the four buttons: the gold arrow and both scores trace exactly these numbers.
