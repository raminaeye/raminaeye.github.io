Knowledge Distillation

You have a giant model that works great and costs a fortune to run. How do you squeeze it into a small one without tanking performance? That is what Knowledge Distillation (KD) does, and the trick is embarrassingly simple: take a big, accurate, pre-trained model, call it the teacher, and train the small model, the student, to copy the teacher's behavior.

This is not a toy trick. DistilBERT is BERT-base with 40% of the parameters chopped off, and it still keeps 95% of the performance. Same story with the distilled MobileNet variants taught by ResNet-50. The pattern is always the same: the big model you cannot afford to serve teaches the small model you can.

The problem is a deployment problem. The most reliable way to squeeze more accuracy out of a model is to train a whole ensemble and average the predictions, but an ensemble is too expensive to serve, especially when the individual models are large neural nets. Earlier work showed an ensemble's knowledge could be compressed into a single model (Bucila et al.); distillation is the simplest version of that idea: train one small model that behaves like the big one.

The key insight: the teacher's soft outputs carry information the hard labels never will. A model that says an image is 90% cat, 8% dog, 2% car is telling you which mistakes are reasonable, and that ranking, the "dark knowledge", is what the student should learn.

Soften the teacher's softmax with a temperature and train the small model to match those soft targets, alongside the usual hard-label loss.

On MNIST the results surprised even the authors, and the technique significantly improved the acoustic model of a heavily used commercial system.

Soft predictions: what the teacher really knows

Instead of training the student model solely on the hard ground-truth labels (e.g., "this image is a cat"), the student also learns from the "soft" predictions produced by the teacher. These soft predictions are the probability distribution over all classes that the teacher model outputs. This distribution contains richer information, revealing how the teacher "thinks" (e.g., "this image is 90% likely a cat, 8% a dog, and 2% a car").

To make this information more useful, the teacher's output logits are typically softened using temperature scaling:

The temperature controls the smoothness of the output distribution. Take the teacher's raw scores for each class, the logits, divide them by the temperature, then run the usual softmax to get probabilities.

  • At a temperature of 1, you get the standard softmax output.
  • Above 1, the distribution becomes softer, shrinking the differences between class probabilities. This better reveals the teacher's knowledge about class similarities (e.g., that a truck is more similar to a car than to a cat).
  • Below 1, the distribution becomes sharper, approaching a one-hot encoding.

Using a higher temperature helps the student learn the teacher's inductive biases and provides a much better training signal, especially when true labels are sparse or noisy.

Distillation loss: match the teacher's soft targets

The student answers to two bosses at once: the true labels, and the teacher's soft predictions. One loss term for each, mixed together:

The final loss is a weighted average of these two components, controlled by a single mixing weight:

$$ \mathcal{L} = (1-\alpha) \cdot \mathcal{L}_{CE}(\text{student_logits, true_labels}) + \alpha \cdot \mathcal{L}_{KL}(\text{student_logits}_T, \text{teacher_logits}_T) $$

The point: the student learns from two signals at once. The frozen teacher supplies soft targets; the dataset supplies the true label; the mixed loss blends them at weight 0.7 and 0.3 and trains only the student.

The loss blends two lessons: the true labels keep the student honest, and the teacher's softened outputs show it the shape of the answer. The mixing weight decides how much of each lesson the student hears. The extremes tell the story: lean fully toward the teacher and the student learns pure imitation; lean fully toward the labels and the teacher fades out entirely.

In code it is a few lines: 

def blended_distillation_loss(student_logits, teacher_logits, labels, T=2.0, alpha=0.7):
    # 1. Distillation (soft target) loss: KL divergence between softened distributions
    soft_targets = F.softmax(teacher_logits / T, dim=1)
    student_log_probs = F.log_softmax(student_logits / T, dim=1)
    soft_loss = F.kl_div(student_log_probs, soft_targets, reduction='batchmean') * (T * T)

    # 2. Hard loss: standard cross entropy with ground truth
    hard_loss = F.cross_entropy(student_logits, labels)

    # 3. Weighted combination
    return alpha * soft_loss + (1.0 - alpha) * hard_loss

One detail worth noticing: the KL term is multiplied by the temperature squared. Dividing logits by the temperature shrinks the gradients by roughly the temperature squared, so the original paper suggests multiplying it back to keep the two loss terms on a comparable scale. Without it, the teacher's signal would barely move the weights.

What happens: distilling a ResNet-18 into a small CNN

The full experiment lives in the companion notebook. The setup: the teacher is a ResNet-18 (ImageNet-pretrained, classifier fine-tuned on STL-10), and the student is DeeperStudentCNN, a plain 4-block CNN with a fraction of the teacher's size. Distillation runs with temperature 2.0, mixing weight 0.7, Adam at learning rate 1e-3, for 10 epochs.

First, the teacher's accuracy, which is the target we are chasing:

92.58%

Then the student, trained with the distillation loss above:

53.46%
Student test accuracy (STL-10)
DeeperStudentCNN, response-based KD

53% is a long way from 93%, but keep perspective: this student is tiny, trained for only 10 epochs, and it learned entirely by watching the teacher. The loss fell steadily the whole way, so it was still improving when training stopped. Distillation got a small model somewhere meaningful in 10 epochs. That is a fast win.

I also tried forcing the student to mimic the teacher's intermediate features, adding an MSE term between feature maps alongside the CE and KL terms (mixing weights 0.5, 0.3, 0.2 across the three terms, temperature 2.0, same 10 epochs):

42.48%
Student test accuracy (STL-10)
DeeperStudentCNN, feature-based KD

Worse, not better. More supervision is not always better supervision. Matching raw activations across very different architectures is hard: feature scales differ, and forcing a small network to reproduce a big network's internals can fight the classification objective. More on that in the caveats.

Beyond logits: feature, relation, and self-distillation

Response-Based Distillation (Logit Distillation)

This is the one I used in the experiment above, and it is the most common form of KD you will run into. The student only watches the teacher's final outputs, nothing internal. You never see the teacher's reasoning process, but you rarely need to: the softened logits already carry most of the signal. DistilBERT did exactly this, and later distilled MobileNet variants too. The distillation loss is the KL divergence between the temperature-scaled outputs of the teacher and the student:

The student only has to match the teacher's softened outputs, and minimizing it drags the student's bars onto the teacher's, the same motion you watched in the temperature toy above.

Feature-Based Distillation

Here you make the student copy the teacher's intermediate features, not just the final answers. It sounds like strictly more supervision, and sometimes it helps. But remember what happened in my experiment: forcing a tiny student to reproduce a ResNet's internals dropped accuracy from 53.46% to 42.48%. Feature scales differ across architectures, and the extra objective can fight your classification loss. If you try this, normalize the features and pick the layer carefully.

Relation-Based Distillation

Instead of copying absolute values, the student copies relationships: which examples the teacher thinks are similar, how classes sit relative to each other in the teacher's space. Think of it as learning the teacher's map of the world rather than its exact coordinates. It is more forgiving when the student is too small to match the teacher's exact numbers.

Self-Distillation

The weird one: the model teaches itself. Train a teacher, then train a student with the exact same architecture on the teacher's outputs, and you somehow get a better model. It sounds like a free lunch, and it almost is: the soft targets act as a regularizer and smooth out the optimization. People usually iterate it once or twice and just bank the gains.

Distillation pitfalls: capacity gaps, temperature, and teacher mistakes

  • More supervision is not always better supervision. In my notebook, adding the feature-matching MSE term dropped the student from 53.46% to 42.48%. If you do feature distillation, normalize the features and pick the layer carefully, or you will fight your own classification objective.
  • Mind the capacity gap. A student that is far smaller than the teacher may simply be unable to mimic it, and distillation stalls. When the gap is huge, an intermediate "teacher assistant" model sometimes helps more than distilling directly.
  • Temperature needs tuning. Too low and you get hard labels with no dark knowledge; too high and every class looks equally likely, washing out the signal. Values between 2 and 5 are the usual starting range.
  • The student inherits the teacher's mistakes. Whatever biases and blind spots the teacher has get baked into the soft targets, so distill from a teacher you trust.
