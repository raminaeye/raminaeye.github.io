BYOL: Bootstrap Your Own Latent

Train one network to predict a slowly updated copy of itself, with no other images to push away, and it still does not collapse.

Before you start Self-Supervised Learning Contrastive Learning

On this page

As in Contrastive Learning, training pulls the embeddings of two random edits of a photo, its views, together, but here with no negatives (other images pushed away). Every training step, one network learns while its target, the other view’s embedding it must match, comes from a momentum encoder (a copy whose weights follow a moving average of the network’s) or from the same network with no gradient flowing through it (a stop-gradient). Each method adds one part that stops every image from getting the same output.

1 Pulling pairs together with no negatives should collapse

This page answers how BYOL learns with no negatives and without collapsing.

If the only signal is make the two views match, one vector for every image matches every pair and says nothing; this is collapse. Negatives forbid it; without them, nothing in the loss does.

In a subtler dimensional collapse, the embeddings spread along only a few directions of the space and stay flat along the rest; Failure Modes and Checks plots it.

2 BYOL predicts the output of a slowly updated copy of itself

BYOL (Bootstrap Your Own Latent, Grill et al. 2020; latent means the embedding space) trains one network to predict a momentum encoder’s embedding of the other view, with no negatives, and still does not collapse. Its trained online network turns view 1 into a prediction \(p_{1}\) through an encoder, a projector (a small head, as in SimCLR) and a predictor (a second small head that guesses the target). The target network, an encoder and projector with no predictor, turns view 2 into the target \(z_{2}\). It gets no gradients and is an exponential moving average (EMA) of the online weights.

Two views in

One photo, edited twice. Each view goes through its own side of the network.

The online side predicts, the target stays fixed

The online side turns view 1 into a prediction. The target side gives the target and takes no gradients.

The loss pulls them together

The loss is the gap between the prediction and the target. Lowering it turns the prediction toward the target.

The target follows by EMA

After the step, the target keeps most of its old weights and takes a small step toward the online weights.

BYOL’s loss is the squared distance between the unit-length prediction and the unit-length target, then the same with the views swapped, summed.
\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{L} = \|\htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{\bar{p}_{1}} - \htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{\bar{z}_{2}}\|^{2}\)
The loss \(L\) is the squared distance between two directions. The bar scales a vector to unit length, so \(\bar{p}_{1}\) and \(\bar{z}_{2}\) are the prediction and the target as directions, and \(\lVert\rVert^{2}\) is the squared length of the gap between them. Unit length makes the loss depend only on direction, so the network cannot lower it by shrinking its outputs toward zero, and prediction and target are compared on one scale whatever their raw sizes.
\(\displaystyle = 2 - 2\cdot \langle \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{\bar{p}_{1}}, \htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{\bar{z}_{2}}\rangle\)
Expanding the square of two unit vectors leaves 2 minus twice their dot product, written with angle brackets \(\langle , \rangle\). For unit vectors that dot product is the cosine of the angle between them, so \(L\) is at its lowest when the two agree and at its highest when they point opposite ways, and lowering \(L\) is the same as raising the cosine. The code computes it in this form.

The target follows MoCo’s momentum rule. Each step it keeps a share \(m\) of its old weights, and \(m\) rises from 0.996 to 1.

The snippet computes eq line 2 for both view orders on toy vectors; cosine_similarity scales both to unit length itself.

import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

def byol_loss(p, z):
    cos = F.cosine_similarity(p, z, dim=-1)
    return (2 - 2 * cos).mean()               # batch mean

B, D = 8, 256
p1, p2 = torch.randn(B, D), torch.randn(B, D)   # online predictions
z1, z2 = torch.randn(B, D), torch.randn(B, D)   # targets
L = byol_loss(p1, z2) + byol_loss(p2, z1)

  2. 2Toy predictions \(p\) and targets \(z\), the two ends of Figure 2.
  3. 3Section 2 eq line 1 plus the same with the views swapped.

With no negatives to collect, BYOL needs no big batch.

3 Three parts hold collapse off

Removing any one of three parts collapses BYOL. In BYOL’s own ablations, removing the predictor collapses it, and changing how the target updates collapses it too; a later method, SimSiam, removed the momentum encoder and kept only the stop-gradient and the predictor.

  1. The stop-gradient treats the target’s output as fixed, so the two sides cannot walk together to a constant.
  2. The predictor, on the online side only, learns to guess the target from the online output. A constant output gives it nothing to guess from, so the authors argue training moves away from the constant output (BYOL sec. 3.2).
  3. The EMA target lags the online network: the slow target changes little per step, so the authors argue the predictor can stay near the best prediction of it (BYOL sec. 3.2).

4 One BYOL training step, in code

This snippet runs one BYOL step, then moves the target toward the online network by EMA.

import copy, math
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F

def byol_loss(p, z):
    return (2 - 2 * F.cosine_similarity(p, z, dim=-1)).mean()

def mlp(d_in, d_out=256):
    return nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(d_in, 4096), nn.BatchNorm1d(4096), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(4096, d_out))

encoder = nn.Sequential(nn.Flatten(), nn.Linear(3 * 32 * 32, 512))
online = nn.Sequential(encoder, mlp(512))       # encoder + projector
predictor = mlp(256)
target = copy.deepcopy(online).requires_grad_(False)
params = [*online.parameters(), *predictor.parameters()]
opt = torch.optim.SGD(params, lr=0.2, momentum=0.9)
k, K_STEPS, M_BASE = 0, 1000, 0.996             # step k

x1, x2 = torch.rand(64, 3, 32, 32), torch.rand(64, 3, 32, 32)
p1, p2 = predictor(online(x1)), predictor(online(x2))
with torch.no_grad():
    z1, z2 = target(x1), target(x2)
L = byol_loss(p1, z2) + byol_loss(p2, z1)
opt.zero_grad()
L.backward()
opt.step()

m = 1 - (1 - M_BASE) * (math.cos(math.pi * k / K_STEPS) + 1) / 2
with torch.no_grad():
    for p_t, p_o in zip(target.parameters(), online.parameters()):
        p_t.mul_(m).add_(p_o, alpha=1 - m)
  1. 1The loss of Section 2 eq line 2, as in the snippet there.
  2. 2The two-layer MLP used for the projector and the predictor.
  3. 3Online network, predictor, target copy and optimizer, as in Figure 2; LARS (an SGD variant that scales each layer’s step to its weight size) is not in PyTorch, so SGD stands in.
  4. 4Toy views, predictions, no-gradient targets and the loss, Section 2 eq lines 1 and 2.
  5. 5Updates the online side only, the side trained by gradients in Figure 2.
  6. 6Cosine schedule, which lowers a value smoothly along a cosine curve, for \(m\) and the target EMA, the EMA arrow in Figure 2.

5 When to use BYOL

Use BYOL to pretrain an image encoder when labels are scarce, then put a linear probe or fine-tuning on top with the few labels you have. How far a few labels go after pretraining is taught on Self-Supervised Learning, with its figure.

The paper reports results on par with or better than the state of the art on transfer and semi-supervised benchmarks, so the field uses are image classification with few labels and transfer to new image tasks.

The paper’s run took about 8 hours on 512 TPU v3 cores, and BYOL needs no big batch (Figure 3).

BYOL runs every training step. It carries a target network alongside the online network, and updates the target by EMA after each step.

6 Failure modes and tips

No predictor. Cause: the predictor is removed from the online side. Symptom: the probe collapses to 0.2% (Figure 4). Fix: keep the predictor on the online side only.

Target copied every step. Cause: \(m\) is 0, so the target copies the online network each step. Symptom: the probe collapses to 0.3% (Figure 4). Fix: keep \(m\) high and rising to 1.

Frozen random target. Cause: \(m\) is 1 from the start, so the target never learns from the online network. Symptom: 18.8%, weak but not collapsed (Figure 4). Fix: let \(m\) rise to 1 only over the run, never start there.

Tips. Keep the predictor on the online side only. Keep \(m\) high and rising to 1. Small batches barely matter (Figure 3), so do not chase big batches for BYOL.

BYOL, SimSiam and DINO differ in what they add around one shared pattern: all three keep the target side behind a stop-gradient. BYOL keeps a target network that follows by EMA and predicts its output; SimSiam drops the target network and keeps only the stop-gradient and the predictor; DINO swaps the loss for teacher shares, centered and sharpened.

SimSiam: Simple Siamese Representation Learning comes next, which drops the target network entirely.
