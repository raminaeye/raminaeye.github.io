L1 and L2 Regularization

How a cost on the weights keeps a model simple: L2 shrinks every weight, L1 sets many exactly to zero, and weight decay is L2 applied in the update step.

1 What a weight penalty is and the problem it solves

A large MLP trained on noisy labels draws a boundary that bends around single points. It can only do that because its weights grew large: a sharp bend needs big weights. Logistic regression on data it can separate perfectly shows the same habit in its plainest form, inflating its weights without end to push every probability toward zero or one.

A weight penalty adds a cost for large weights to the training loss. Training then trades a little fit on the training data for smaller weights, and smaller weights mean a smoother, simpler function that carries over better to new data. The two common forms are L2, which charges for each weight’s square, and L1, which charges for its size.

The penalty acts at every optimizer step on every penalized weight. It keeps no state and costs one sum over the weights. Biases and normalization gains are usually left out, since they set offsets and scales rather than how sharply the function bends (common practice, shown with PyTorch parameter groups in Section 8).

2 The two penalties

Both add one term to the data loss. They differ only in how a weight’s cost grows with its size.

L2 adds the squares of the weights to the loss; L1 adds their sizes.
\(\displaystyle {\htmlClass{eqt sym-loss}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-loss)}{\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}}} = \htmlClass{eqt sym-loss}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-loss)}{\mathcal{L}}} + \htmlClass{eqt sym-lambda}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-lambda)}{\lambda}} \sum_i \htmlClass{eqt sym-w}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-w)}{w_i}}^{2}}\)
L2. \(\htmlClass{eqt sym-loss}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-loss)}{\mathcal{L}}}\) is the data loss, how wrong the model is on the training data. \(\htmlClass{eqt sym-lambda}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-lambda)}{\lambda}}\) (lambda) is the penalty strength: raise it and the weights shrink and the boundary smooths; at zero there is no penalty; very large and every weight sits near zero, so the model underfits. The sum runs over every penalized weight \(\htmlClass{eqt sym-w}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-w)}{w_i}}\). Squaring makes big weights cost far more than small ones, so the model prefers many small weights to a few large ones.
\(\displaystyle {\htmlClass{eqt sym-loss}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-loss)}{\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}}} = \htmlClass{eqt sym-loss}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-loss)}{\mathcal{L}}} + \htmlClass{eqt sym-lambda}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-lambda)}{\lambda}} \sum_i \big\lvert \htmlClass{eqt sym-w}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-w)}{w_i}} \big\rvert}\)
L1, the same structure as line 1 with a different cost shape. The bars take each weight’s size without its sign, so every unit of weight costs the same whether the weight is big or small. A weight that barely helps the fit is not worth its cost, so training sets it to zero.
So both trade data loss for smaller weights; L2 punishes the biggest weights hardest, while L1 charges evenly and empties out the weights that do little.

Both penalties are older than neural networks. L2 on a linear model is ridge regression (Hoerl and Kennard 1970), and L1 on a linear model is the lasso (Tibshirani 1996), both on Regression.

3 How each penalty pushes a weight

Training follows the slope of the loss, so what a penalty does is set by its slope. Lines 1 and 2 give each penalty’s push; lines 3 and 4 show the update that lands L1 weights exactly on zero.

Each penalty pushes on a weight with its gradient, and L1 needs one extra step to stop at zero.
\(\displaystyle {\frac{\partial}{\partial \htmlClass{eqt sym-w}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-w)}{w_i}}} \Big(\htmlClass{eqt sym-lambda}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-lambda)}{\lambda}}\, \htmlClass{eqt sym-w}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-w)}{w_i}}^{2}\Big) = 2\,\htmlClass{eqt sym-lambda}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-lambda)}{\lambda}}\, \htmlClass{eqt sym-w}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-w)}{w_i}}}\)
The fraction of curly d’s is the slope of the L2 penalty as one weight changes. The push is proportional to the weight: a big weight is pulled back hard, a small one gently, so the pull fades as the weight shrinks and the weight approaches zero without reaching it.
\(\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}&\frac{\partial}{\partial \htmlClass{eqt sym-w}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-w)}{w_i}}} \Big(\htmlClass{eqt sym-lambda}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-lambda)}{\lambda}}\, \big\lvert \htmlClass{eqt sym-w}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-w)}{w_i}} \big\rvert\Big) \\ &\quad = \htmlClass{eqt sym-lambda}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-lambda)}{\lambda}}\, \operatorname{sign}\!\big(\htmlClass{eqt sym-w}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-w)}{w_i}}\big)\end{aligned}}\)
sign gives the direction toward zero: plus for a positive weight, minus for a negative one. The L1 push has the same size whatever the weight, so a small weight is driven to zero as hard as a big one. At zero the slope is undefined, since the V has a corner there (a subgradient, any slope between the two sides, is used in its place).
\(\displaystyle {\htmlClass{eqt sym-w}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-w)}{u_i}} = \htmlClass{eqt sym-w}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-w)}{w_i}} - \htmlClass{eqt sym-eta}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-eta)}{\eta}}\, \frac{\partial \htmlClass{eqt sym-loss}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-loss)}{\mathcal{L}}}}{\partial \htmlClass{eqt sym-w}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-w)}{w_i}}}}\)
First take the ordinary step on the data loss alone. \(\htmlClass{eqt sym-eta}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-eta)}{\eta}}\) (eta) is the learning rate, the size of each step, and \(\htmlClass{eqt sym-w}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-w)}{u_i}}\) is the weight after that step.
\(\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}&\htmlClass{eqt sym-w}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-w)}{w_i}} \leftarrow \operatorname{sign}\!\big(\htmlClass{eqt sym-w}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-w)}{u_i}}\big) \\ &\times \max\!\big(\lvert \htmlClass{eqt sym-w}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-w)}{u_i}} \rvert - \htmlClass{eqt sym-eta}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-eta)}{\eta}}\htmlClass{eqt sym-lambda}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-lambda)}{\lambda}},\, 0\big)\end{aligned}}\)
Builds on line 3. Then shrink the weight toward zero by a fixed amount, the learning rate times the strength, and stop at zero instead of crossing it: max picks the larger of the shrunk size and zero, and sign puts the direction back. This is the proximal step, also called soft thresholding (Duchi and Singer 2009). It is what lands L1 weights exactly on zero, where line 2 is undefined.
So L2 pulls in proportion to size and never quite reaches zero, while L1 pushes evenly and, with the stop at zero, parks weak weights exactly there.

4 The geometric view

The same contrast has a picture. Read as a constraint, the penalty keeps the weights inside a ball (L2) or a diamond (L1) around the origin, and the solution is where the rings of equal data loss first touch that shape. The diamond’s corners lie on the axes, so the first touch is often a corner, where one weight is zero.

5 Weight decay: L2 inside the update

Instead of adding the L2 term to the loss, an optimizer can shrink the weights directly at each step. That is weight decay (Krogh and Hertz 1992), the form most training code uses. It builds on line 1 of the card above.

Shrink every weight by a small share, then take the data step.
\(\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}&\htmlClass{eqt sym-w}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-w)}{w}} \leftarrow \big(1 - \htmlClass{eqt sym-eta}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-eta)}{\eta}}\htmlClass{eqt sym-lambda}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-lambda)}{\lambda'}}\big)\, \htmlClass{eqt sym-w}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-w)}{w}} \\ &\qquad - \htmlClass{eqt sym-eta}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-eta)}{\eta}}\, \nabla_{\htmlClass{eqt sym-w}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-w)}{w}}} \htmlClass{eqt sym-loss}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-loss)}{\mathcal{L}}}\end{aligned}}\)
The bracket shrinks every weight by a small fixed share; the last term is the ordinary step down the data loss’s gradient. \(\htmlClass{eqt sym-lambda}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-lambda)}{\lambda'}}\) (lambda-prime) is the decay strength. Under plain SGD this is exactly the L2 penalty of line 1 again, with lambda-prime standing for twice lambda, because the L2 gradient is a pull proportional to the weight.
So for plain SGD, weight decay and an L2 term in the loss are the same thing written two ways.

With adaptive optimizers such as Adam the two stop being the same: an L2 term in the loss has its gradient rescaled per weight along with everything else, so weights with large gradients are barely regularized. AdamW applies the shrink directly in the update instead (see AdamW in Optimizers in Practice). PyTorch’s optimizers default to no weight decay, except AdamW at 0.01.

7 The tool: penalty geometry

A two-input logistic gesture detector: one input is an EMG channel’s envelope that carries the signal, the other is mostly noise. The plane shows the two weights, with rings of equal data loss around the unpenalized best fit. Pick a penalty and drag its strength; the dot follows gradient steps to the penalized answer.

Figure 4. Illustrative detector. L1 puts the noise weight at exactly zero; L2 only shrinks both.

With L1, raising the strength first shrinks both weights, then drives the noise weight onto the axis, where it stays while the signal weight keeps working. With L2 both bars shrink together and neither reaches zero. That is the whole difference between the two penalties, seen in one plane: a corner on the axis versus a round edge.

8 In practice

In PyTorch the usual pattern puts biases and normalization gains, the one-dimensional parameters, in their own group with no penalty. An L1 term is added to the loss by hand; L2 is set as the optimizer’s weight decay.

decay = [p for n, p in net.named_parameters() if p.ndim > 1]
no_decay = [p for n, p in net.named_parameters() if p.ndim <= 1]
l1 = sum(p.abs().sum() for p in decay)
loss = loss_fn(net(x), y) + lam * l1
opt = torch.optim.SGD([
    {"params": decay, "weight_decay": wd},
    {"params": no_decay, "weight_decay": 0.0},
], lr=lr)
  1. 1Matrices and kernels are penalized; biases and gains are not.
  2. 2The L1 term of line 2 in Section 2, added to the data loss.
  3. 3L2 as weight decay, the update of Section 5, on the decay group only.

Choose the strength on validation data, trying values spread evenly on a log scale, since useful strengths span several orders of magnitude.

  • Sparse click prediction. Ad click-prediction models with huge numbers of features use L1 so most weights are exactly zero and the model stays small to store and serve (McMahan et al. 2013).
  • Support vector machines. The \(C\) setting in an SVM acts as the inverse of an L2 strength: a small \(C\) is a strong penalty.
  • Ridge and lasso are the linear-model versions on Regression.
  • Magnitude pruning is a related idea: removing the smallest weights after training (Pruning).

Weaker regularization than expectedL2 added to the loss while training with Adamuse AdamW (Optimizers in Practice)

Underfitting, shifted outputsbiases and normalization gains penalizedparameter groups, as in the code above
The penalty hits some features far harder than othersinputs on very different scalesstandardize features first (the per-channel z-score in Data Augmentation, preprocessing)

Tips: tune the strength on validation, never on test data; report whether biases were penalized when comparing runs.
