This page builds on RL Basics and Aligning Language Models with RL. The terms below are the ones this page keeps using.

  • An agent is the learner that picks the moves. A state is the agent’s situation right now; a move, or action, is one choice the agent can make in a state; a reward is the number the world hands back after each move, good or bad. (RL Basics)
  • A token is a word or a piece of a word; a language model writes its reply one token at a time. (Aligning Language Models with RL)
  • Here the agent is the language model. Here the state is the prompt plus the tokens written so far, and the move is the next token. Here the reward is one number for the whole reply, given when the reply ends.
  • RL, short for reinforcement learning, is learning which moves to make from rewards rather than from labelled answers. (RL Basics)
  • A policy is the agent’s rule for choosing a move in each state; it may always pick the same move or pick by chance. The policy is the language model being trained to write replies. (RL Basics, Aligning Language Models with RL)

1Why the usual route is slow and costly

Training a language model from people's preferences the usual way, RLHF, reinforcement learning from human feedback, is slow and costly, because it keeps writing fresh replies while it trains.

The running example is Reddit TL;DR, a set of Reddit posts with a short summary written underneath. A model writes two summaries of one post, and the task is to say which summary is better.

A preference pair is one prompt, two replies, and a label saying which reply a person preferred. (Reward Models from Human Preferences)

DPO, direct preference optimization, learns straight from such pairs.

It trains no separate scorer and writes no replies while it trains.

1.1The usual route trains a scorer first

First the scorer is trained, then the model writes replies and is pushed toward high scores.

In the DPO paper, the summary pairs and people's picks come from an earlier OpenAI study of summaries.

Each summary in Figure 1 is only a sentence or two long.

DPO uses the two summaries of Figure 1 as a pair, with no score attached to either.

DPO trains on fixed pairs and never runs the write, score, update loop, in which the model writes fresh replies, scores them and updates.

DPO changes the policy by raising the chosen summary’s chance and lowering the rejected one’s.

A reward model is a language model with a one-number output, trained on people's comparisons so the reply they preferred scores higher. (Reward Models from Human Preferences)

DPO's loss instead scores each summary with the policy itself.

RLHF then trains the policy with PPO, proximal policy optimization, the usual RL method for language models.

DPO skips that loop entirely.

PPO is helped by a critic, a second network that predicts the score each partial reply, a reply only partly written, will end with.

DPO's loss needs no guess of how a partial summary will score, because it compares whole summaries.

SFT, supervised fine-tuning, trains the model on replies people wrote with the ordinary next-token loss; RL usually starts from that model. (Aligning Language Models with RL)

DPO starts from that model too.

The reference model \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\) is a frozen copy of the SFT model. (Aligning Language Models with RL)

KL measures how different two probability distributions are, zero when they match; the KL penalty charges the policy for drifting away from the reference model. (Aligning Language Models with RL)

DPO keeps only the policy and the frozen reference in memory.

2The policy already carries a reward

DPO's key result is that the policy itself carries a reward for every reply.

For any reward, the policy that earns the most reward minus the KL penalty is the reference with each reply boosted by its reward, as the best-policy card in the next section shows.

Closed-form means the best policy can be written down as a formula, with no training loop needed to find it, but the formula needs the true reward and a sum over every possible reply, so it cannot be used directly. The best-policy card in the next section is that formula.

Read backwards, this gives a reward for any policy, the implicit reward: how much more the policy likes a reply than the reference does.

So one supervised loss on fixed pairs can train the policy, as the implicit-reward card in the next section states.

3From the KL objective to a pairwise loss

An expected value is the average over everything that can happen by chance, weighted by how likely each outcome is. (Probability and Bayes Rule, RL Basics)

Every method in this family starts from the same KL objective.

4.1Only the policy trains, on a fixed set of pairs

A DPO step computes the loss on a batch, a group of fixed pairs trained on together, and takes one gradient step.

Training is one pass, or a few epochs, over a fixed pair dataset, each epoch one full pass over the pairs. The policy trains and the reference stays frozen.

Each pair needs two forward passes, two runs of the model over a summary to score it, for each model, one for the chosen summary and one for the rejected one. So training touches only the policy.

5The DPO loss in PyTorch

Inputs are each summary's summed token log-probabilities, shape (batch,), for the policy and the frozen reference (Aligning Language Models with RL, scoring a whole reply). The loss is minimized.

import torch.nn.functional as F
def dpo_loss(pol_w, pol_l, ref_w, ref_l, beta=0.1):
    r_w = beta * (pol_w - ref_w)                  # [1] line 3 of the implicit-reward card, chosen
    r_l = beta * (pol_l - ref_l)                  # [1] line 3 of the implicit-reward card, rejected
    loss = -F.logsigmoid(r_w - r_l).mean()        # [2] the DPO loss card
    margin = (r_w - r_l).detach()                 # [3] logged margin
    return loss, margin, r_w.detach()             # watch r_w too (see When the preferred reply gets less likely too)
  1. 1Line 3 of the implicit-reward card, chosen and rejected.
  2. 2The DPO loss card.
  3. 3The logged margin, watched in the section When the preferred reply gets less likely too.

6Chosen and rejected against the reference

Each bar is how likely the model is to write that reply, on a log scale, where each equal step along the bar multiplies the chance by the same amount. The loss only needs the gap to grow, so when the two replies look alike to the model, for example when they differ in only a few tokens, pushing the rejected one down also pulls the chosen one down, so both can become less likely; the section When the preferred reply gets less likely too names the papers behind this.

7Training with DPO in practice

TRL, Hugging Face's open library for training language models with these methods, has a DPO trainer. The DPO authors also publish an official reference implementation.

Because the reference model is frozen, its log-probabilities can be computed once before training, which saves memory and time.

During training, watch the logged margin and the chosen reward, the implicit reward of the chosen reply, which training libraries log.

The learning rate \(\eta\) sets how big a step each update takes. (RL Basics)

The paper ran three tasks: IMDb sentiment (movie reviews, to write positive ones), Reddit TL;DR, and Anthropic HH (helpful and harmless) single-turn dialogue. Judging used win rates, the share of comparisons where GPT-4 preferred this method’s reply over the reference summary (TL;DR) or the preferred reply (HH), checked against human raters.

On TL;DR summaries DPO was preferred slightly more often than PPO, without running RL.

8When to choose DPO

  • You have fixed human or AI preference pairs.
  • Compute is limited.
  • The task is chat-style alignment.

When a program can check the answer, prefer GRPO, group relative policy optimization. Its rule rewards are numbers a program computes.

DPO learns from a fixed dataset of pairs and never from replies the current model just wrote, so it needs no sampling loop, a loop that writes fresh replies to learn from.

That is why DPO fits when the pairs already exist.

9When the preferred reply gets less likely too

Likelihood displacement is the name one study gives to the chosen reply becoming less likely during DPO.

DPO-Positive adds a penalty whenever the chosen reply becomes less likely than under the reference.

Filtering out pairs that look too alike, or adding the ordinary SFT loss on the chosen reply, also helps.

Label smoothing treats each label as slightly uncertain, so the loss stops pushing once the margin reaches a set size.

IPO, identity preference optimization, is a variant whose loss aims for a fixed margin instead of an ever larger one.

Plain DPO's loss keeps pushing the margin up without end.
