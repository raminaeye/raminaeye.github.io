1 Why not just score replies by hand

Training a language model to give good replies needs a score for every reply it writes, including replies no person has seen, and nobody can score millions of replies by hand.

The running example is a prompt, "Explain the moon landing to a 6 year old". A model writes several replies to it, such as a simple story or a list of dates, and a person says which reply is better.

One example carries the whole page. The moon-landing pair follows the reward model from its first score to the day the score is pushed too far.

Figure 1 shows the raw material a reward model learns from: one prompt, two replies, and a person’s pick between them. The pick is the label: the story is marked better than the list of dates, so the pair teaches the scorer that this kind of reply should score higher. Take the moon-landing prompt: the person chooses the story over the dates, and that choice becomes one training pair for the reward model.

InstructGPT trained its GPT-3 language model to follow instructions with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), a recipe that trains a model by trial and error against a learned score.

Scores on a rating scale drift between people; comparisons are more consistent.

A preference pair is one prompt, two replies, and a label saying which reply a person preferred.

A reward model is a language model with a one-number output, trained on people's comparisons so the reply they preferred scores higher.

Some papers call it a preference model.

A prompt and a reply go in, and one score comes out.

Figure 2 shows the reward model’s two jobs: scoring a new reply, and learning from a labeled pair. Scoring takes a prompt and a reply in and gives one number out, while the pair below trains the model, pushing the preferred reply’s score above the rejected one’s. Take the moon-landing prompt and the simple story: the model scores the story, and the pair of story over dates teaches it to keep that score above the dates’ score.

2 Learn a score whose gaps predict preferences

The reward model usually starts from a language model already trained on replies people wrote, a step called supervised fine-tuning (SFT); its word-prediction layer is replaced by a layer that outputs one number.

Only the gap between the two scores matters, so the story reply should score above the dates reply by whatever amount the data supports.

3 From score gaps to a training loss

3.1 Scores into win probabilities

The rule in the Bradley-Terry card, which turns the gap between two scores into the chance that one reply wins, is called the Bradley-Terry model.

It needs only the two scores, never a rating scale.

The sigmoid squashes any number into the range 0 to 1, so a score gap becomes a chance (Logistic Regression).

So when the story outscores the list of dates by a wide margin, the story is almost sure to win.

Equation 1. Bradley-Terry. Two scores do not by themselves say which reply a person would pick, so the scorer needs a rule that turns the gap between them into a pick. A person choosing between the simple story and the list of dates for a six year old makes the same call.

3.2 One ranking gives many pairs

The replies to one prompt, ranked best to worst by one person. Every pair from the ranking is a training pair. A batch is a group of training examples handled together. All pairs of one prompt go into one batch element so the model does not overfit to a prompt it saw many times.

Intuition. One ranking is worth many labels: every reply is paired with every reply below it.
The mathematics. Each line joins one pair, winner above loser, so a longer ranking gives many more pairs, each carrying its own winner and loser.
Example. Take the slider at six replies: a short answer and a poem join below the dates, and every new line joins one more pair.

Equation 2. Reward-model loss. One prompt gives a ranking of several replies, and every pair in the ranking should teach the scorer at once, not one pair at a time. A teacher grading a whole ranked list of answers, rather than one answer on its own, faces the same job.
1\(\displaystyle \begin{aligned} \htmlClass{eqt sym-loss}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-loss)}{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{RM}}}}(\psi)&=-\,\htmlClass{eqt sym-avg}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-avg)}{\mathbb{E}_{x}}}\Bigg[\dfrac{1}{\htmlClass{eqt sym-count}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-count)}{\binom{K}{2}}}}\\ &\qquad\sum_{(\htmlClass{eqt sym-y}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-y)}{y_w}},\htmlClass{eqt sym-y}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-y)}{y_l}})\in\htmlClass{eqt sym-y}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-y)}{\mathcal{P}(x)}}} \end{aligned}\)
Intuition. One prompt’s ranking gives many pairs, and the loss averages over prompts and pairs.
The mathematics. This line says \(\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{RM}}}(\psi)\) equals minus the expected value over \(\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{x}\) of the bracket, one over \(\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\binom{K}{2}}\) times the sum over the pairs from \(\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\mathcal{P}(x)}\). \(\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{RM}}}\) of \(\psi\) is the loss, one number scoring how badly the reward model does, as a function of its weights \(\psi\). The leading minus turns a log-chance into a loss, since the log of a chance is never positive. \(\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{\mathbb{E}_{x}}\) is the average over labeled prompts, built as a sum over the possible prompts, each share times its chance, added up. Inside the bracket, the sum adds one term per pair, and the fraction divides by the number of pairs, \(\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\binom{K}{2}}\), so one prompt’s share is the average over its pairs, not their total, where \(\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{K}\) is the number of replies ranked for this prompt. The subscript names the pairs in \(\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\mathcal{P}(x)}\), the set of all pairs from prompt x’s ranking, winner \(\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{y_w}\) first and loser \(\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{y_l}\) second, and each pair’s term sits on the line below.
Example. Take one moon-landing prompt: every pair adds its own term, averaged into one share.
2\(\displaystyle \begin{aligned} \quad\log\htmlClass{eqt sym-sigmoid}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-sigmoid)}{\sigma}}\big(&\htmlClass{eqt sym-score}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-score)}{r_\psi}}(\htmlClass{eqt sym-x}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-x)}{x}},\htmlClass{eqt sym-y}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-y)}{y_w}})\\ &\quad-\htmlClass{eqt sym-score}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-score)}{r_\psi}}(\htmlClass{eqt sym-x}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-x)}{x}},\htmlClass{eqt sym-y}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-y)}{y_l}})\big)\Bigg] \end{aligned}\)
Intuition. Each pair teaches through its log-chance: right orderings cost almost nothing, wrong ones cost a lot.
The mathematics. This line says the term added for one pair is the log of \(\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{\sigma}\) of the score gap. Log \(\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{\sigma}\) is the log of the sigmoid of the score gap, the same gap as in Equation 1, with \(\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{r_\psi}(\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{x}, \htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{y_w})\) the winner’s score for prompt \(\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{x}\) minus \(\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{r_\psi}(\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{x}, \htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{y_l})\) the loser’s, and \(\psi\) its weights. The log of a chance is zero at one and negative below it, so a right ordering adds almost nothing and a wrong ordering adds a large negative number. That is why the loss pushes hardest on wrong orderings. A closing bracket after this term closes the average the first line opened. This is binary cross-entropy: the negative log of the chance of the labeled outcome, with the label always the winner wins. So Equation 2 says this: the loss is the average over prompts of the average over each prompt’s pairs of the negative log-chance the winner wins.
Example. Take the story above the dates: its pair adds almost nothing, while a pair ordered backwards adds a large negative number.
Take one moon-landing prompt with a ranking of replies, the story on top and the dates below it. Every pair from that ranking adds its own log-chance term, the terms are added up and divided by the number of pairs, and the prompts are averaged the same way, so a single pair the scorer orders backwards raises the loss while the pairs it orders right hold it down.

So the reward model learns to score the preferred reply higher on every pair.

Other pages call this loss the Bradley-Terry loss, after the rule in the Bradley-Terry card.

5 A reward model and its loss in PyTorch

A token is a word or a piece of a word; a language model writes its reply one token at a time. (Aligning Language Models with RL)

A reward model is a language model, usually the SFT model, with its word-prediction layer replaced by one number. body is any module that turns token ids into hidden states. Hidden states are the numbers the model keeps for each token as it reads. The loss is minimized.

The code below reads the moon-landing ranking: the story, the joke, the one-line answer and the list of dates.

import itertools, torch, torch.nn as nn, torch.nn.functional as F
class RewardModel(nn.Module):                        # [1] language-model body + one-number head
    def __init__(self, body, hidden):
        super().__init__()
        self.body, self.head = body, nn.Linear(hidden, 1)
    def forward(self, ids, last):                    # score read at the last token
        h = self.body(ids)                           # (batch, length, hidden)
        return self.head(h[torch.arange(len(ids)), last]).squeeze(-1)  # torch.arange(len(ids)) counts the replies so each reads its own last token; .squeeze(-1) drops the extra dimension
def pairs_from_ranking(ranked):                      # [2] all K-choose-2 pairs
    return list(itertools.combinations(ranked, 2))   # (better, worse)
def rm_loss(rm, win_ids, win_last, lose_ids, lose_last):   # [3] the reward model loss card, one prompt
    gap = rm(win_ids, win_last) - rm(lose_ids, lose_last)  # line 2 of the Bradley-Terry card, inside sigma
    return -F.logsigmoid(gap).mean()                 # sum over the prompt's pairs / C(K,2)
  1. The reward model: a language-model body with a one-number head; the score is read at the last token.
  2. Every pair from one ranking, better first, as the Bradley-Terry card needs them.
  3. The reward-model loss card for one prompt: the gap is line 2 of the Bradley-Terry card, inside the sigmoid, and the mean is the sum over the prompt’s pairs divided by their number.

Each call takes all the pairs of one prompt as one batch, so .mean() is the sum over that prompt’s pairs divided by their number, the reward model loss card’s bracket; averaging these losses over prompts gives \(\mathbb{E}_x\).

6 Pushing too hard on a learned score

6.1 The study: a gold score stands in for people

What happens when a language model is trained hard to raise a learned score? Measuring it needs a true quality to compare against, and people cannot score every reply a pushed policy writes. So the large study on pushing a policy hard uses a stand-in: a large reward model, 6B, plays the part of people. It is called the gold reward model, and its score is the true quality for the study.

The reward model a real run pushes on is trained like the one in section 8: a 6B model started from a GPT-3 fine-tuned on public language tasks such as question answering and classification, its scores shifted so the replies people wrote average 0, and its accuracy checked by holding out each group of labelers in turn.

The study trains smaller reward models, 3M to 3B, on labels the gold model wrote. Those smaller models are the proxies, stand-in scorers that copy the gold model’s picks, and the policy is pushed on them, not on the gold model itself.

The policy is a 1.2B language model. It is pushed against each proxy two ways: with PPO, proximal policy optimization, the usual RL update, and with best-of-n, writing several replies and keeping the one the proxy scores highest.

What the study measures is how far the policy’s replies move from where they started, and the gold score of the replies at each distance. Distance is the square root of the KL, which behaves like a distance: KL measures how different two probability distributions are, zero when they match. The main runs use no KL penalty, so nothing holds the policy back from moving far.

6.2 What pushing finds

Pushed a little, the policy writes replies the gold model scores higher too. Pushed further, the proxy score keeps rising while the gold score turns down. The proxy keeps rewarding the move, but the true quality falls. The tool in Proxy score versus true quality shows the two curves: the proxy score climbs, and true quality rises, peaks, then falls.

Bigger proxies hold up longer. The larger the proxy, the further the policy can be pushed before the gold score turns down, because the bigger proxy copies the gold model more closely.

The turn appears under both ways of pushing. PPO and best-of-n show the same rise and fall of the gold score, though the exact curve differs by method. What matters is how far the policy moves, not how it gets there.

A KL penalty does not change the shape, it only slows the move. The KL penalty charges the policy for drifting away from the reference model, a frozen copy of the SFT model, so with a penalty the policy moves less far for the same training. That is stopping early by another name, not a cure: pushed far enough, the gold score still turns down. Stopping early means halting training before the policy moves too far from where it started.

What over-scoring looks like in practice is reward hacking: the policy finds replies that score high without being good. The policy learned what the scorer likes, not what people like. A score that rewards length alone is one such scorer, since longer replies score higher whether or not they are better. New replies to the moon-landing prompt pushed this far would chase the score the same way, and the simple story people wanted can lose to a reply the scorer likes more.

Table 1 lists the reward-model settings InstructGPT used and what changing each one does. The settings trade labeling effort against training quality: more replies per prompt means more pairs per minute of labeling, and more epochs means more passes over the same pairs. Take the replies ranked per prompt: ranking more replies gives more pairs to learn from, but each ranking takes longer to make.

The section 5 code plugs into a normal training loop: the optimizer holds the reward model's weights, the loader hands over one prompt's pairs at a time, and each pass ends with one optimizer step.

opt = torch.optim.Adam(rm.parameters(), lr=9e-6)   # [1] the paper's optimizer and rate
for win_ids, win_last, lose_ids, lose_last in loader:  # [2] one prompt's pairs per batch
    loss = rm_loss(rm, win_ids, win_last, lose_ids, lose_last)
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()    # [3] one optimizer step per batch
  1. Adam at the paper's learning rate, 9e-6, on a cosine schedule down to a tenth by the end of training.
  2. Each batch is one prompt's pairs, scored by the section 5 model and loss.
  3. One optimizer step per batch: clear the gradients, run the loss backwards, move the weights.

InstructGPT ran this loop for one epoch over 33,000 prompts at 64 prompts per batch, about 515 optimizer steps. The 6B weights take 12 GB in half precision, and Adam's two full-precision moments plus the full-precision master copy the paper keeps add about 72 GB more, so the training state is about 84 GB before gradients and activations. To run this without writing the loop, TRL's RewardTrainer trains a reward model from chosen and rejected replies with the same loss.

The reward model is trained once before RL, and again when new comparisons arrive. Hired labelers compared replies to prompts sent to the OpenAI API.

The labelers ranked pairs like the moon-landing pair, one reply against another.

The reward model started from a GPT-3 fine-tuned on public language tasks such as question answering and classification. Its scores are shifted so the replies people wrote average 0, and its accuracy is checked by cross-validation over held-out groups of labelers, with each group held out once.

9 When a learned score is the right reward

A learned score is the right reward whenever the reward is a human opinion no rule can check, such as helpfulness, tone or harmlessness. It is not needed when a program can check the answer.

Where the want is a human opinion, as with the moon-landing story, hand scoring cannot cover every reply, so a learned score is the way this page scores it.

DeepSeek-R1, a model trained to write out its reasoning before it answers, trained on answers a program checked, uses such checks instead of a reward model for its math and code answers.

Opinions such as helpfulness have no such program.
