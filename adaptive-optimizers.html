1 One learning rate cannot fit every direction

Every figure here uses the loss of the series, L = 5x² + 0.2y², started at (−3.5, 5). I call its shape, a long narrow valley, the ravine; it stands for the ill-conditioned loss of a real network, where one direction is far steeper than another. Its numbers are illustrative, computed by script. Plain SGD at α 0.15 takes this first step.

x moves 17.5 times farther, though y has farther to go.
axisgradientSGD step
x (steep)−35+5.25
y (flat)2−0.30
ratio x to y17.517.5
SGD, recap
θ ← θ − α·g
θ is the weights, α the learning rate and g the gradient; the same α multiplies every coordinate's gradient.

The steep axis caps α (SGD, Momentum and Nesterov, section 1.1), so the flat axis crawls. The methods below give each coordinate its own step size.

2 AdaGrad

AdaGrad divides each coordinate's gradient by the size of all its past gradients.

AdaGrad's running sum of squares and divided step
Gt = Gt−1 + gt²
Square today's gradient (the sign drops out) and add it to a running total that never forgets.
θ ← θ − α·gt√Gt + ε
√G is the total size of past gradients, in gradient units. g√G compares today's push with all past pushes, and α turns it into distance. ε keeps the divide away from zero.

At step 1, √G = |g|, so every coordinate steps exactly α. After that the sum only grows, so the steps only shrink. GloVe word vectors were trained with AdaGrad at an initial rate of 0.05 (Pennington et al. 2014): a rare word's row gets few gradients, so its steps stay large.

3 RMSProp

RMSProp replaces AdaGrad's forever-sum with an average that forgets. With a decay rate β of 0.99, the share of the old average kept each step, a value from k steps ago gets this weight.

Recent values count most; the last 100 steps hold 63% of the weight.

An exponential moving average
at = β·at−1 + (1 − β)·ut
Keep the fraction β of the old average and mix in 1 − β of today's value u.
at = (1 − β)·ut + (1 − β)β·ut−1 + …
Written out, the value from k steps ago is weighted (1 − β)βk. The weights add up to 1, and the window is about 11 − β steps.

This one average also keeps batch norm's running statistics. RMSProp applies it to g² and calls the average E. At its first step, with β2 0.99 and α 0.5:

Both axes jump 5, ten times α, from (−3.5, 5) to (1.5, 0).

RMSProp's average of squared gradients and divided step
Et = β2·Et−1 + (1 − β2)·gt²
The moving average above with u = g², keeping 99% of the old average and mixing in 1% of today's squared gradient.
θ ← θ − α·gt√Et + ε
√E is the typical recent gradient size; g√E is today's push in units of recent pushes.
√E1 = √1 − β2·|g1|
The average starts at zero, so after one step it holds only 1 − β2 of g². Its root is 10 times too small, so the first step is 10 times too big.

RMSProp's memory is about 100 steps at β2 0.99 instead of forever. The memory matters when gradient sizes change, as when a balancer cuts one task's weight (Gradient Balancing Inside the Training Loop, section 7.1).

4 Adam

Adam keeps momentum's average direction, RMSProp's average size, and a fix for the zero start of both.

4.1 Two running averages

Adam averages the gradient into m and its square into v, both from zero; this v is not momentum's velocity. At step 1 on the ravine:

m keeps 10% of the gradient, v keeps 0.1% of its square.
axisg1m1v1
x−35−3.51.225
y20.20.004
Adam's two moving averages
mt = β1·mt−1 + (1 − β1)·gt
The first moment m is the moving average of the gradient, the recent average direction.
vt = β2·vt−1 + (1 − β2)·gt²
The second moment v is the moving average of the squared gradient, the recent average size. This is RMSProp's E renamed; it is not momentum's velocity.

m is momentum's velocity scaled by (1 − β1): an average instead of a sum (SGD, Momentum and Nesterov, section 2).

4.2 The betas set how far back each average looks

Figure 3. β2 0.999 looks back about 1,000 steps.

Both must stay below 1; at 1 an average never updates and its correction divides by zero.

4.3 Correcting the zero start

Both averages start at zero, so for a steady gradient the uncorrected step grows.

Uncorrected, the step grows from 3.16α to 5.80α in five steps; corrected, it is 1.00α on every row.
t1 − β1t1 − β2tuncorrected step ÷ α
10.1000.00103.16
20.1900.00204.25
30.2710.00304.95
40.3440.00405.44
50.4100.00505.80

Dividing each average by the total weight it has received so far makes it a true average from step 1.

Bias correction
m̂t = mt1 − β1t
1 − β1t is the weight m has collected: 0.1 at t 1, 0.19 at t 2, close to 1 after about 10 steps.
v̂t = vt1 − β2t
1 − β2t grows only 0.001 per step, 0.632 at t 1,000, so v needs its fix for about 1,000 steps.

m's weight grows about 0.1 per step while v's grows only 0.001, so the raw ratio of m to √v keeps growing; correcting m alone would make step 1 31.6α, which is why both are corrected. Every Adam run takes these corrected first steps, the DQN agent's at 5e-4 too (Deep Q-Networks, section 4).

4.4 One full Adam step

On the first step, x's gradient is −35 and y's is 2. SGD would move x 17.5 times farther than y. Adam moves both by 0.5.

Corrected, both ratios are ±1, so both steps are α.
axisgm̂/√v̂step at α 0.5
x−35−1.00+0.50
y2+1.00−0.50

Adam steps by the average direction divided by the average size, times α.

The Adam step
θ ← θ − α·m̂t√v̂t + ε
m̂ is which way this coordinate has been pushed lately; √v̂ is how hard. Their ratio is close to ±1 when the gradient is steady and smaller when it keeps changing. α turns the ratio into distance; ε matters only when gradients are as small as ε.

So each coordinate steps about α, whatever the size of its gradient; the divide is element by element (Vectors and Matrices, section 6.3). This is RMSProp's step with m̂ in place of g, both averages corrected. Late in training a coordinate that gets its first gradient steps 3.16α, like uncorrected step 1 above.

Because m̂/√v̂ is near ±1, α is close to the step length.

This step, line by line
m = β1·m + (1 − β1)·g
v = β2·v + (1 − β2)·g²
m̂ = m1 − β1t
v̂ = v1 − β2t
θ ← θ − α·m̂√v̂ + ε

The code below is the same five steps.

import numpy as np

def adam_step(theta, g, m, v, t, lr=1e-3, b1=0.9, b2=0.999, eps=1e-8):
    m = b1 * m + (1 - b1) * g
    v = b2 * v + (1 - b2) * g * g
    m_hat = m / (1 - b1 ** t)
    v_hat = v / (1 - b2 ** t)
    theta = theta - lr * m_hat / (np.sqrt(v_hat) + eps)
    return theta, m, v

theta = np.array([-3.5, 5.0])
m = np.zeros(2)
v = np.zeros(2)
for t in range(1, 6):
    g = np.array([10 * theta[0], 0.4 * theta[1]])
    theta, m, v = adam_step(theta, g, m, v, t, lr=0.5)
  1. 1The two moving averages, m and v.
  2. 2Bias correction of both.
  3. 3The step, element by element; returns the new averages too.
  4. 4The ravine from (−3.5, 5); the first step is (0.5, −0.5), as in the figure.

Gradient balancing meets this divide in Adam's per-coordinate scaling and the tilt of Adam's step (Gradient Balancing Inside the Training Loop, sections 7 and 7.2).

4.5 How Adam ignores the gradient's scale, and where it stops

Switch a loss from the mean over a batch of 256 examples to the sum, and every gradient is 256 times larger (Gradient Balancing Inside the Training Loop, section 5). Multiply every gradient by c:

SGD would need α 0.15/256 to behave as before; Adam needs no change.
cSGD step 1 on xAdam step 1 on x

Scale invariance
c·m̂√c²·v̂ = m̂√v̂ for c > 0
Scaling every gradient by c scales m̂ by c and √v̂ by c, so the ratio and the step do not change. It works per coordinate, so it also undoes the 17.5 to 1 split between the axes.
Every gradient times c

Figure 6. SGD's step scales with c; Adam's does not.

Invariance has two limits. The first is ε. For a steady gradient of size |g|:

Only a coordinate with near-zero gradients, such as a rarely seen token's row, feels ε.
|g|step ÷ α
1e−70.91
1e−80.50
1e−100.01
Where ε takes over
step ÷ α = |g||g| + ε
Near |g| = ε the step is half of α; well below ε, Adam acts like SGD with rate αε.

The second limit is time. Invariance holds for a steady scale, not for one that moves within v's window. After 2,000 steps at gradient 1 the gradient drops to 0.1.

For hundreds of steps Adam steps about 10 times smaller than α, so its step does shrink when the gradient shrinks, just later. LLaMA trains with β2 0.95, a window of about 20 steps; with it the same drop recovers to 0.9α 118 steps after the drop.

4.6 What Adam's momentum carries after the gradient changes

Coasting
mt+k = β1k·mt
With no new gradient (g = 0 from step t on), m shrinks by 0.9 per step.
vt+k = β2k·vt
v shrinks by 0.999 per step, so √v by only 0.9995, and the step shrinks by about 0.9 per step, like momentum with μ 0.9.

Momentum at α 0.01 builds its step up to 10α, about 0.1. Adam at α 0.1 keeps its step near α, also 0.1. Both then coast about 9 last steps. After a turn, √v̂ is still large on the old axis and small on the new one, so the divide boosts the new direction: Adam's step is within 45° of it at once, while m takes 7 steps. That is the case of a multitask run where one task's gradient suddenly dominates a shared weight (Gradient Balancing Inside the Training Loop, section 6.1).

5 Nadam

Builds on Nesterov momentum (SGD, Momentum and Nesterov, section 3)

Nadam puts the Nesterov preview into Adam: it uses the momentum the next step will use.

Nadam (Dozat 2016, constant β1)
m̄t = (1 − β1)·gt1 − β1t + β1·mt1 − β1t+1
The first term is today's gradient with its own correction; the second is the corrected momentum one step ahead. At step 1 this is g + 0.474g, so the step is 1.47α.
θ ← θ − α·m̄t√v̂t + ε
Adam's step with m̄ in place of m̂.

PyTorch ships it as torch.optim.NAdam, which multiplies β1 by a warm-up factor, so its momentum weight μ starts near 0.45 and rises toward β1 over thousands of steps.

7 Adaptive optimizers in PyTorch

Each runs once per training step. The DQN page's Adam call is this same update.

opt = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=1e-3, betas=(0.9, 0.999), eps=1e-8)
loss.backward()
opt.step()
state = opt.state[next(model.parameters())]
state["exp_avg"]      # m, the first moment
state["exp_avg_sq"]   # v, the second moment
state["step"]         # t, used by the bias correction
