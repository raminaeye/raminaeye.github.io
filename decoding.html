Softmax, Temperature and Decoding

On this page

A language model writes a reply one word at a time. At every step a decoder network like the one in The Transformer Block scores every word, or word piece called a token, in its vocabulary. Decoding is the rule that turns those scores into the one word written next. Softmax first turns the scores into probabilities. The rule then either chooses the likeliest words, which gives the same reply every time, or draws a word at random, which is why the same question can get two different answers. It runs once for every generated token, and only when the model writes text. Training on fixed text never decodes, because the loss reads the true next token from the data.

Every example uses one toy step. After the cat sat on the, a model scores ten candidate words.

1 Softmax turns scores into probabilities

Softmax is the first step of every decoding method. It takes the scores for all the words and outputs one probability per word. The raw scores are logits, any real numbers, and they do not add to anything. Softmax makes them a probability distribution, positive numbers that add to 1, while keeping their order.

Each word’s chance is its exponentiated logit as a share of the total over all words.

pi = exp(zi)Σj exp(zj)
zi is the logit of word i. pi is its probability. The top, e raised to the logit, is always positive, so even a negative logit gets a share. It also grows fast, and each extra 1 of logit multiplies the term by about 2.7. Σj adds the same term over every word j in the vocabulary. Dividing by that total makes the pi add to 1. Every word is divided by the same total, so the order of the logits is kept. Adding one number to every logit multiplies top and bottom by the same factor, so it changes nothing.

A logit like 1000 makes the exponential overflow to infinity, and infinity over infinity gives NaN, not a number. Subtracting the largest logit first divides the top and bottom by the same number, so every pi stays the same (Goodfellow et al. 2016, section 4.1), and the biggest term becomes 1.

The same softmax picks the weights in Attention and feeds the loss in Cross-Entropy Loss.

2 Greedy and beam search look for the likeliest reply

Greedy decoding and beam search both look for the reply the model finds likeliest, with no randomness, so they give the same reply every time. Greedy looks one word ahead, and beam search compares whole replies. Greedy decoding writes the likeliest word and moves on, so a likely first word can force unlikely words after it. Beam search keeps the best few partial replies at every step, its beam width, and keeps the best reply at the end.

Greedy takes the top word at each step. Beam search ranks whole replies by their summed log probability.

xt = argmaxi pi
Greedy writes the word with the largest probability. t counts the steps. xt is the word written at step t. argmaxi returns the word i whose pi is largest, not the largest value itself. Later steps play no part in the choice.
s = Σt log P(xt | x<t)
A reply’s score adds up the log probability of each of its words. s is that score. x<t stands for the words before step t. P(xt | x<t) is the chance of xt given those words. A sum of logs is the log of the product, so the highest s marks the reply whose whole chance is largest. Each chance is at most 1, so each log is at most 0 and every word lowers s. Logs also keep long products from rounding to 0. Greedy maximizes one term at a time. Beam search keeps several partial replies and compares their full sums.
The beam width is num_beams in Hugging Face, default 1, which is greedy. A wider beam compares more replies at that many times the cost, and a narrower one compares fewer. The nucleus sampling paper’s width 16 still loops (Figure 14).

Beam search ranks partial replies by summed log probability, which ranks them by the chance of the whole reply.

Every word adds a negative log, so the plain sum s favors short replies. The replies in Figure 3 all have two words, so it never shows this bias. To remove it, beam search divides the score by a power of the length.

The corrected score is the summed log probability divided by the reply length raised to a power.

snorm = sLα
s is the summed log probability, and L is the reply length in words. α (alpha) sets how hard to correct. Dividing a negative score by a larger number pulls it toward 0, so long replies lose less. With α = 0 the bottom is 1 and nothing changes. With α = 1 the score is the average log probability per word. Google’s translation system found 0.6 to 0.7 usually worked best for this form.
α is length_penalty in Hugging Face, default 1.0, so its beam search divides by the generated length unless told otherwise. A larger α favors longer replies, and a smaller one favors shorter replies.

Speech recognition uses the same two methods over alignments in CTC Loss. Beam search works well when the input pins down the output, as in translation, summarization and writing a sentence from a table of data.

3 The likeliest text loops

For open-ended writing, the likeliest reply turns out to loop. Holtzman et al. (2020), the paper that introduced nucleus sampling, gave GPT-2 Large, a 774-million-parameter language model from 2019, the opening of a story and let beam search continue it.

Repetition feeds itself, because each repeat makes the next one likelier.

A repetition penalty from CTRL (Keskar et al. 2019), a 1.63-billion-parameter Salesforce language model, makes a repeat less likely by lowering the logit of every word already written.

A used word’s logit is divided by the penalty before the softmax.

zi ← ziθ
← means the logit is replaced by the new value. θ (theta) is the penalty, a number above 1. CTRL used about 1.2 with greedy decoding. Dividing a positive logit by θ moves it toward 0, which lowers that word’s share after the softmax, and a larger θ lowers it more. Unused words keep their logits, so they gain the share the used word loses. Dividing a negative logit would raise it. Hugging Face Transformers, a widely used Python library for language models, multiplies a negative logit by θ instead, so a used word always loses ground.
θ is repetition_penalty in Hugging Face, default 1.0, which is no penalty. A larger θ pushes harder away from used words, a smaller one pushes less, and a value below 1 would reward repeats.

The penalty lowers every used word alike, so it also lowers words that should repeat, such as a name or the.

4 Sampling draws words at random

Sampling picks the next word at random instead of taking the likeliest one. Each word is drawn in proportion to its probability, so nothing is maximized and the output varies from run to run. Pure sampling draws each word with chance pi, straight from the softmax. Two runs give two replies. The catch is the tail, the many words that each have a small chance. In the toy, moon, piano, Tuesday and of make no sense after the cat sat on the. These unlikely words hold about 4% together.

A small risk per word becomes a near certainty over a long reply, and one unlikely word pulls the words after it off topic.

5 Temperature sharpens or flattens the distribution

Temperature is one number that sets how random sampling is. It outputs a reshaped distribution with the same word order, sharper toward the top word or flatter toward the tail. It does this by dividing every logit by that number before the softmax.

Temperature rescales every logit, and the softmax turns the result into new probabilities.

pi = exp(ziτ)Σj exp(zjτ)
Every logit is divided by one number before the softmax. τ (tau), the temperature, is that positive number. With τ = 1 it is the plain softmax from Section 1. The ratio of two words’ chances is e raised to the gap between their logits, and dividing by τ divides that gap too. Below 1 the gaps grow, so the top word’s lead grows and the distribution sharpens. Above 1 the gaps shrink and the distribution flattens toward the tail. Every logit is divided by the same positive number, so the order of the words never changes.
τ → 0 ⇒ pmax → 1
As τ shrinks toward 0, the likeliest word takes all the probability. → means approaches and ⇒ means so. pmax is the probability of the likeliest word. Divided by a tiny τ, every gap below the top logit becomes huge, so the top word’s term dwarfs the rest. Sampling then always draws the top word, which is greedy. As τ grows large every gap shrinks to 0, and every word’s share approaches the same value, one over the number of words.
τ is temperature in Hugging Face, default 1.0, the plain softmax.

Neither a low nor a high τ solves the tail problem. A low τ drifts back toward greedy, and a high one gives the tail more probability. The same temperature softens a teacher’s probabilities in Knowledge Distillation, which writes it T, and sets how sharply similarities become probabilities in InfoNCE and CLIP. The Transformer Block has its own temperature slider.

6 Top-k, top-p and min-p drop the unlikely words

A cut, also called truncation, decides which words may be drawn at all. It keeps the likeliest words, sets the chance of the rest to 0, rescales the kept ones, and sampling then draws only from them. The simplest cut keeps a fixed number of words, and the cuts after it fix its weak spot.

6.1 Top-k keeps a fixed count

Top-k keeps a fixed number of the likeliest words at every step.

Top-k keeps a word only if it is among the k likeliest.

keep i if rank(i) ≤ k
rank(i) is the word’s place when the words are sorted from likeliest down, so the top word has rank 1. k is the number of words kept, such as 40. The count is the same at every step, whatever the distribution looks like. A larger k lets more of the tail in, and k = 1 is greedy.
k is top_k in Hugging Face, default 50. A smaller k drops good words at flat steps, and k = V, the vocabulary size, keeps every word, which is pure sampling. The nucleus sampling paper tests 40 and 640, and 10 is used for stories.

One fixed k cannot fit every step, because some steps have many good words and others have one. The nucleus sampling paper says it with probability mass, meaning total probability, and with tokens, the word pieces a model writes: “Flat distributions lead to many moderately probable tokens, while peaked distributions concentrate most probability mass into just a few tokens.”

6.2 Top-p keeps a fixed share

Top-p, also called nucleus sampling, fixes this by keeping a share of the total probability instead of a count.

Top-p keeps the fewest top words whose chances add up to a set share of the total probability.

Σkept pi ≥ ptop
ptop is the share of the total probability to keep, such as 0.9. Walk down the sorted words adding their probabilities and stop at the first word where the total reaches it. When the distribution is peaked one or two words reach the share, and when it is flat many words are needed. A larger ptop keeps more of the tail. The kept words are the nucleus.
ptop is top_p in Hugging Face, default 1.0, which keeps every word. A smaller ptop keeps a smaller nucleus and safer text, and near 0 only the top word stays, which is greedy. The nucleus sampling paper uses 0.95.

Top-p keeps many words when the distribution is flat and few when it is peaked. The captioner in From BLIP to the Q-Former uses top-p at 90%.

6.3 Min-p keeps words above a moving bar

Min-p (Nguyen et al. 2025) adapts in a different way. It keeps the words above a threshold, the bar, that moves with the top word’s probability.

Min-p keeps every word whose chance is at least a set fraction of the top word’s.

keep i if pi ≥ pbase · pmax
pbase is that fraction, such as 0.1. The bar is pbase times the top word’s pmax, so it rises when the model is confident and falls when the distribution is flat. A larger pbase cuts more of the tail.
pbase is min_p in Hugging Face, unset by default. A smaller pbase lowers the bar and keeps more words. 0 keeps every word, which is pure sampling, and 1 keeps only words tied with the top, which is greedy. The docs suggest 0.01 to 0.2. The min-p paper suggests 0.05 to 0.1, and 0.5 to 0.7 for very high τ.

All three cuts then renormalize, rescaling the kept words so they add to 1 again.

After any cut, each kept word’s chance is rescaled so the kept words add to 1.

p′i = piΣkept pj
p′ (p prime) is the new distribution. Σkept adds over the kept words only. That total is below 1 after a cut, so dividing by it scales the kept words up until they add to 1 again. Every kept word is divided by the same number, so the kept words keep their ratios. A cut word gets a new probability of 0 and is never drawn.

7 Each job needs its own decoding method

The right decoding method depends on the job. A task with one right answer wants the likeliest reply, and open-ended writing wants text that is varied but stays on topic. The nucleus sampling paper measured both sides. It wrote 5,000 passages of up to 200 tokens with each method, each from a short opening of web text the model never trained on. Perplexity per token is how surprised the model is by the text, where higher means less predictable. Loops is the share of passages that end stuck repeating a phrase of two or more tokens at least three times.

  • Greedy suits short answers with one right reply, such as the capital of Australia. Its risk is looping on long text.
  • Beam search suits output that the input pins down, such as translation. Its risk is bland, looping open-ended text, and short replies without the length correction.
  • Pure sampling is rarely used alone. Its risk is the tail, which pulls long text off topic.
  • Top-k is a simple cut, such as k = 10 for stories (Fan et al. 2018). Its risk is that one k cannot fit both flat and peaked steps.
  • Top-p suits open-ended writing, with ptop from 0.9 to 0.95. Its risk is a high τ applied first, which lets unlikely words back in.
  • Min-p was proposed for sampling at a high τ, with pbase from 0.05 to 0.1 suggested (Figure 11). Its risk is a nearly flat distribution with a low pbase, where the bar sinks with the top word and unlikely words pass.
  • A repetition penalty can be added to any method against loops. Its risk is lowering words that should repeat.

8 How decoding runs in practice

8.1 The generate loop

In a real system the decoding rule sits inside a generate loop. Each pass runs the model on the text so far, takes the logits at the last position, applies the penalty, temperature and cut, picks one token, appends it and repeats until an end-of-text token or a length limit. A key-value cache stores the attention keys and values of every past token, so each pass computes only the new token instead of the whole text. The rules themselves are quick passes over one row of V logits, where V is the vocabulary size; the min-p authors call that filtering “negligible overhead”.

8.2 Code for each method

The toy sampling step in NumPy, from the ten logits to one drawn word.

import numpy as np

z = np.array([2.0, 1.2, 0.8, 0.5, 0.1, -0.3, -1.0, -1.8, -2.2, -2.6])
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

def softmax(z, tau=1.0):
    e = np.exp((z - z.max()) / tau)  # max first: no overflow
    return e / e.sum()

def top_p(p, p_top=0.9):
    order = np.argsort(p)[::-1]  # likeliest first
    n_keep = np.searchsorted(np.cumsum(p[order]), p_top) + 1
    p_prime = np.zeros_like(p)
    p_prime[order[:n_keep]] = p[order[:n_keep]]
    return p_prime / p_prime.sum()

p = softmax(z, tau=1.0)
p_prime = top_p(p)
word_id = rng.choice(len(p), p=p_prime)
print(p[:3].round(2), np.count_nonzero(p_prime))  # [0.43 0.19 0.13] 5

Greedy and beam search on the tree of Figure 3, where width 1 is greedy.

from math import log

TREE = {(): {"sat": 0.5, "is": 0.4, "ran": 0.1},
        ("sat",): {"on": 0.4, "down": 0.3, "there": 0.3},
        ("is",): {"sleeping": 0.9, "here": 0.05, "fat": 0.05},
        ("ran",): {"away": 0.8, "off": 0.2}}

def beam_search(width, depth):
    beams = [(0.0, ())]  # (s, prefix)
    for _ in range(depth):
        pool = []
        for s, prefix in beams:
            for word, P in TREE[prefix].items():
                pool.append((s + log(P), prefix + (word,)))
        beams = sorted(pool, reverse=True)[:width]
    return beams[0]

s, words = beam_search(width=1, depth=2)
print(round(s, 2), words)  # -1.61 ('sat', 'on')
s, words = beam_search(width=2, depth=2)
print(round(s, 2), words)  # -1.02 ('is', 'sleeping')
  1. 1The reply tree of Figure 3, each prefix mapped to its next-word chances.
  2. 2Grows every kept reply by every next word and adds log P to s, line 2 of the beam eq-card.
  3. 3Keeps the best replies up to the width, steps 3 to 5 of Figure 3.

The same sampling step in PyTorch on a real model, GPT-2, inside a generate loop that runs under torch.no_grad because nothing is trained.

import torch
from transformers import AutoModelForCausalLM, AutoTokenizer

tok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("gpt2")
model = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained("gpt2").eval()

def next_token(z, tau=0.7, k=50):
    z = z / tau  # temperature first
    kth = torch.topk(z, k).values[:, -1:]
    z = z.masked_fill(z < kth, float("-inf"))  # top-k cut
    p = torch.softmax(z, dim=-1)  # renormalized over the kept k
    return torch.multinomial(p, num_samples=1)

ids = tok("The cat sat on the", return_tensors="pt").input_ids
with torch.no_grad():
    for _ in range(40):
        z = model(ids).logits[:, -1, :]  # (1, V)
        ids = torch.cat([ids, next_token(z)], dim=-1)
print(tok.decode(ids[0]))
  1. 1Loads GPT-2 and its tokenizer.
  2. 2Divides the logits by τ, the temperature eq-card.
  3. 3Sets every logit below the k-th largest to minus infinity, the top-k eq-card.
  4. 4Softmax over the kept words, which renormalizes them, then one draw.
  5. 5The generate loop of Section 8.1, one token per pass.

Hugging Face’s model.generate runs that loop with a key-value cache, and each call below is one method.

from functools import partial
from transformers import AutoModelForCausalLM, AutoTokenizer

tok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("gpt2")
model = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained("gpt2")
inputs = tok("The cat sat on the", return_tensors="pt")
gen = partial(model.generate, **inputs, max_new_tokens=40)

greedy = gen()
beam = gen(num_beams=4)
sampled = gen(do_sample=True, temperature=0.7, top_k=0, top_p=0.9)
no_loops = gen(repetition_penalty=1.2)  # greedy, CTRL penalty
print(tok.decode(sampled[0], skip_special_tokens=True))

8.4 Gotchas

  • With no settings, generate is greedy and stops after 20 new tokens. temperature, top_k and top_p do nothing unless do_sample is on; the code adds them only when sampling.
  • Sampling alone already keeps only 50 tokens, because top_k defaults to 50. Pass top_k=0 for top-p by itself.
  • The penalty counts the prompt’s tokens too, so words from the question get pushed down.
  • Libraries order the steps differently (Figure 12), so the same settings give different probabilities in Hugging Face and in llama.cpp.
  • The PyTorch loop in Section 8.2 re-reads the whole text at every pass, while model.generate keeps the key-value cache and feeds only the new token.
  • Beam search runs B replies through every model pass, so its work and cache grow B times.

The Hugging Face Generation docs list every argument, and the Generation strategies guide shows each method in use.

8.5 Decoding during training

RL fine-tuning is the main place where training decodes. In Aligning Language Models with RL the model samples its own replies during training, so decoding shapes which replies get rewarded. Can the model’s hidden layers tell when it is about to pick an unlikely word? Activation Probes reads those layers directly.
