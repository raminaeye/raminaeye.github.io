A speech model rarely reads raw samples or a full spectrogram. Feature extraction turns each short STFT frame, one every 10 ms or so, into a few dozen numbers that keep the smooth outline of the spectrum (its envelope), which tells sounds apart, and drop most of the pitch. The usual chain is a power spectrum, a mel filterbank that bends frequency the way ears hear, and a log, which gives the log-mel features; for MFCCs (mel-frequency cepstral coefficients) a cosine transform then orders the result from broad shape to fine detail.

1 The mel scale

Ears hear pitch in roughly equal steps of ratio, not of hertz; a step of a few hundred hertz sounds like a big jump low down and like almost none high up. The mel scale bends frequency so that equal mel steps sound like equal pitch steps.

Frequency in hertz to mels (the HTK formula).
\(\displaystyle m = {2595 \log_{10}\!\left(1 + \frac{f}{700}\right)}\)
\(f\) in Hz in, \(m\) in mels out; 2595 and 700 are the formula’s fixed constants (O’Shaughnessy 1987).
Well below 700 Hz it is nearly a straight line, well above it it grows like a log, so high frequencies get squeezed together.
The scale is pinned so a 1 kHz tone is 1000 mels, the reference point of Stevens et al. (1937). Section 2 lays its triangles evenly on this scale.

Slaney’s version of the scale, from his Auditory Toolbox and the default in librosa unless you pass htk=True, is a straight line below 1 kHz. Its units differ from HTK’s, so Figure 2 scales it up to meet the HTK curve there and compares the shapes.

2 The mel filterbank

Lay triangles evenly along the mel axis, so they come out narrow at low frequencies and wide at high ones, and let each one sum the power under it.

The energy in mel band \(\ell\).
\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{E_{\ell}} = {\sum_{k} \htmlStyle{color:#a3540f}{F_{\ell}[k]} \cdot \htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{\lvert X[k]\rvert^{2}}}\)
\(\lvert X[k]\rvert^{2}\) is the power in FFT bin \(k\) of one frame (Fourier Transform and the FFT).
\(F_{\ell}[k]\) is triangle \(\ell\)’s weight on bin \(k\), zero outside the triangle.
\(E_{\ell}\) is band \(\ell\)’s energy, \(\ell\) running over the \(N_{\text{mel}}\) bands (40 in the tools).

3 Why the log

Builds on the decibel (Audio & Signals Basics, section 1.1)

Loudness is heard roughly on a log scale, and a louder voice or another microphone multiplies the spectrum, as any filter does (How FIR and IIR Filters Shape Magnitude and Phase).

A gain on the spectrum becomes an offset.
\(\displaystyle \log\bigl(g\,\htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{E_{\ell}}\bigr) = {\log g + \htmlStyle{color:#117a65}{\log E_{\ell}}}\)
\(g\) is a gain on the whole spectrum (louder voice, different microphone). After the log it adds the same constant to every band. It builds on the band energies of Section 2; the DCT of Section 4 works on these logs, and Section 5 removes the offset.

4 Why the DCT

Builds on principal component analysis (Principal Component Analysis, section 2)

Neighboring mel bands rise and fall together. The DCT (discrete cosine transform) rewrites the 40 log energies as amounts of cosines across the bands, ordered from slow to fast change; like principal component analysis (PCA) it packs correlated numbers into a few that matter most. Applied to the log spectrum it gives a spectrum of a spectrum, hence the name cepstrum (Fourier Transform and the FFT). Davis and Mermelstein (1980) found these coefficients the best of the representations they compared for word recognition.

The cepstrum splits a spectrum into its slow shape, the envelope, and its fine detail; that split is also one way to remove a room’s echo (Anushiravani 2016; Reverberation and Dereverberation, section 8).

Forty bands at one level give only the first coefficient, \(c_{0}\), the overall level; a straight tilt across them puts almost everything else into the second, \(c_{1}\), one slow tilt.

MFCC number \(q\) of one frame.
\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#c0392b}{c_{q}} = {\sum_{\ell} \htmlStyle{color:#117a65}{\log E_{\ell}} \cdot b_{q}[\ell]}\)
\(c_{q}\) is coefficient \(q\), how much the log band energies look like cosine number \(q\) across the bands. \(q = 0\) is the overall level, small \(q\) slow changes across frequency (the envelope), large \(q\) fast ones (the pitch ripple).
\(\displaystyle b_{q}[\ell] = {\cos\!\left( \frac{\pi q \left( \ell + \tfrac{1}{2} \right)}{N_{\text{mel}}} \right)}\)
Cosine number \(q\) sampled at band \(\ell\). librosa uses this DCT-II with orthonormal scaling (norm='ortho') on dB values, which only rescales each \(c_{q}\). It builds on the log energies of Section 3, and Section 5 tracks how these coefficients change.

The test sound is synthetic: a voice’s harmonics under a vowel-like envelope over a faint breath-noise floor. The lowest bands are narrower than the gap between harmonics, so the harmonics show as ripple there. In Figure 6 a few coefficients rebuild only the envelope; many bring the pitch ripple back.

5 Deltas and normalization

One frame is a snapshot. Deltas add how each coefficient is changing, and cepstral mean normalization removes what never changes. A coefficient that rises steadily, as at a vowel onset, has a delta equal to its rise per frame.

Deltas and mean normalization for one coefficient at frame \(t\).
\(\displaystyle \Delta c_{t} = {\frac{\sum i\,(c_{t+i} - c_{t-i})}{60}}\)
For one coefficient, \(c_{t}\) at frame \(t\), the slope of a straight line fitted to the 9 frames \(t - 4\) to \(t + 4\); \(i\) runs from 1 to 4, and 60 is twice the sum of the squared offsets, which makes the result a slope per frame. librosa computes the same slope with a Savitzky-Golay filter, a sliding least-squares polynomial fit, of width 9.
\(\displaystyle \tilde{c}_{t} = {c_{t} - \operatorname{mean}_{t} c_{t}}\)
Subtracting each coefficient’s average over the clip removes a fixed microphone color, the constant \(\log g\) from Section 3.

6 Other features

Simpler numbers each pick out one property of a frame.

Six simpler features of one frame of \(N\) samples \(x[n]\), whose spectrum \(X[k]\) has bin \(k\) at frequency \(f_{k}\).
\(\displaystyle \text{RMS} = {\sqrt{\frac{1}{N} \sum_{n} x[n]^{2}}}\)
The root mean square of the samples, a measure of loudness, used for voice activity and levels.
\(\displaystyle \text{ZCR} = {\operatorname{mean} 1\bigl[x[n]\, x[n-1] < 0\bigr]}\)
The zero-crossing rate, the share of neighboring sample pairs that change sign; \(x[n]\) is sample \(n\), \(1[\cdot]\) is 1 when the condition holds, else 0, and the mean runs over the \(N - 1\) neighbor pairs. It is high for hiss like “s”, so it separates voiced from unvoiced sounds.
\(\displaystyle \text{centroid} = {\frac{\sum_{k} f_{k} \lvert X[k]\rvert}{\sum_{k} \lvert X[k]\rvert}}\)
The center of mass of the magnitude spectrum, which reads as brightness.
\(\displaystyle \sum_{k\le r} \lvert X[k]\rvert = {0.85 \sum \lvert X[k]\rvert}\)
The roll-off is \(f_{r}\), the frequency of bin \(r\), below which 85% of the magnitude sits (librosa’s default), high for bright sounds and low for dull ones.
\(\displaystyle \text{flatness} = {\frac{e^{\operatorname{mean} \log P}}{\operatorname{mean} P}}\)
The geometric mean (\(e\) to the mean log) over the arithmetic mean of the power \(P = \lvert X[k]\rvert^{2}\), both means taken over the bins. It is near 0 for a tone and high for noise, and comes near 1 only when the noise spectrum is averaged over many frames (Power Spectral Density).
\(\displaystyle \text{chroma}_{p} = {\sum_{k\to p} \lvert X[k]\rvert^{2}}\)
The energy folded into the 12 pitch classes \(p\), C to B, adding every bin whose frequency rounds to that note in any octave; used for key and chords.

One librosa call per feature on a short clip of noise; each returns shape (1, frames), except chroma’s 12 rows.

import numpy as np
import librosa

SR = 22050  # librosa's default sample rate
y = np.random.default_rng(0).standard_normal(SR)
feature = librosa.feature
rms = feature.rms(y=y)
zcr = feature.zero_crossing_rate(y)
centroid = feature.spectral_centroid(y=y, sr=SR)
rolloff = feature.spectral_rolloff(y=y, sr=SR)  # default roll-off share
flatness = feature.spectral_flatness(y=y)
chroma = feature.chroma_stft(y=y, sr=SR)     # (pitch classes, frames)

7 Which feature when

Builds on Gaussian mixture models (Gaussian Mixture Models, section 2)

TaskUsual input
Speech recognition with a networkLog-mel, stopping after the log so the network learns the rest
Older speech recognitionMFCC with deltas
Speaker identityMFCC with mean normalization; a Gaussian mixture can model the cloud of MFCC frames

The Conformer (Gulati et al. 2020, Conformer) reads log-mel frames from 25 ms windows every 10 ms, trained with SpecAugment, which blanks out random blocks of bands and of time steps. The CTC speech experiments of Graves et al. (2006, CTC Loss) used MFCCs plus log energy and their first derivatives, a few dozen numbers per frame.

MFCCs with deltas and mean normalization in librosa, on white noise.

import numpy as np
import librosa

SR, N_FFT, HOP, N_MELS, N_MFCC = 22050, 2048, 512, 40, 13  # librosa defaults, with the tools' band and coefficient counts
y = np.random.default_rng(0).standard_normal(SR)
S = np.abs(librosa.stft(y, n_fft=N_FFT, hop_length=HOP))**2
fb = librosa.filters.mel(sr=SR, n_fft=N_FFT, n_mels=N_MELS, htk=True)
mel = fb @ S
log_mel = librosa.power_to_db(mel)
mfcc = librosa.feature.mfcc(S=log_mel, n_mfcc=N_MFCC)
delta = librosa.feature.delta(mfcc)
mfcc_cmn = mfcc - mfcc.mean(axis=1, keepdims=True)

8.1 Gotchas

  • librosa.feature.mfcc takes keyword arguments only, so mfcc(y, sr) fails.
  • Toolkits differ in window, mel normalization and liftering (a reweighting of the coefficients), so their MFCCs do not match.
