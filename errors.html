Understanding Model Errors

Know which error you are fighting before you reach for a fix.

Your model is stuck. Which error is actually biting you? I ask that before I touch anything, because each error gets fixed differently and the wrong fix wastes the whole run. Even with lots of training data, my models never perform perfectly. I split my total error into a budget with four parts: a floor of label noise no model can remove, plus three sources I can actually fight:

Your total error is a budget with four parts.
True Error = Irreducible Error + Approximation Error + Estimation Error + Optimization Error
Read the line as a sum: your true error is the irreducible floor plus the three errors you can actually fight. The irreducible part is label noise, a floor no fix removes. Approximation error is the part from a model too weak to draw the truth, estimation error the part from too little data, and optimization error the part from training that missed the right weights. The line's job is diagnosis before treatment: each part takes a different fix, so I work out which part is large before I reach for any of them, because the wrong fix wastes the whole run.

I think of error as a budget. Every point of error above the floor sits in exactly one of these three buckets, and each bucket gets fixed differently. No point tuning the optimizer when the bucket that is full is "my model is too weak."

Approximation error: the model is too weak to draw the truth

Start with the family I picked. My model class is the family of functions my chosen model form can represent at all, every curve it could ever draw. If the true function is not in that family, no training run finds it. The model is too weak for the job, full stop. When I land here, I stop tuning and reach for a stronger model, a more expressive one that can represent more complex functions.

Estimation error: too little data, so the model memorizes noise

Say my model class is strong enough. I can still starve it. This is error from not having enough data: with a small dataset I end up overfitting to noise, so I cannot find the right parameters. The tell is the generalization gap, the difference between my test error and my training error. Training error low and test error high means the gap is wide and my data ran out. My fixes are more data, regularization (penalties or tricks that stop the model leaning on quirks of the training data), or data augmentation (new training examples made by transforming the ones you already have).

Optimization error: the right weights exist and training missed them

Sometimes the family is fine and the data is fine, and training still blows it. This is error due to not finding the best parameters within my model class. The usual culprits are a non-convex loss function (a loss surface with many hills and valleys instead of one smooth bowl), bad local minima (bottoms that are not the deepest bottom, so training settles into one and stops improving), or saddle points (flat spots where the slope vanishes in every direction without being a bottom). I reach for a better optimizer or a good schedule for the learning rate, the step size that scales each update.

The first two fight each other, and that fight is the classic bias-variance trade-off. Bias is the miss I bake in when my model is too weak: even the best fit in my class lands systematically off the truth. Variance is the opposite miss: my model is flexible enough, but it bends to fit the noise in the data I happened to collect, so a different dataset would give me a different model. Push bias down with a stronger model and variance usually climbs, because the stronger model can chase noise too. Push variance down with a simpler model and bias climbs back, because the simpler model misses more of the truth. I cannot minimize both at once. I can only choose where to sit. Optimization error does not play this trade. It is its own beast. Here is how I figure out which one is actually biting me:

  • Both errors sit high. If I see high training error and high test error, my bias is too high and the model is underfitting. I get a better model.
  • Training error is low and test error is high. My model is too complex relative to my dataset size, so I overfit: low training error but high test error, a large generalization gap, the difference between the two errors. I add more data, regularize, or even go with a simpler model.
  • My training error decreases too slowly or plateaus early. That one is on training, not the model or the data: an optimization error. I adjust the optimizer.
  • My test error improves with more data. That is an estimation error, and the data was the bottleneck.
  • My test error plateaus even with more data. That is an approximation error. The model itself is too limited, and data cannot fix it.
  • My training error drops with more expressive models, models that can represent more complex functions. That is an approximation error. If the training error stays high instead, that is an optimization error.
  • My test error sits much higher than my training error, and adding more data improves the test error. That is variance, the estimation error above. If the test error does not improve, I may be looking at the irreducible error in the data, also known as the Bayes error.

The shape of your two curves tells you which bucket is full. Diagnose first, then reach for the matching fix.

I always read this picture in words first. As capacity grows, and by capacity I mean the model size, how much the model can represent, the test error falls while the model is underfitting, then climbs once it starts memorizing the training data. That minimum is the sweet spot. Too little capacity and both errors sit high; too much and the test error climbs the overfitting wall. A larger dataset sinks the whole test curve and moves the sweet spot right.

Two lines: the training curve, and the test curve built on it.
Training Error = 8Capacity + 0.2
Capacity is the model size. The training error is the first curve: the 8 over Capacity term falls as the model grows, down to the 0.2 floor. Every error value in this toy is in percent, so that floor is 0.2 percent.
Test Error = Training Error + 30 × Capacity4 × Data Size + Capacity
The test error is the training error plus a penalty, and the stacked fraction is a division: 30 times Capacity, divided by 4 times Data Size plus Capacity. Read it that way and the behavior follows: the penalty grows as capacity grows, because capacity sits on the top line, and shrinks as the dataset grows, because data size sits on the bottom line. Because capacity also sits in the bottom line, the penalty levels off instead of growing forever: past a point the model has memorized everything there is to memorize. The training curve sinks toward the dashed floor as the model grows. The test curve falls toward it first, then climbs away from it once the penalty takes over, and neither curve can cross below it: that floor is the label noise.

Irreducible error: the noise floor no model can remove

Can I tune my way past every error? No. Irreducible error is the part I cannot remove, no matter what model, data, or optimizer I use. It comes from the randomness or noise inherent in the data itself.

Say the true relationship between an input x and a target y has noise from natural randomness baked in: even a perfect model cannot predict that noise away. This floor is the Bayes error, the lower bound on test error. When I keep improving the model and the test error plateaus, I suspect I am hitting it.

Here is my check. If my training error is low (optimization succeeded), my test error is close to my training error (low variance), and increasing the model size or data does not dramatically reduce the error, I am probably as close as I can be to the irreducible noise floor.

The signature I look for: both training and test error sit low and close to each other. That is when I go to my boss and say, "I've hit the theoretical minimum error achievable on this dataset, and nothing I do will decrease this inherent noise."

No model can capture this because it is not a learnable signal. That is when I ask for data from another modality, another kind of sensor or signal, or sensory data to help the model with more information about the true latent state, the true underlying condition the sensors only hint at. Combining these complementary signals can reduce the noise floor.

I keep one concrete example from the literature for this, so it does not sound like hand-waving: LeNet-5 was an early convolutional network built to read handwritten digits, and the bigger modern CNNs (convolutional neural networks, networks whose layers scan an image for small local patterns) that followed it push the same job much further:

Evaluate on new users and noisy sensors, not just lab data

What do I actually check when I evaluate a model?

  • Accuracy: Does it classify correctly?
  • Latency: Is it fast enough for real-time use?
  • Robustness: Does it handle new users and noisy sensors?
  • Usability: Does it work for daily scenarios, not just on lab data?

I test three ways:

  1. Offline Testing: Cross-validation (splitting the data into parts, training on some and testing on the held-out part, then rotating which part is held out) within and across subjects and measuring metrics.
  2. Online Testing: I test the system in streaming mode, with data arriving continuously and each item processed as it comes rather than from a stored set, measuring latency, stability, and false positives, the cases where the system reports an event that did not happen. I check throughput too, how many items the system processes per unit time. How many errors per hour?
  3. Stress Testing: I vary conditions like placement and noise level, and augment with synthetic perturbations, small changes I invent and add on purpose to shake the system. Is the system able to recover quickly from an error?

Overfitting is memorizing your subjects: claim generalization only after cross-subject tests

Overfitting is the model memorizing your training data, noise and all, so it faceplants on anything new. Usual suspects: too much model, too little data, noisy labels. As I said earlier, a large gap between training and test error is the tell.

Three tests: new subjects, new sessions, new conditions

With wearables, I have to be careful here. Training and testing on seen subjects is a much easier problem because the model can memorize subject idiosyncrasies, so the test accuracy would look much better than it would on a new person.

  • Cross-Subject Generalization: I train on some people and test on entirely new people. I can do a form of cross-validation by testing on a held-out subject and repeating this across all subjects, then averaging the performance.
  • Temporal Generalization: This is session-to-session robustness. Does a model trained on a subject's data from day 1 work on that same subject's data from day 2? Is it robust to time variance?
  • Domain Shift: Does varying the placement or recording conditions impact the model's performance? Or does the model collapse under realistic shifts?

So before I claim generalization or start looking at power laws, the pattern where error falls by a steady factor each time the data or the model grows by the same multiple, I need a large enough dataset and enough experiments behind the claim. Only then does performance over dataset sizes mean anything. A power law earns its place here as a prediction, how much error a larger dataset or a larger model should buy, and that prediction is worth nothing until the pattern has been measured across enough sizes to trust it.

When new users fail, the model overfit subject-specific patterns

When that happens, my model has overfit to subject-specific patterns. I fix it through the data, the model, and how I adapt.

First, I collect more diverse training data. That is the most important step. With wearables, I want to adapt efficiently to new subjects without negative transfer, where the adapted model ends up worse than one trained on the new subject alone. The failure I am guarding against is adaptation overwriting useful general features with subject-specific noise. So commonly, I freeze most of the network, holding those weights fixed so they stop training, and fine-tune the final layers, training just those layers a little more on the new subject. Or I introduce adapter modules like LoRA (low-rank adapters: small trainable matrices added beside the frozen weights) and bottleneck layers (narrow layers that force information through a small channel) for fine-tuning.

There is also layer-wise unfreezing, where I start with head-only fine-tuning (at first only the head, the final output layers, trains), and if performance plateaus, I unfreeze higher encoder layers, the earlier layers that build features from the raw input, one by one to keep fine-tuning from washing out the general features the encoder already learned, the failure called catastrophic forgetting, where training on the new subject erases what the model learned before. I can use weight regularization to keep personalized weights close to the pre-trained ones, the weights the model learned earlier, on other data, before this task. Or I can keep a smaller buffer of original subjects and train jointly on all of them to avoid drifting too far toward subject-specific patterns.

I can try different augmentation techniques and regularization, covered on the Regularization and Data Augmentation page. And I spend some time on normalizing and pre-processing the data per subject so I can align the amplitude of the signals across users. I can do this in an unsupervised fashion, with no labels and no correct answers at all, where the stream of data from new users is what adapts my normalization layers.

That is the diagnosis. Say the curves told me the model overfits: training error sinks while test error climbs. How do I fight it? Two ways, constrain the model or grow the data: Regularization and Data Augmentation.
