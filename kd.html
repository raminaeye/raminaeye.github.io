Knowledge Distillation

You have a giant model that works great and costs a fortune to run. How do you squeeze it into a small one? Knowledge Distillation (KD) is embarrassingly simple: train the small model, the student, to copy a big pre-trained model, the teacher.

DistilBERT is BERT-base (a pretrained language model that reads text in both directions) with 40% of the parameters chopped off, and it keeps 97% of BERT's performance on the GLUE language benchmark. Same story with distilled MobileNet variants (small networks for phones) taught by ResNet-50 (a deep residual network). The big model you cannot afford to serve teaches the small model you can.

The problem is deployment. An ensemble of averaged models is the most reliable accuracy boost, but too expensive to serve. Bucila et al. showed an ensemble's knowledge could be compressed into one model; distillation is the simplest version of that idea.

The key insight: the teacher's soft outputs carry information the hard labels never will. A teacher that says 90% cat, 8% dog, 2% car is telling you which mistakes are reasonable. That ranking, the "dark knowledge", is what the student learns, from the teacher's softmax (the step that turns raw scores into probabilities) softened with a temperature, alongside the usual hard-label loss.

In Hinton et al.'s paper the distilled student nearly matched the big ensemble on MNIST (handwritten digits), and the same compression significantly improved the acoustic model (the part that scores which words the audio contains) of a heavily used commercial speech system.

Soft predictions: what the teacher really knows

Besides the hard label ("this is a cat"), I train the student on the teacher's soft predictions, its probability over all classes. To make them more useful I soften the teacher's logits, its raw score per class, with temperature scaling:

Temperature scaling: divide the logits by T, then softmax.
si = ziT
zi is the teacher's logit for class i and T the temperature; si is the softened score.
pi = exp(si)Σj exp(sj)
The softmax turns the scores into soft probabilities pi; the sum over all classes j makes them add to 1. At T = 1 this is the standard softmax. Above 1 the distribution softens and shows class similarities (a truck is closer to a car than to a cat). Below 1 it sharpens toward one-hot, all probability on one class.

With a higher temperature the student picks up the teacher's inductive biases (tendencies its architecture and training baked in) and gets a much better signal, especially when labels are sparse or noisy.

Distillation loss: match the teacher's soft targets

The student answers to two bosses: the true labels, through cross-entropy (the loss that punishes a confident wrong answer hardest), and the teacher's soft predictions, through KL divergence (one number for how far apart two probability distributions sit). A mixing weight α (alpha) blends them:

One distillation step in one line: the total loss blends the teacher's soft lesson with the true labels.
L = (1−α)·LCE(student_logits, true_labels) + α·T2·LKL(student_logitsT, teacher_logitsT)
Read it left to right: the total loss L is the labels' share, 1 minus α, of LCE, the cross-entropy of the student's logits against the true labels, plus α's share of LKL, which compares the student's temperature-softened logits against the teacher's, its term carrying T2, read as T squared. The true labels keep the student honest.

The subscript T means both sets of logits are softened at the same temperature T (see the soft predictions section) before the KL term compares them.

The frozen teacher supplies soft targets, the dataset supplies the true label, and the mixed loss blends them at weights 0.7 and 0.3 and trains only the student.

At the extremes, α = 1 is pure imitation of the teacher and α = 0 drops the teacher entirely. In PyTorch the equation takes a few lines, numbered as soft loss, hard loss, blend: 

def blended_distillation_loss(student_logits, teacher_logits, labels, T=2.0, alpha=0.7):
    # 1. Distillation (soft target) loss: KL divergence between softened distributions
    soft_targets = F.softmax(teacher_logits / T, dim=1)
    student_log_probs = F.log_softmax(student_logits / T, dim=1)
    soft_loss = F.kl_div(student_log_probs, soft_targets, reduction='batchmean') * (T * T)

    # 2. Hard loss: standard cross entropy with ground truth
    hard_loss = F.cross_entropy(student_logits, labels)

    # 3. Weighted combination
    return alpha * soft_loss + (1.0 - alpha) * hard_loss

The T squared is there because dividing logits by T shrinks the soft term's gradients by roughly T squared, so the original paper multiplies it back to keep the two terms on a comparable scale.

One training step, walked through

Every training step the teacher sets soft targets, the student guesses, the loss mixes both lessons, and the student updates.

One distillation step: the loss blends the soft lesson with the true labels, and the temperature sets how soft.
L = (1−α)·LCE + α·T2·LKL
The labels' share, 1 minus α, of the hard lesson, plus α of the soft lesson scaled by T2 (T squared), which keeps the softened signal from barely moving the weights.
soft targets = softmax(zT)
Divide the teacher's raw scores z by T, then softmax: a higher temperature spreads the probabilities and shows what the teacher finds similar.
T temperature: higher, softerLKL soft lesson: KL divergence, the gap between two distributions (student against teacher, both softened)LCE hard lesson: cross-entropy against the true labelsα the blend weight; L the total losssoftmax(zT) the soft targets; z the teacher's raw scores

The student learns the teacher's ranking, not just its answer; a hard label would teach only "cat".

The gap the distillation loss trains on is the KL divergence between the teacher's soft targets and the student's softened guess:

KL divergence: how far the student's bars sit from the teacher's.
KL(p ‖ q) = Σi pi log piqi
pi is the teacher's soft probability for class i and qi the student's. Each class adds pi times the log of their ratio; the total is zero when the bars match exactly and grows as they move apart.

In the toy below the student's scores start at zero, so the gap starts wide; each gradient step, a small nudge downhill on the loss, pulls the student's bars toward the teacher's and the gap shrinks toward zero.

What happens: distilling a ResNet-18 into a small CNN

The full experiment lives in the companion notebook. The teacher is a ResNet-18 pretrained on ImageNet (a large labeled image dataset) with its classifier fine-tuned on STL-10 (96×96 color images in 10 classes). The student is DeeperStudentCNN, a plain 4-block CNN (convolutional neural network) a fraction of the teacher's size. The run, with Adam (an optimizer that adapts each weight's step size) over 10 epochs (full passes over the data), in outline: 

teacher.eval()                                   # frozen ResNet-18
opt = torch.optim.Adam(student.parameters(), lr=1e-3)
for epoch in range(10):
    for x, y in train_loader:                    # STL-10 batches
        with torch.no_grad():
            teacher_logits = teacher(x)
        loss = blended_distillation_loss(student(x), teacher_logits, y,
                                         T=2.0, alpha=0.7)
        opt.zero_grad()
        loss.backward()
        opt.step()

First, the teacher's accuracy, which is the target we are chasing:

Then the student, trained with the distillation loss from Distillation loss: match the teacher's soft targets:

53.46%
Student test accuracy (STL-10)
DeeperStudentCNN, response-based KD, learning from the teacher's final outputs only

53% is a long way from 93%, but this student is tiny, trained for only 10 epochs, and learned entirely by watching the teacher. Its loss was still falling when training stopped. That is a fast win.

I also tried making the student mimic the teacher's intermediate features, adding an MSE (mean squared error) term between the two networks' feature maps alongside the CE and KL terms (mixing weights 0.5, 0.3, 0.2 across the three terms, temperature 2.0, same 10 epochs):

42.48%
Student test accuracy (STL-10)
DeeperStudentCNN, feature-based KD, also matching the teacher's intermediate features

Worse, not better. Matching raw activations (each layer's outputs) across very different architectures is hard: feature scales differ, and copying a big network's internals can fight the classification objective.

Beyond logits: feature, relation, and self-distillation

Response-Based Distillation (Logit Distillation)

The form in my ResNet-18 experiment, and the most common. The student watches only the teacher's final outputs, nothing internal, and the loss is the KL divergence above. The softened logits already carry most of the signal; DistilBERT and later distilled MobileNet variants did exactly this.

Feature-Based Distillation

The student copies the teacher's intermediate features, not just the final answers. Sometimes it helps, but it backfired in the experiment above. If you try it, normalize the features and pick the layer carefully.

Relation-Based Distillation

The student copies relationships: which examples the teacher thinks are similar, how classes sit relative to each other. The teacher's map of the world, not its exact coordinates, which is more forgiving for a student too small to match the teacher's numbers.

Self-Distillation

The weird one: a student with the teacher's exact architecture, trained on the teacher's outputs, somehow ends up better. Almost a free lunch: the soft targets act as a regularizer (they keep the model from memorizing label noise) and smooth the optimization. People usually iterate once or twice and bank the gains.

Distillation pitfalls: capacity gaps, temperature, and teacher mistakes

  • Feature distillation backfired in my notebook (the experiment above).
  • Mind the capacity gap. A student far smaller than the teacher may be unable to mimic it. A "teacher assistant" sized in between, taught by the teacher and then teaching the student, sometimes helps more than distilling directly.
  • Temperature needs tuning. Too low and you get hard labels with no dark knowledge; too high and every class looks equally likely. Values between 2 and 5 are the usual starting range.
  • The student inherits the teacher's mistakes. Whatever biases and blind spots the teacher has get baked into the soft targets, so distill from a teacher you trust.
