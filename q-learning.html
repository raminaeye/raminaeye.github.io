A robot that only knows how good each spot is still cannot pick a step unless it knows where every step leads. How can it choose well without that?

The agent is the learner that picks the moves, as taught in RL Basics. A state is the agent's situation right now; a move, or action, is one choice the agent can make in a state; a reward is the number the world hands back after each move, good or bad, as taught in RL Basics. Here the agent is a warehouse robot that must reach its charging dock. It starts in one bottom corner and the dock sits in the other. The floor is a grid of cells, one per place the robot can stand. It knows only which cell it stands in, not where each move leads or what it costs.

Figure 1 shows the floor the robot learns on: four rows by twelve columns, the robot at bottom left and the dock at bottom right. The red squares are the ledge, the green square is the dock, and the question marks stand on moves the robot has not tried yet. Take the robot at the start: every move wears a question mark, since no score exists yet for any of them.

An episode is one run from the start until it ends, as taught in RL Basics. The ledge is the strip of floor between the start and the dock. Sutton and Barto's textbook calls the ledge a cliff and the dock the goal. Reaching the dock can take many moves.

Each move's reward is minus the battery it uses, so the best total is the one closest to zero. Every move costs a little battery; stepping onto the ledge costs a lot and puts the robot back at the start; only reaching the dock ends the episode. The cheapest way to the dock is the shortest path that never steps onto the ledge.

1 Scores for moves, not just for places

A move is only worth making if what follows it is worth having. The return is all the reward still to come, with later rewards counted a little less, as taught in RL Basics. A state's value \(V(s)\) is the return the agent expects from state \(s\), as taught in RL Basics. A policy is the agent's rule for choosing a move in each state; it may always pick the same move or pick by chance, as taught in RL Basics. On this floor every value is below zero, because every step costs battery.

Knowing how good a spot is does not yet say which step to take. A model is the agent's learned guess of where a move leads and what it pays, as taught in RL Method Families. A move's value \(Q(s,a)\), also called its score, is the return the agent expects if it makes move \(a\) in state \(s\) and plays on from there, as taught in RL Basics. Epsilon-greedy usually plays the top-scored move and, with chance \(\epsilon\), a random one instead, as taught in RL Basics. From a cell away from the walls, each of the four moves leads to a different neighbouring cell.

Figure 2 shows the loop the robot runs on every step, from standing in a cell to changing one score. Each box is one stage: pick a move, take it and pay its cost, judge it by its reward plus how good the next cell looks, then change that score. The repeat arrow sends the loop back to the start. Take a step toward the dock: the loop picks the move, pays its cost, judges it against the next cell, and nudges that move's score.

Best play is the policy that earns the most return; \(Q^{*}\) is each move's value under it, as taught in RL Basics. The robot learns these scores only from its own steps, one cell at a time.

RL Basics taught how to improve a cell's value \(V(s)\). To choose a move from \(V\) alone, the robot would need to know where each move leads, which is a model it does not have. A score for each move, \(Q(s,a)\), lets it choose by comparing scores, with no model at all. And it wants the scores of best play, not of whatever it happens to do now.

2 A table of move scores

One score per cell and move

What. Picture the floor as a table: one row per cell, one column per move, every entry a guess of its score.

How. Compare the four scores in the robot's row; the top one is the move to play.

Why. When it works, the robot picks its best move by reading the table, with no model of where moves lead.

A score to aim at

What. Picture the robot after one step: the target is the number its score is pulled toward.

How. Add the step's reward to the best score of the landing cell, counted by the discount, which weighs later rewards.

Why. When it works, each score is pulled toward what best play would earn there, so the table climbs toward the best route.

Take the best of what follows

What. Picture the landing cell: its four moves carry four scores, and the target takes only the best.

How. Scan the four scores in the landing cell and keep the largest; the rest stay out.

Why. When it works, the target aims at best play while the robot wanders, so the scores end at best play.

A tabular method stores one entry per state, or per state and move. So a step only ever changes the one entry it played.

Figure 3 shows the table itself: every cell split into a triangle per move, shaded by that move's score. Brighter triangles mean less battery still to spend, so the bright cells sit near the dock and the ledge stays red. Take the cell just above the dock: its downward triangle shines brightest, since that move ends the episode cheapest.

The discount \(\gamma\) sets how much a reward one step later still counts.

An expected value is the average over everything that can happen by chance, weighted by how likely each outcome is, as taught in Expected value and RL Basics. The sign \(\mathbb{E}\) marks an expected value, as taught in Probability. On this floor the average hardly matters, because the same move from the same cell always has the same result.

Take the robot in the cell just above the dock. Best play is to step down onto the dock, and that move's score is minus the cost of that last step.

2\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{Q^{*}}}(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s_t}}, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a_t}}) = \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\mathbb{E}}}\big[\, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#196f3d}{y^{*}_t}} \mid \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s_t}}, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a_t}} \,\big]\)
Intuition. A move's score is an average over what the floor might do after it. The spot value averages over the moves best play picks first, while the move value pins one move first.
The mathematics. \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{Q^{*}}}(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s_t}}, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a_t}})\) is the score of the cell \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s_t}}\) and move \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a_t}}\) just played. Example. Take a move that stays on the floor: its score averages the target over the one landing the floor returns, the step's reward plus the best score from there.
A move that stays on the floor lands on a cell whose best next score still counts, so its target adds the discounted best score from there. The two moves from that cell carry different targets because they land in different cells.

On this floor that makes the best scores climb toward zero as the cells get closer to the dock.

3 Aiming every score at best play

Move a little, not all the way

What. Picture the played entry: it moves a little toward its target, never all at once.

How. Take the surprise, the gap between target and guess, and add a share of it to the played entry, sized by the learning rate.

Why. When it works, the scores creep toward best play step by step, and one step never rewrites a guess.

Bootstrapping means putting your own current guess for the next state inside the target, as taught in RL Basics. The TD (temporal difference) error \(\delta\) is how much one step surprised the agent, the target minus the current guess, as taught in RL Basics. A fall is a step onto the ledge: the robot pays the big cost and goes back to the start, so it must walk the whole floor again.

The learning rate \(\eta\) sets how big a step each update takes, as taught in RL Basics. The robot steps along an aisle toward the dock and pays a little battery; it judges that move by its reward plus the best it believes it can do from the new cell.

\(\arg\max\) picks the move whose score is largest, as taught in RL Basics. The greedy policy plays the top-scored move in each state. While the scores are still guesses, the top entry can point the wrong way, and the next steps correct it.

Equation 2. The table holds guesses, not best-play scores, so the target must be built from what the table holds now.

Intuition. The target starts with the pay the move just earned, before anything after it is added. That pay alone is the whole target when the move ends the episode.

Intuition. The surprise is the gap between the target and the guess, saying how far the played score missed and in which direction.
The mathematics. \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#b03a2e}{\delta_t}}\) is the surprise at step \(t\), one number: the target minus the score the table already holds. \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#196f3d}{y_t}}\) is the target from line 2, built from the table's guesses. \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{Q}}(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s_t}}, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a_t}})\) is the score the table holds for the cell \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s_t}}\) and move \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a_t}}\) just played. Example. Take a step whose target sits above the score the table holds: the surprise is positive, so the guess must rise.
4\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{Q}}(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s_t}}, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a_t}}) \leftarrow \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{Q}}(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s_t}}, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a_t}}) + \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\eta}}\, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#b03a2e}{\delta_t}}\)
Intuition. The played entry takes one small step toward its target, and the rest of the table stays put.
The mathematics. \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{Q}}(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s_t}}, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a_t}})\) on the left is the score the table holds for the cell and move just played. 5\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\pi_{\text{greedy}}}}(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s}}) = \arg\max_{\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a}}}\, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{Q}}(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s}}, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a}})\)
Intuition. Once the scores are nudged, the robot plays the best one. The greedy policy is the rule that reads the top-scored move off the table.
The mathematics. \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\pi_{\text{greedy}}}}\) is the greedy policy, a rule that takes a cell \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s}}\) and returns one move. argmax over \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a}}\) scans the four moves in that cell and returns the move itself, not its score: the move whose \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{Q}}\) entry is largest. \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{Q}}(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s}}, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a}})\) is the table's score for move \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a}}\) in cell \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s}}\), the same table the update above keeps nudging. While learning, the robot plays epsilon-greedy around this policy, sometimes a random move instead of the top-scored one. \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\pi_{\text{greedy}}}}(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s}})\) equals argmax over \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a}}\) of \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{Q}}(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s}}, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a}})\). Together the five lines say one thing: aim each score at its target, nudge it by its surprise, and play the top-scored move.
Example. Take the robot in a cell where one move's score stands above the rest: the greedy policy plays that move, unless a random move is drawn instead.
Take a step toward the dock from the cell above it, where the target comes out above the score the table holds. The surprise is positive, so the entry for that cell and move is nudged upward by a share of the surprise, while every other entry stays as it was. The next time the robot stands in that cell, the top-scored move is read off the nudged table, and the same five lines run again.

The target never uses the move epsilon-greedy will pick next, only the best one, so random exploring moves change which steps arrive but not what the target aims at; that is why the table ends at best-play scores. Off-policy means learning the values of one policy while playing another. Q-learning is off-policy, since it learns best play while it plays epsilon-greedy. One step costs little: a max over the four moves in the next cell, then one write to the table, as the two lines of code in Section 5, A table, a max and a nudge after every step show.

4 A step on the floor and the score it nudges

Figure 4 shows one update: battery bars hanging down, with the walkthrough card below it. Each bar is one move's score where the robot stands. Stages 1 and 3 light no line. Stage 2 lights line 5, unless the step explores. Stages 4 and 5 light lines 1 and 2, except on the last step, where only line 1 lights. Stage 6 lights line 3, and stage 7 lights line 4. Take the last step: the move lands on the dock, so the target is just the step's reward and only the played bar changes.

Equation 2 again, walked one stage at a time. One real step on the floor becomes one small change in what the robot believes, so a tried move looks better or worse next time.

3\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#b03a2e}{\delta_t}} = \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#196f3d}{y_t}} - \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{Q}}(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s_t}}, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a_t}})\)
Intuition. The walk now measures the gap between the target and the guess. That gap is the surprise this step carried.
The mathematics. At stage 6 of the walk, \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#b03a2e}{\delta_t}}\) is the surprise at step \(t\), one number: the target minus the score the table already holds. \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#196f3d}{y_t}}\) is the target from line 2, built from the table's guesses. \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{Q}}(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s_t}}, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a_t}})\) is the score the table holds for the cell \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s_t}}\) and move \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a_t}}\) just played. When the target sits above the guess, \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#b03a2e}{\delta_t}}\) is positive and the guess must rise; when it sits below, \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#b03a2e}{\delta_t}}\) is negative and the guess must fall; when they match, \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#b03a2e}{\delta_t}}\) is zero and the guess stays. \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#b03a2e}{\delta_t}}\) equals \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#196f3d}{y_t}}\) minus \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{Q}}(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s_t}}, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a_t}})\). Line 4 moves the guess by a share of this surprise.
Example. Take the exploring step, a random move: its target sits above the score the table holds, so the surprise is positive.
4\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{Q}}(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s_t}}, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a_t}}) \leftarrow \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{Q}}(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s_t}}, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a_t}}) + \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\eta}}\, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#b03a2e}{\delta_t}}\)
Intuition. At stage 7 of the walk, the played entry moves part of the way toward its target.
The mathematics. \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{Q}}(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s_t}}, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a_t}})\) on the left is the score the table holds for the cell and move just played. The arrow reads replace with: the old entry plus \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\eta}}\) times \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#b03a2e}{\delta_t}}\). \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\eta}}\) is the learning rate, the size of each step the guess takes toward the target: a bigger \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\eta}}\) moves the guess further in one step; a smaller \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\eta}}\) moves it less. The plus adds a fraction of the surprise, not the whole surprise. \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{Q}}(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s_t}}, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a_t}})\) gets replaced by \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{Q}}(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s_t}}, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a_t}})\) plus \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\eta}}\) times \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#b03a2e}{\delta_t}}\). Line 5 plays the top-scored move from the table this line nudges.
Example. Take the exploring step again: its entry moves up by a share of the surprise, and the rest of the table stays put.
5\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\pi_{\text{greedy}}}}(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s}}) = \arg\max_{\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a}}}\, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{Q}}(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s}}, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a}})\)
Intuition. The walk ends with the choice the nudged table makes. The greedy policy reads the top-scored move off that table.
The mathematics. \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\pi_{\text{greedy}}}}\) is the greedy policy, a rule that takes a cell \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s}}\) and returns one move. argmax over \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a}}\) scans the four moves in that cell and returns the move itself, not its score: the move whose \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{Q}}\) entry is largest. \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{Q}}(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s}}, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a}})\) is the table's score for move \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a}}\) in cell \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s}}\), the same table the update above keeps nudging. While learning, the robot plays epsilon-greedy around this policy, sometimes a random move instead of the top-scored one. \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\pi_{\text{greedy}}}}(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s}})\) equals argmax over \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a}}\) of \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{Q}}(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s}}, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a}})\). Together the five lines say one thing: aim each score at its target, nudge it by its surprise, and play the top-scored move.
Example. Take the next step the walkthrough shows: the robot reads the top-scored move off the nudged table and plays it, unless a random move is drawn.

5 A table, a max and a nudge after every step

Gymnasium is a library of ready-made practice worlds. The code below runs on Gymnasium's CliffWalking-v1, which is this floor. The table comes first, then the move choice.

import gymnasium as gym
import numpy as np

env = gym.make("CliffWalking-v1")
Q = np.zeros((env.observation_space.n, env.action_space.n))
n_moves = env.action_space.n
GAMMA = 1.0
LR = 0.5
EPSILON = 1.0
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
  1. 1Make the floor and an all-zero table with one row per square and one column per move. Every score starts at zero; Section 6, How the starting scores steer the robot explains why that matters on this floor.
  2. 2The discount, the learning rate and the exploring chance, with the values the settings table lists, and the random draw the moves use.

The move choice is epsilon-greedy.

def act(s, eps):
    if rng.random() < eps:
        return int(rng.integers(n_moves))
    return int(np.argmax(Q[s]))
  1. 1Epsilon-greedy picks a random move with chance eps.
  2. 2Otherwise it picks the top-scored move in the row.

After every move, the update is two lines.

y = r if terminated else r + GAMMA * Q[s_next].max()
Q[s, a] += LR * (y - Q[s, a])
  1. 1The target. Here r is \(r_{t+1}\), the step's reward, and s_next is \(s_{t+1}\), the next cell.
  2. 2The Q-learning update, nudging the played entry toward its target.

Then the loop that runs the robot, episode after episode.

eps = EPSILON  # fixed here; later on this page it shrinks
for episode in range(200):
    s, info = env.reset()
    terminated = False
    while not terminated:
        a = act(s, eps)
        s_next, r, terminated, truncated, info = env.step(a)
        if terminated:
            y = r
        else:
            y = r + GAMMA * Q[s_next].max()
        Q[s, a] += LR * (y - Q[s, a])
        s = s_next
  1. 1Each episode starts at the start square and runs until the dock.
  2. 2Each step plays a move, and the floor answers with a reward and the next square. terminated is true only at the dock, and this floor is never cut off, so truncated never ends it. info holds the extra detail the floor hands back with each answer; on this floor it carries nothing the code uses.
  3. 3The update from the block above, then move on to the next square.

6 How the starting scores steer the robot

Starting every score at zero is hopeful when every real score is below zero, because moves not yet tried then look best. The robot's route is the path its best moves trace from the start, drawn as arrows in Figure 5; it counts only if it reaches the dock without a loop. A gloomy start puts every score far below any real score; once one route reaches the dock its scores rise above all the others, so the robot can keep that first route even when a shorter one exists.

7 Updating after every step, and exploring less over time

Wander early, trust the map late

What. Picture the robot early on: every score is a guess, so it tries untested moves; late on, it mostly plays its top-scored move.

How. After each episode, multiply the exploring chance by a decay factor just under one, never below its floor.

Why. When it works, early wandering finds the dock and late play follows the best route.

Q-learning updates after every step, while the episode is still going. It learns online, from a stream of moves, and never has to store whole episodes or wait for them to end. There is no batch of steps to gather and no network to train.

The table is a NumPy array, indexed by cell and move. Every score starts at zero, as How the starting scores steer the robot explains. Only reaching the dock ends the episode, so only that step uses the step's reward alone.

Equation 3. The robot explores a lot early, when every score is a guess, and mostly plays its top-scored move only once the chance has shrunk to its floor, when the scores have settled.

1\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\epsilon}} \leftarrow \max(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\kappa}}\, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\epsilon}},\ \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\epsilon_{\min}}})\)
Intuition. Exploring shrinks a little after every episode, but never below a floor. The exploring chance is the share of steps where the robot tries something untested.
The mathematics. \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\epsilon}}\) is the chance epsilon-greedy takes a random move instead of the top-scored move. The arrow reads replace with: the old \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\epsilon}}\) is thrown away and replaced by what max picks. Inside, \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\kappa}}\) times \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\epsilon}}\) multiplies the old \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\epsilon}}\) by \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\kappa}}\), the decay factor just under one, so the chance shrinks a little after each episode. max takes the larger of the two, so the shrink stops at the floor: \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\epsilon}}\) never drops below \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\epsilon_{\min}}}\). \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\epsilon}}\) gets replaced by the max of \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\kappa}}\) times \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\epsilon}}\) and \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\epsilon_{\min}}}\). So the exploring chance shrinks a little after each episode and never drops below its floor.
Example. Take the robot early in training, when its exploring chance is still high: it tries untested moves often, and the chance shrinks a little after each episode.
Take the robot early in training, when its exploring chance is still high and it tries untested moves often, and late in training, when the chance has shrunk but is still well above its floor, so it wanders often and plays its top-scored move more than at the start. The shrink never reaches zero, so even late in training the robot tries a random move now and then, and a move it has never tried can still be found.

In code, the shrink happens at the end of each episode.

eps = max(EPS_MIN, eps * EPS_DECAY)

7.1 A short run locks in, a long run need not

Watkins and Dayan proved that, if every move is tried often enough in every cell, the table's scores settle on the best-play scores. Within the tool's short run a gloomy start can lock in the first route it finds, long before every move has been tried often enough.
