Speech recorded across a room sounds distant because the walls send back delayed copies of it. The room is one filter, its impulse response \(h\): this page describes \(h\), measures how long it rings, and removes its late tail from a single recording.

1 A room is a filter

Clap once in a lecture hall and the microphone records the clap, then the hall answering it. That answer is the room’s impulse response \(h\). A room is a linear time-invariant (LTI) filter: speak a word instead, and every sample of the word gets the same answer, so the room’s tail runs on after each sound. The room material on this page follows the thesis by Anushiravani (2016).

A recording made in a room is the dry sound filtered by the room, plus noise.
\(\displaystyle y = x \ast h + v\)
\(x\) is the dry sound, what a microphone right at the mouth would record. \(h\) is the room response from the talker to the microphone, and \(\ast\) is convolution, so \(y\) stacks one copy of \(h\) per sample of \(x\), as the word in Figure 2 shows. \(v\) is background noise. Every later section works on this line.

2 Echoes

The ear hears copies of a sound as separate echoes when they arrive about 50 to 100 ms apart; closer copies fuse into reverberation. A film editor matching a dubbed line to a scene’s echo can read those copies off a recording: find the peaks of the recording’s log power over time, keep each peak’s delay and height relative to the first sound, and rebuild the echo as delayed, smaller clicks. The simplest room response is exactly that, a few clicks far apart.

An echo kernel is the sound plus delayed, smaller copies of it.
\(\displaystyle h[n] = \delta[n] + 0.5\, \delta[n - D] + 0.25\, \delta[n - 2D]\)
This is a simple \(h\) for the line of section 1. \(\delta[n]\) is one click at sample 0, so \(\delta[n - D]\) is the same click \(D\) samples later. So the kernel plays the sound, a half-height copy \(D\) samples later and a quarter-height copy after \(2 D\).

3 The parts of a room response

A real room sends back thousands of copies. The first to arrive is the direct sound, then a few distinct early reflections off the nearest walls, then so many reflections that they merge into a dense tail that dies away smoothly.

Early reflections colour the sound but help a listener understand it. The late tail smears each sound over the next, and that is the part section 9 removes.

4 How long a room rings

The standard measure of a room is how long its tail takes to fall by 60 dB, called \(T_{60}\). Offices sit around 0.2 to 0.6 s and large churches near 3 s (Pacheco and Seara 2008). A bigger room rings longer, because sound travels further between walls; softer walls ring shorter, because each bounce loses more. Figure 5 draws a box-shaped room.

Sabine’s rule: volume makes a room ring, absorbing surface stops it.
\(\displaystyle T_{60} = 0.161 \cdot \frac{V}{S \cdot a}\)
\(T_{60}\) is in seconds; 60 dB is a factor of 1,000 in amplitude (decibels). \(V\) is the volume in \(\mathrm{m}^{3}\), \(S\) the total surface in \(\mathrm{m}^{2}\), and \(a\) the average share of sound each surface soaks up per bounce, from 0 to 1. The constant 0.161 is \(24 \ln 10\) divided by the speed of sound, 343 m/s, with ln the natural log. Because \(a\) sits under the line, doubling it halves the ring time, as the rows of Figure 5 say. The rule ignores temperature, furniture and frequency.

The direct sound weakens with distance, 6 dB per doubling, while the room’s tail fills the space at about the same level everywhere. Somewhere the two are equal: that distance is the critical distance \(d_{c}\).

How far from the talker the room takes over.
\(\displaystyle d_{c} = 0.057 \cdot \sqrt{\frac{V}{T_{60}}}\)
\(d_{c}\) is in metres, \(V\) in \(\mathrm{m}^{3}\) and \(T_{60}\) in seconds. The constant 0.057 comes from Sabine’s 0.161 and the way the direct sound spreads over a sphere. Inside \(d_{c}\) the direct sound is louder; beyond it the microphone hears mostly room. A bigger room pushes \(d_{c}\) out, a longer \(T_{60}\) pulls it in. The form assumes a talker that sends sound equally in all directions. Section 8 uses it: a microphone inside \(d_{c}\) is the cheapest fix.

5 A model of the tail

Builds on white noise and its flat PSD (Power Spectral Density, section 1) and the bell curve (Common Probability Distributions, section 5)

The dense tail looks like noise, and it falls by the same number of decibels every second. Polack’s model writes it as exactly that: white noise, whose power is spread evenly over frequency, under a falling exponential. Figure 7 shows how its envelope falls.

A room tail is Gaussian white noise that fades at a fixed rate.
\(\displaystyle h[n] = b[n] \cdot e^{-\zeta n}\)
\(b[n]\) is Gaussian white noise, a fresh bell-curve draw per sample. The exponential is the envelope in Figure 7: it multiplies the amplitude by the same factor every sample. Only the decay \(\zeta\) carries the room.
\(\displaystyle \zeta = \frac{3 \ln 10}{T_{60} \cdot f_{s}}\)
\(\zeta\) is the decay per sample. At \(n = T_{60} \cdot f_{s}\), one \(T_{60}\) in samples, the exponent is \(3 \ln 10\), so the envelope has fallen by a factor of 1,000, which is \(-60\) dB. A longer \(T_{60}\) gives a smaller \(\zeta\) and a slower fade.

Real tails die faster at high frequencies, where air and soft surfaces absorb more, so \(T_{60}\) is usually given per frequency band. Simulators that need the early reflections too place mirror images of the talker behind each wall, the image method of Allen and Berkley (1979).

6 Reading the decay off a response

A measured tail is too noisy to read a slope off directly. Schroeder’s trick is to add up, at each sample, all the energy still to come. Summed from each time to the end, the energies of a falling tail lie close to a straight line in dB (Figure 8); the last part bends because the sum stops.

Turn a noisy tail into a straight line, then read \(T_{60}\) off its slope.
\(\displaystyle \text{EDC}[n] = \sum_{m \ge n} h[m]^{2}\)
The energy decay curve (EDC) at sample \(n\) is the energy still to come, shown in dB relative to its start. Summing smooths the noise in \(b[n]\) away, as the straight line of Figure 8 shows.
\(\displaystyle T_{60} = \frac{60\ \text{dB}}{\text{decay rate in dB/s}}\)
The slope turned into a time: a steeper line falls 60 dB sooner, so the room rings shorter.
\(\displaystyle T_{60} \approx 2 \cdot T_{30}\)
A real recording rarely has 60 dB of range above its noise, so ISO 3382, the international standard for measuring rooms, fits a least-squares line (Linear Regression, section 2.3) from \(-5\) to \(-35\) dB. \(T_{30}\) is the time that line takes to fall those 30 dB, and doubling it gives \(T_{60}\). On a clean tail the fit reads the room’s own value.

Background noise under a tail adds energy that never fades, so the EDC flattens onto a noise floor. Where that floor sits above \(-60\) dB, the curve never reaches \(-60\) dB, which is why the fit stops at \(-35\) dB.

7 Measuring a real room

Builds on the order rule (LTI Systems and Convolution, section 6)

To measure \(h\), play a click and record. A popped balloon is the classic click, but the thesis finds its “dynamic range and the frequency range … are not broad enough”, and repeated pops “vary too much”. Acousticians play a sine sweep instead, a tone that glides from low to high frequency, loud and the same every time.

Because convolution order does not matter, convolving the recording with the sweep’s inverse pairs the sweep with its inverse, which leaves a click, and a click convolved with \(h\) is \(h\) itself (Figure 10). The loudspeaker and microphone add their own responses, so flat devices are used or their responses are removed by deconvolution. Measured responses are shared in databases such as the Aachen Impulse Response (AIR) database (Jeub et al. 2009), so a dry sound can be placed in a real room by convolution.

8 Why a room is hard to undo

Builds on undoing a known filter (The Wiener Filter, section 6)

The goal is the dry \(x\) from one wet recording \(y\). Inverting \(h\) directly fails for three reasons. A room response is usually not minimum phase, meaning a response whose inverse is both stable and causal, so its exact inverse needs future samples or grows without bound. Frequencies where the room nearly cancels itself blow up the noise when divided. And Wiener deconvolution needs \(h\), which one recording does not give.

The first fix is position: a microphone inside \(d_{c}\) (section 4) records more talker than room. When that is not possible, the methods of Figure 11 remain.

9 Removing the late tail

Builds on the spectral-subtraction gain (Spectral Subtraction for Denoising, section 5) and frames (The STFT and Spectrograms, section 2)

Lebart, Boucher and Denbigh (2001) treat the late tail as noise that can be predicted. In each frequency bin of the short-time Fourier transform (STFT), the tail now is whatever was in that bin a moment ago, faded by the room’s decay. They tested one microphone on rooms with \(T_{60}\) from 0.34 to 1.7 s and reported large gains for a speech recognizer.

Lebart, Boucher and Denbigh (2001) split each bin’s sound at \(T_{d}\), commonly 40 ms: what arrives before that split counts as early sound, and what arrives after it counts as late tail. Over \(T_{d}\) the room’s decay shrinks a bin’s power by a fixed factor, so the late tail now is the bin’s power \(T_{d}\) ago times that factor.

The late tail now is the room’s decay applied to what the bin held a moment ago.
\(\displaystyle \Delta = \frac{3 \ln 10}{T_{60}}\)
\(\Delta\) is the decay per second, the \(\zeta\) of section 5 times \(f_{s}\). A room that rings longer fades more slowly.
\(\displaystyle P_{\text{late}}[t] = e^{-2\Delta T_{d}} \cdot P_{y}[t - T_{d}]\)
\(P_{y}[t]\) is the reverberant power in one STFT bin of the frame at time \(t\); every line here holds bin by bin. \(T_{d}\) is the early and late split in seconds. The 2 is there because power is amplitude squared.
Spectral subtraction with the late tail as the noise, floored.
\(\displaystyle G[t] = {\max\left(1 - \frac{P_{\text{late}}[t]}{P_{y}[t]},\ \beta\right)}\)
\(G\) is the power gain for the bin: the share that is not late tail. \(\beta\), a small floor, plays the role it plays in spectral subtraction: no bin drops to an empty hole. This page uses \(\beta = 0.1\), a teaching value. The more of a bin’s power is predicted late tail, the lower its gain. The bin keeps its phase, the timing part of each STFT value, unchanged.

Late-tail removal runs frame by frame and causally, in front of a speech recognizer or as offline cleanup. The paper used 128-point Hamming frames with 75% overlap, zero-padded to 256. On top of the STFT it costs one exponential factor per recording and one division per bin per frame.

10 Reverberation in code

A simulated room from section 5, a stand-in dry sound through it, and \(T_{60}\) read back with the \(T_{30}\) fit.

import numpy as np, scipy.signal as sps

fs, t60 = 16000, 0.5
n = np.arange(int(1.2 * t60 * fs))
zeta = 3 * np.log(10) / (t60 * fs)
h = np.random.default_rng(0).standard_normal(n.size) * np.exp(-zeta * n)

dry = np.random.default_rng(1).standard_normal(fs)
wet = sps.fftconvolve(dry, h)

energy = np.cumsum(h[::-1] ** 2)[::-1]
edc_db = 10 * np.log10(energy / energy[0])

i5, i35 = np.argmax(edc_db <= -5), np.argmax(edc_db <= -35)
slope = np.polyfit(n[i5:i35] / fs, edc_db[i5:i35], 1)[0]
t30 = -60 / slope  # the time for a 60 dB fall
  1. 1The tail model at \(T_{60} = 0.5\) s, 16 kHz: Gaussian noise times \(e^{-\zeta n}\), 0.6 s long.
  2. 2A second of white noise stands in for speech; the wet signal is \(x \ast h\).
  3. 3The EDC: square, sum from the end backwards, convert to dB.
  4. 4Fit a line from \(-5\) to \(-35\) dB and turn its slope into the time for 60 dB.

Late-tail removal on that wet signal, with the settings of the walkthrough. The frames are this page’s choice, not the paper’s: 512 samples with a hop of 128 samples, and the split \(T_{d}\) is counted back in frames.

f, t, Y = sps.stft(wet, fs, nperseg=512, noverlap=384)
Py = np.abs(Y) ** 2
td, hop = 0.04, 512 - 384

d = round(td * fs / hop)
delta = 3 * np.log(10) / t60
p_late = np.zeros_like(Py)
p_late[:, d:] = np.exp(-2 * delta * td) * Py[:, :-d]

beta = 0.1
G = np.maximum(1 - p_late / np.maximum(Py, 1e-12), beta)
_, clean = sps.istft(np.sqrt(G) * Y, fs, nperseg=512, noverlap=384)
  1. 1The STFT of the wet signal and its power \(P_{y}\) per bin and frame.
  2. 2The late power: the frame 5 back, times the fade factor \(e^{-2\Delta T_{d}}\).
  3. 3The floored gain is a power gain, so the bins are scaled by its square root before the inverse STFT.

pyroomacoustics (Scheibler et al. 2018) builds rooms with the image method. Its inverse_sabine function turns a target \(T_{60}\) into wall absorption, and a room’s measure_rt60 reads it back from the simulated response. Its default sampling rate is 8 kHz, so set the room to 16 kHz for this page’s settings.
