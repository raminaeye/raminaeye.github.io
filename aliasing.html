Aliasing and Anti-Aliasing Filters

Why fast tones look slow.

Sampling keeps one value at fixed intervals and throws away everything in between. A tone faster than half the sampling rate then leaves exactly the same samples as a slower tone, so it shows up at the wrong frequency, and nothing after the analog-to-digital converter (ADC) can undo that. The cure is to remove those fast tones before they are sampled, with an analog low-pass filter, or to sample fast and filter digitally before slowing the rate down. The analog filter acts continuously on the input; the digital one acts once per kept sample.

1 Aliasing on film

Film takes 24 pictures per second and shows you nothing in between, so your eye joins each spoke to the nearest spoke in the next picture. The wheel in Figure 1 turns a little less than one spoke gap per picture, so the nearest spoke in the next picture sits slightly behind, and the wheel seems to creep backward.

A sampled sine has the same problem. Between two samples a fast sine can turn almost a full cycle, and the dots then trace a slow wave instead. That false, slower tone is called an alias. The line marked Nyquist in Figure 2 sits at half the sampling rate, the limit Section 2 explains.

2 The Nyquist limit

The wheel looked right as long as it turned less than half a spoke gap per picture. For a sine the limit is half a cycle per sample, which puts the highest safe frequency at half the sampling rate \(f_{s}\).

Phone calls are sampled at 8 kHz, speech recognizers take 16 kHz and music CDs use 44.1 kHz, and each keeps only the frequencies below half its rate.

The highest frequency the samples can describe.
\(\displaystyle f_{\text{Nyquist}} = \frac{f_{s}}{2}\)
The Nyquist limit \(f_{\text{Nyquist}}\) is half the sampling rate \(f_{s}\), the number of samples per second, because a tone needs more than two samples per cycle to be told apart from a slower one. Doubling \(f_{s}\) doubles this limit; anything faster folds back below it.
\(\displaystyle f_{\text{max}} < \frac{f_{s}}{2}\)
The limit is strict. A sine exactly at \(f_{s}/2\) can be sampled at its zero crossings and read as all zeros, so \(f_{\text{max}}\), the highest frequency in the signal, must sit strictly below half the rate. It builds on the wheel of Section 1 and is used by the fold rule in Section 3 and by every rate change in Section 5.

Shannon proved this sampling theorem in 1949, building on Nyquist (1928): a signal with nothing above \(f_{s}/2\) is fully pinned down by its samples.

3 Sampling copies the spectrum

Builds on the spectrum (Audio & Signals Basics, section 1)

Why does a fast tone land at a specific slower one? Sampling copies the whole spectrum to every multiple of \(f_{s}\). In Figure 4 each triangle stands for the band of frequencies a signal holds.

The spectrum after sampling.
\(\displaystyle X_{s}(f) = {f_{s} \sum_{k} X(f - k f_{s})}\)
\(X_{s}(f)\) is the spectrum after sampling and \(X(f)\) the continuous signal's spectrum. Sampling multiplies the signal by a row of spikes one sample period apart, which in frequency makes copies: each term \(X(f - k f_{s})\) is \(X\) shifted by \(k f_{s}\), and \(\sum_{k}\) adds one for every whole number \(k\), positive or negative. The factor \(f_{s}\) only scales the height. Where a copy overlaps the original band, the two can no longer be pulled apart, and that overlap is aliasing. It builds on the spectrum and is used by the fold rule and by upsampling in Section 5.2.

The copy that lands between 0 and \(f_{s}/2\) is the one you see. That gives the fold rule. A whine just below the sampling rate, such as an old TV’s, folds to near 0.

Where a tone at frequency \(f\) shows up after sampling.
\(\displaystyle f_{\text{alias}} = \lvert f - k f_{s}\rvert\)
\(f_{\text{alias}}\) is the frequency the samples show. A tone at \(f\) also sits in every copy, at \(f - k f_{s}\), and the samples show whichever copy lands between 0 and \(f_{s}/2\). The whole number \(k\) picks that copy, and the absolute value folds a negative frequency back to positive.
\(\displaystyle f_{\text{alias}} = f_{s} - f\)
This is the case \(k = 1\), for \(f\) between \(f_{s}/2\) and \(f_{s}\). As \(f\) rises past Nyquist the alias falls by the same amount, so a tone just above \(f_{s}/2\) shows up just below it. It builds on the copies card and is used by the tool in Figure 5 and by the filter in Section 4.

Samples of a tone above Nyquist and of its alias; the last line prints True because the samples match.

import numpy as np

def alias_demo(fs, f_tone, n_samples):
    f_alias = abs(f_tone - fs)          # f_s - f
    n = np.arange(n_samples)            # sample index n
    tone = np.cos(2 * np.pi * f_tone * n / fs)
    alias = np.cos(2 * np.pi * f_alias * n / fs)
    print(np.allclose(tone, alias))
  1. 1Puts the tone's alias at \(f_{s} - f\) (eq card in Section 3, line 2).
  2. 2Samples both tones the same number of times and checks that the dots are the same.

Playback rebuilds the one smooth wave below Nyquist through the dots, so a tone past Nyquist plays back as its alias even though every sample was right.

4 Filter before you sample

Builds on the decibel (Audio & Signals Basics, section 1.1)

Take a recording with a wanted signal below Nyquist and an interferer above it. Once the interferer has folded, no digital filter can tell its alias from a real signal at that frequency. So an analog low-pass anti-aliasing filter in front of the ADC removes what lies above Nyquist before sampling.

analog signal low-pass filter ADC digital samples

\(\lvert H(f)\rvert\) is how much of frequency \(f\) a filter lets through; How FIR and IIR Filters Shape Magnitude and Phase builds it from scratch. The Butterworth low-pass stays as flat as possible below its cutoff \(f_{c}\). Its order \(p\) counts its poles, the factors in the bottom of its response, and each pole adds 20 dB per decade (ten times the frequency) of fall past the cutoff.

How much power a Butterworth low-pass of order \(p\) lets through.
\(\displaystyle \lvert H(f)\rvert^{2} = {\frac{1}{1 + \left(\frac{f}{f_{c}}\right)^{2p}}}\)
The ratio \(f/f_{c}\) measures frequency in cutoffs. At the cutoff, \(f = f_{c}\), exactly half the power passes. Below the cutoff the ratio raised to \(2p\) stays near 0 and almost everything passes; above it the term grows fast, and a higher order \(p\) makes the fall steeper, \(20p\) dB per decade. It builds on the decibel and on \(\lvert H(f)\rvert\), and is used by Figure 7, Figure 8 and the digital low-pass in Section 5.

A cutoff right at Nyquist is only \(3\,\text{dB}\) down there, so an interferer just above Nyquist still gets through and folds.

The Butterworth card in decibels for four cutoff and order pairs, plus the wanted band; a lower cutoff and a higher order both push the interferer down.

import numpy as np

def butter_db(f, f_c, p):
    return -10 * np.log10(1 + (f / f_c) ** (2 * p))

def evaluate(f_wanted, f_int, cases):
    wanted = butter_db(f_wanted, *cases[0])  # the wanted band
    interferer = [butter_db(f_int, f_c, p) for f_c, p in cases]
    return wanted, interferer
  1. 1Gain in dB of a Butterworth low-pass (eq card in Section 4, line 1).
  2. 2The wanted band passes almost untouched.
  3. 3The interferer for each cutoff and order pair, the points of Figure 7.

Higher order has a price. The anti-aliasing Butterworth is an analog circuit, and its group delay, how long each frequency is held back, changes with frequency, more so at higher order. Its digital copy is an IIR filter, one that feeds its own output back, and it also delays frequencies unevenly. How FIR and IIR Filters Shape Magnitude and Phase shows why that bends a waveform.

5 Changing the sampling rate

Section 4 filtered the signal before the converter. Once audio is digital, its rate often still has to change, to store fewer samples or to feed a model built for one rate. Two recordings must also share a rate before they can be compared or matched (Anushiravani 2016).

5.1 Sample fast, then slow down in software

A steep analog filter is expensive, and its group delay grows more uneven as it gets steeper. Sampling much faster than needed lets a gentle analog filter do the job; a digital low-pass then removes the rest, and the downsampling factor \(Q\) says how many samples go in for each one kept. That low-pass followed by keeping one sample in \(Q\) is called decimation.

Figure 11 runs it on a rising tone, keeping one sample in four.

The sampling rate after keeping one sample in \(Q\).
\(\displaystyle f_{s,\text{new}} = \frac{f_{s}}{Q}\)
\(f_{s,\text{new}}\) is the rate after. Keeping one sample in \(Q\) divides the rate by \(Q\), and with it the Nyquist limit, which drops to \(f_{s,\text{new}}/2\). Anything between the new and the old limit would fold, so the digital low-pass removes it first. It builds on the Nyquist limit of Section 2 and the low-pass of Section 4, and is used by the any-ratio card in Section 5.3.

A spectrogram stacks short spectra side by side over time, with frequency going up and brightness for strength.

5.2 Speeding up inserts zeros, then fills them

Whisper, OpenAI’s speech recognizer, resamples all audio to 16,000 Hz before it computes its spectrogram features (Radford et al. 2022, section 2.2). An 8 kHz phone recording therefore has to be upsampled by a whole-number factor \(L\). Upsampling puts \(L - 1\) zeros between neighboring samples. The zeros create an image, a mirror copy of each tone, because the spectrum copy centered on the old rate (Section 3) now lies inside the new band.

Nothing folds, so upsampling causes no aliasing; only the image has to be removed.

Upsampling by a whole number \(L\), then filling the zeros.
\(\displaystyle x_{\text{up}}[L n] = x[n]\)
\(x\) is the original signal and \(x_{\text{up}}\) the signal at the new rate \(L f_{s}\). Each old sample \(x[n]\) lands at position \(L n\), and the \(L - 1\) positions between hold 0. In frequency this leaves the spectrum plus its image.
\(\displaystyle y[n] = {L \cdot (h \ast x_{\text{up}})[n]}\)
\(y\) is the upsampled signal, \(h\) a low-pass with its cutoff at the old Nyquist \(f_{s}/2\), and \(\ast\) is convolution, where each output sample adds up the nearby input samples, each weighted by \(h\) (taught in full in LTI Systems and Convolution, section 5). Removing the image fills each zero with a value between its neighbors, and the factor \(L\) restores the level, since only one sample in \(L\) carried signal. It builds on the copies of Section 3 and is used by Section 5.3.

SciPy’s resample_poly does both steps.

5.3 Resampling by any ratio

Music at 44.1 kHz going into that 16 kHz recognizer is not a whole-number step either way. The way through is to upsample by one whole number, low-pass once, and keep one sample in another.

The rate after upsampling by \(\text{up}\) and keeping one sample in \(\text{down}\).
\(\displaystyle f_{s,\text{new}} = {f_{s} \cdot \frac{\text{up}}{\text{down}}}\)
\(\text{up}\) and \(\text{down}\) are whole numbers with no common factor, the ratio of the new rate to the old one in lowest terms. The rate in between is \(f_{s} \cdot \text{up}\). One low-pass at the lower of the two Nyquist limits removes the images of the zeros and anything that would fold when one sample in \(\text{down}\) is kept. It builds on Section 5.1 and Section 5.2 and is used by equalization matching (Power Spectral Density, section 5).

6 In practice

Anti-aliasing sits in two places, an analog low-pass right before the ADC and a digital low-pass before every downsampling step in software.

A rising tone downsampled with SciPy, once by slicing (bad) and twice with a low-pass first (good, good2), then a sweep taken from 44.1 kHz to 16 kHz.

import numpy as np
from scipy import signal

def resampling_demo(fs, factor, dur_s, f_top, f_top_44):
    t = np.arange(dur_s * fs) / fs
    sweep = signal.chirp(t, f0=0, t1=dur_s, f1=f_top)

    bad = sweep[::factor]                     # no filter, folds
    good = signal.decimate(sweep, factor)
    good2 = signal.resample_poly(sweep, up=1, down=factor)

    x44 = signal.chirp(np.arange(dur_s * 44100) / 44100, f0=0, t1=dur_s, f1=f_top_44)
    x16 = signal.resample_poly(x44, 160, 441)  # one low-pass at 8 kHz
  1. 1Makes a rising sweep.
  2. 2Keeps one sample in factor with no filter, so the sweep folds (eq card in Section 5.1).
  3. 3Low-passes first, then keeps one sample in factor, so nothing folds.
  4. 4Goes from 44.1 kHz to 16 kHz with up 160, one low-pass and down 441 (eq card in Section 5.3).

6.1 Gotchas

  • Zero phase runs the filter forward and then backward over the whole signal, so it needs samples that have not arrived yet; on a live stream set zero_phase=False.
