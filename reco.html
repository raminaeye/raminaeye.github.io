Recommender Systems

People who liked this.

What does "people who liked this also liked that" actually mean? It means taste has a shape. Put every movie as a point in user space: two movies are close if the same users rated them the same way. Or put every movie as a point in genre space: two movies are close if they share genres. Either way, "recommend" becomes "find the nearest neighbors." That is all a recommender is: geometry plus a similarity score.

The notebook tries three geometries on the MovieLens data: similarity from ratings, similarity from genres, and learned embeddings. They do not always agree, and the disagreements are the interesting part.

Three ways to say similar

Way one: similar raters. Take the user-movie rating matrix, one row per user, one column per movie, zeros where nobody rated. Take two movie columns and compute their cosine similarity. If the same users gave both movies high ratings, the columns point the same way and the cosine is near 1. On the notebook's data, Toy Story's closest match this way is Toy Story 2 at 0.573.

One honest footnote: the notebook calls this recommender "content-based," but look at what it actually does. No content is involved, just who rated what. It is collaborative filtering wearing a content-based costume.

Way two: similar content. Forget the ratings. Turn each movie's genre list into a binary vector (1 if the movie has the genre, 0 if not) and compute cosine similarity between those. Toy Story and Toy Story 2 share all five genres, so their similarity is exactly 1. This one is the real content-based method.

$$ \cos(a, b) = \frac{a \cdot b}{\|a\| \cdot \|b\|} $$

Here a and b are the two movie vectors being compared, rating columns for rating-cosine or binary genre vectors for genre-cosine, a · b is the dot product, the term Learn the taste vectors uses for affinity, and ||a|| is the length of a vector, the square root of the sum of its squared entries. When the same users rated both movies highly, the columns point the same way and the cosine is near 1, and it is exactly 1 when two movies share all their genres.

Two movies are close if the same users rated them the same way, and, in genre space, two movies are close if they share genres. Each movie is one vector, the cosine is set by the angle between the two vectors, a small angle means similar, and the two methods differ only in what the vector entries are: ratings in one case, genre flags in the other. If the same users gave both movies high ratings, the columns point the same way and the cosine is near 1.

Way three: learn the geometry. Instead of hand-picking the vectors, learn them. Give every user and every movie a 20-number embedding, plus a bias each, and train the whole thing to predict ratings with gradient descent. The prediction for a user and a movie is a dot product plus biases.

The rating table is mostly empty

Here is the actual problem. A real ratings table has thousands of users and thousands of movies, and each user rated a handful. Almost every cell is empty. "Recommend" means guessing those empty cells: what would this user rate that movie?

Similarity alone struggles here. Cosine needs two movies rated by the same users, and in a mostly-empty table two movies often share no raters at all. Then the comparison says nothing. What you want is a model that learns from the ratings that exist and generalizes to the ones that do not.

Learn the taste vectors

Matrix factorization gives every user a short taste vector and every movie a short vibe vector. The predicted rating is the dot product: when a user's taste lines up with a movie's vibe, the prediction is high.

Two corrections on top: a user bias, because some people rate everything high, and an item bias, because some movies are just widely loved.

In the letters of the one line that runs this whole section: pu is user u's taste vector, qi is movie i's vibe vector, bu is that user's bias, bi is that movie's bias, and r̂ui is the predicted rating of user u for movie i.

$$\hat{r}_{ui} = p_u \cdot q_i + b_u + b_i$$

Nothing here is hand-picked. Gradient descent adjusts all the vectors and biases to fit the ratings you have, minimizing prediction error. This is exactly the notebook's forward():

def forward(self, user_ids, item_ids):
    user_embeds = self.user_embed(user_ids)
    item_embeds = self.item_embed(item_ids)
    user_bias = self.user_bias(user_ids)
    item_bias = self.item_bias(item_ids)
    dot_product = (user_embeds * item_embeds).sum(dim=1)
    return dot_product + user_bias.squeeze() + item_bias.squeeze()
What this shows
  • The point: the sparse ratings table factors into two thin tables: one short row per user, one short row per movie. Multiplying them fills every empty cell with a prediction.
  • The thin tables are the whole model: a short row per user and movie (4 here, 20 in the notebook) stands in for the full table.

Comparing users directly stops working when the table is empty. The fix that stuck: learn short vectors for users and movies, and predict ratings with dot products (Koren et al.).

Factorization fills the blanks

The ratings table is mostly blank: each user rated only a handful of the movies in it. Factorization fills a blank by prediction, not by lookup. Once training has given every user a taste vector and every movie a vibe vector, the entry for a pair that never met is the one line from Learn the taste vectors applied to that pair: dot the two vectors, add the two biases. Recommending is then just ranking the blanks: predict the unrated movies for one user and put the highest predictions first.

The hybrid adds the content back

The learned model and the genre similarity capture different things, so the notebook combines them: the same embeddings, plus the genre-cosine score as an extra signal. Learned taste plus stated content.

The scoreboard needs a ruler. Square each prediction error so big misses hurt more than small ones, average over all test ratings, then take the square root to get back to rating units. That is RMSE. An RMSE of 2.54 means the typical prediction misses by about two and a half stars. Lower is better.

The setup: MovieLens small-latest, movies.csv and ratings.csv merged on movieId, pivoted to a user-by-movie matrix with missing ratings filled as 0. Collaborative filtering: embedding dim 20, Adam at lr 0.001, 50 epochs, MSE loss. Hybrid: same embeddings plus the genre cosine similarity, 20 epochs. I reran the notebook's training setup to check the two RMSE numbers.

Collaborative filtering alone gets test RMSE 2.54. The hybrid gets 2.47. The content signal helps, a little.

When it helps and when it does not

Reach for it when you have plenty of ratings per user and per movie. The vectors are only as good as the ratings that trained them.

Keep in mind the cold start: a new user or a new movie has no ratings, so it has no vector, and the model has nothing to say. Genre similarity still works on day one: a new movie arrives with its genres attached.

And the item bias is honest about a real skew: widely loved movies get recommended to everyone. If you want surprising picks instead of popular ones, the raw prediction needs re-ranking.

Taste has a shape, and the ratings are enough to learn it: two short vectors, one dot product, two biases.
