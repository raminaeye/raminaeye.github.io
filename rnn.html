Recurrent Neural Networks: RNN, GRU, and LSTM

Order matters

We all know what feedforward networks are. They assume a fixed-size input. It takes an input, multiplies it with some weights, and applies non-linearities (functions that bend their input instead of just scaling it in a straight line, which is what lets a network learn curved and complicated patterns). The order of the input doesn't matter. This is a problem for input sequences, like words in a sentence, stock prices over days, or audio samples, where order actually matters. Different sentences can have different lengths, which would require padding, adding dummy steps to the short sequences so every sequence reaches the same length, or truncating. A feedforward network flattens the whole sequence into one fixed-size input, and the temporal structure, which step came when, is lost.

With recurrence, instead of processing all input at once, you process one input at a time and carry forward some memory.

The RNN update: one equation, reused every timestep

The current hidden state depends on the previous hidden state. That is what gives it memory: at each step the cell mixes today's input with yesterday's memory, squashes the mix, and carries it forward.

today's memory is a squashed mix of today's input and yesterday's memory. What today shouts and what still echoes from yesterday are added together, and a squashing function squeezes the total into the range from minus one to one. That squeeze is the whole tragedy of the vanilla RNN: it keeps the numbers sane, and it slowly erases the past. One more matrix turns the hidden state into the output prediction. Mix and squash, mix and squash: every step fades the past a little, and that washout is exactly what the gates were invented to stop.

Mix the incoming word with the last step's memory, then squash: the new cell state carries both forward. xt, the word entering, and ht-1, memory from the last cell, are the two sides of the mix, and ht, the new cell state, is what comes out. W, U, the learned weights, scale each side, tanh, the squasher, squeezes the total, and · means times: scaling.

The equation's W is the W in the dataflow figure's × W box (it multiplies xt), and the equation's U is the U in the figure's × U box (it multiplies ht-1). A small bias (a learned offset, one per neuron, added so the result can shift even when the inputs are zero) joins the mix, and one more matrix turns the hidden state into the output prediction; neither appears in this equation line.

Backpropagation through time unrolls the loop

Unlike backpropagation, where gradients flow backward through the network and nudge each weight by how much it contributed to the error, flowing layer by layer, in an RNN we have time as well as depth. This is called backpropagation through time (BPTT).

Say the loss is cross-entropy (the standard loss for picking the right class, which grows when the model gives the correct answer little probability), summed over all timesteps.

When we unroll the RNN for T timesteps, it looks like a deep forward network of depth T, where each layer reuses the same weights (the input, recurrent, and output weight matrices). During backprop, gradients flow through the sequence of hidden states. Since parameters are shared, their gradients accumulate across timesteps.

The trouble comes from the recurrent connection. Since each hidden state depends on the previous one, this expands into a long chain rule across time: the chain rule is the rule that a gradient through nested steps is the product of the per step derivatives, so the gradient picks up one multiplication at every timestep.

  • Vanishing gradients. The gradient from the loss has to travel back through every timestep, and each step multiplies it by a tanh derivative, which is at most 1. Multiply a number below 1 by itself T times and it collapses: after a few dozen steps the signal arriving from step T back at step 1 is a whisper. I think of it as shouting down a long hallway where every door eats half the sound. The model simply never learns dependencies longer than that hallway.
  • Exploding gradients. The same chain can run in reverse. If the recurrent weights are large, each timestep multiplies the gradient instead of shrinking it, and the updates blow up to garbage. Same mechanism, opposite disease. This one is at least loud about it: you see NaNs (not a number: the invalid value a calculation produces when the numbers blow up, and once a gradient or the loss becomes one, every update it touches turns into the same invalid value instead of a usable number), not silence.
The model, stated plainly: a gradient multiplied at every timestep grows or shrinks exponentially, straight on a log scale (a scale where equal steps stand for multiplying by the same factor rather than adding the same amount, so steady multiplying draws as a straight line).
gradient size after t steps = wt, and after all T steps = wT
Here w is the per-step multiplier, the weight scale each timestep multiplies the gradient by. In the line, t counts the steps taken so far, T counts all the timesteps in the sequence, and the small raised number is the power: how many times w is multiplied by itself. The gradient magnitude after t steps is w raised to the power t, and after all T steps it is w raised to the power T. That repeated multiplication is the mechanism behind everything after this card: below 1, the signal from early steps shrinks until those steps have nothing left to learn from, and above 1 it grows until the updates break, which is why gates and clipping follow.

The gradient magnitude after t steps is w raised to the power t, and after all T steps it is w raised to the power T. Weight scale w: sets the multiplier per step, 0.50 to 1.50. Timesteps T: sets how far back the dot goes, 1 to 50. T is the number of timesteps, and multiply a number below 1 by itself T times and it collapses. A magnitude above 1e2 (100) counts as exploded, a magnitude below 1e-2 (0.01) counts as vanished, and the range between the two counts as safe.

This is the simplified scalar picture. Real Jacobians (each a matrix of partial derivatives, where a partial derivative measures how much one output nudges when a single input nudges and the rest stay fixed) are matrices, not one number, but the exponential dependence on T is the whole story.

The exploded end has a standard fix, taught in full in the clipping section that follows this tool.

Why gradients vanish (or explode)

exploded (>1e2) safe vanished (<1e-2)

Multiply by slightly-less-than-1 at every step for the default 28 steps and only about a twentieth is left for the first timestep to learn from; that is why the gates exist.

How to play

  • The point: A gradient multiplied by slightly-less-than-1 at every step shrinks to nothing over a long sequence, and slightly-more-than-1 blows it up. That is vanishing vs exploding gradients.
  • What you'll see: A gradient multiplied at every timestep grows or shrinks exponentially, straight on a log scale: a multiplier below 1 vanishes it, and a multiplier above 1 explodes it.
  • Timesteps T: Sets how far back the dot goes, 1 to 50. With the weight scale at 0.90, raising T lands the dot deeper in the vanished zone.
  • Weight scale w: Sets the multiplier per step, 0.50 to 1.50. Pushing it past 1.00 flips the curve from diving into the vanished band to climbing into the exploded band.

Clip exploding gradients: same direction, sane size

You can deal with exploding gradients using gradient clipping. Measure the length of the whole gradient vector, all the parameter gradients flattened into one big vector. If that length is bigger than a threshold, scale every component down proportionally until the length lands exactly on that threshold. Same direction, sane size: a step that wanted to be enormous becomes a step you can actually take.

Now, the main issue with RNNs was really vanishing gradients, which affects long-term memory. The old memory gets mixed in and there's no control over how information passes to the future; the information washes out. The RNN forgets context.

Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU)

The GRU introduces gates to control what information to keep and what to forget.

  • An update gate decides: Do I keep the old memory or replace it with new information?
  • A reset gate decides: Do I ignore the old memory when making new memory, or should I use it?

For example, consider "Ramin wrote the best tutorial, he did it again!". A vanilla RNN might overwrite "Ramin" and forget who "he" was. A GRU's update gate would say "don't overwrite Ramin, hold on to that memory." If the update gate decides to just keep the old memory, the hidden state can flow unchanged for many steps. That means gradients can also flow back easily, solving the vanishing gradients problem.

Conceptually, the hidden state update is a blend of the old memory and the new candidate, steered by the update gate. If the gate is closed, the memory is copied directly from one step to the next. No multiplication, no tanh, just an identity connection. During backprop, the gradients flow through this same path, unchanged. This is a shortcut path or linear highway for memory.

Gates decide what to keep and what to forget

There are 2 gates, both are little feedforward networks with a sigmoid activation, so their outputs are between 0 and 1.

  • Update gate: keep the old hidden state or update it? The sigmoid squashes the result to a number between 0 and 1, interpreted as a switch. 0 means keep old memory, 1 means overwrite with new memory.
  • Reset gate: when computing new candidate memory, how much of the old memory should I ignore?
  • Candidate hidden state: a proposal for new memory. The reset gate decides how much of the previous state is allowed in. If the reset gate is zero, the candidate ignores past memory and depends only on the input.

Finally, one more matrix turns the hidden state into the output prediction.

The update gate asks: of the past, how much do I throw away? The reset gate asks: when I imagine new candidate memory, how much of the past do I let into the room? The candidate is the proposal, and the blend closes the deal. It is a weighted average: fully closed keeps everything, fully open replaces everything, and the network learns the gate setting from data, per step, per neuron. Here is that blend as dataflow:

The GRU in four lines: two gates decide what to keep and what to forget, a candidate memory is proposed, and the update gate blends old and new. Where the equations below use the odot symbol, ⊙, read it as element-wise multiplication, multiplying matching entries one by one rather than mixing entries together.

The GRU in four lines, first the two gates: they decide what to keep and what to forget.
zt = σ(Wz·xt + Uz·ht-1 + bz)
The update gate decides: how much of the new candidate to let in versus keeping the old memory. zt is built from xt, the input, scaled by Wz, that gate's input weight, and ht-1, the old memory, scaled by Uz, that gate's old-memory weight, plus bz, the gate's bias, all squashed by σ, the gate squasher, so it lands between 0 and 1. W, U are the learned weights, and · means times: scaling.
rt = σ(Wr·xt + Ur·ht-1 + br)
The reset gate decides: how much of the old memory the candidate gets to see. rt is built the same way, from the input xt scaled by Wr and the old memory ht-1 scaled by Ur, plus its bias br, with σ, the gate squasher: outputs 0 to 1.
Then the candidate the gates have been preparing, and the blend that decides what survives.
h̃t = tanh(Wh·xt + Uh·(rt ⊙ ht-1) + bh)
Build the candidate: mix the incoming word with the reset-filtered old memory, then squash. h̃t mixes xt scaled by Wh with rt ⊙ ht-1, the reset gate element-wise mixing the old memory, scaled by Uh, so the old memory enters only after the reset gate filters it. ⊙ is element-wise mix, tanh squashes to between -1 and 1, and bh, the last term inside the tanh, is the candidate's bias.
ht = (1−zt)·ht-1 + zt·h̃t
Blend old and new: the update gate picks the mix between keeping the old memory and taking the candidate. ht adds the old memory ht-1 scaled by (1 - zt), the keep share, to the candidate h̃t scaled by zt, the replace share. zt = 0 keeps everything, zt = 1 replaces everything: the update gate is the blend knob.

In the GRUCell code in “One GRU step, in code”, each line here is one line there: the two sigmoid lines build zt and rt (the comments name them update gate and reset gate), the tanh line builds the candidate, and the last line is the blend.

One GRU step, in code

The whole cell is just those four equations. Here it is, one step: 


import torch
import torch.nn as nn

class GRUCell(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, input_dim, hidden_dim):
        super().__init__()
        self.W_z = nn.Linear(input_dim, hidden_dim)
        self.U_z = nn.Linear(hidden_dim, hidden_dim, bias=False)
        self.W_r = nn.Linear(input_dim, hidden_dim)
        self.U_r = nn.Linear(hidden_dim, hidden_dim, bias=False)
        self.W_h = nn.Linear(input_dim, hidden_dim)
        self.U_h = nn.Linear(hidden_dim, hidden_dim, bias=False)

    def forward(self, x_t, h_prev):
        z_t = torch.sigmoid(self.W_z(x_t) + self.U_z(h_prev))  # update gate
        r_t = torch.sigmoid(self.W_r(x_t) + self.U_r(h_prev))  # reset gate
        h_tilde = torch.tanh(self.W_h(x_t) + self.U_h(r_t * h_prev))  # candidate
        h_t = (1 - z_t) * h_prev + z_t * h_tilde  # the blend
        return h_t

Even though the GRU doesn't have a dedicated cell state (a separate memory stream that runs alongside the hidden state rather than being the hidden state itself), it can still preserve long-term memory because the update gate can create identity connections across time steps.

  • Shut: the vault. The update gate is shut: the old memory flows straight through and the candidate is pinched off, so the memory survives unchanged.
  • Open: the reflex. The update gate is wide open: the candidate floods through and overwrites the old memory.
  • Halfway: the blend. The update gate sits halfway: both streams feed the output, part old memory and part new candidate.
  • The reset gate's job. The reset gate pinches the old-memory branch feeding the candidate mixer: starved of the past, the candidate thins out.

This is the paper that introduced the GRU (Cho et al., EMNLP 2014). Below is its real Figure 2, the hidden-unit diagram, with its original caption.

One quirk worth knowing: the paper writes the blend exactly the way this page does, with (1 minus z) weighting the old state and z weighting the new candidate, so fully open replaces the old memory. The flipped form, z weighting the old state, is the later library convention (PyTorch's GRU uses it). Same idea, mirrored knob. If a library's z feels backwards next to the paper, that is why.

Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM)

LSTM (Long Short-Term Memory) separates memory into two parts and uses three gates to carefully control what flows into, out of, and stays within these memories.

The LSTM keeps two books instead of one. The cell state is the long ledger: it runs straight through the cell with no nonlinearity squashing it, so information (and, during backprop, gradients) can ride it across hundreds of steps almost untouched. That unbroken line is the entire trick. The hidden state is the note I hand you right now: the short-term read, what the network is thinking at this step, built from the ledger but filtered for immediate use.

Three gates guard the ledger, and each one is a question the network asks every step:

  1. Forget gate: Do I still need this? It looks at the old ledger and erases the entries that stopped mattering. Ruthless, but somebody has to take out the trash.
  2. Input gate: Is this worth writing down? It decides how much of the new candidate actually gets added to the ledger. Not every thought deserves ink.
  3. Output gate: What do I show right now? It filters the ledger down into the hidden state, the short-term read the rest of the network gets to see.

The LSTM update

The new long-term memory is formed by keeping some of the old memory and adding some new information.

The forget gate decides what fraction of yesterday's balance survives; the input gate decides how much of today's deposit, the candidate, gets added. Notice what is missing: there is no squashing nonlinearity on this line. The cell state is only ever scaled and added, never squashed, which is why gradients walk straight back down the highway instead of vanishing.

The short-term working memory is a filtered view of the long-term memory: the output gate decides how much of the (squashed) balance to show the world right now. Each gate is a mix of the input and the previous hidden state, passed through a sigmoid function. Here is the ledger as dataflow:

The LSTM in six lines: three gates guard a cell state: forget the stale, add the fresh, and output a filtered read.

The LSTM in six lines, first the two gates that guard the cell: forget the stale, let in the fresh.
ft = σ(Wf·xt + Uf·ht-1 + bf)
The forget gate: decides how much of the old cell state to keep versus drain away. ft is built from xt, the input, scaled by Wf, the forget gate's own input weight, and ht-1, the hidden state from the previous step, scaled by Uf, its own hidden-state weight, plus bf, the forget gate's bias, squashed by σ to between 0 and 1, the gatekeeper. W, U are the learned weights, and · means times: scaling.
it = σ(Wi·xt + Ui·ht-1 + bi)
The input gate: decides how much of the new candidate to pour into the cell. it is built from the input xt scaled by Wi and the hidden state from the previous step, ht-1, scaled by Ui, plus bi, the input gate's bias, all squashed by σ to between 0 and 1.
Next the candidate and the cell update itself: the two streams merging.
C̃t = tanh(Wc·xt + Uc·ht-1 + bc)
The candidate cell: fresh information mixed from the word and the last hidden state, squashed. C̃t mixes the input xt, scaled by Wc, with ht-1, scaled by Uc, plus bc, the candidate's bias, and tanh squashes the mix to between -1 and 1.
Ct = ft ⊙ Ct-1 + it ⊙ C̃t
Update the cell: keep what the forget gate saved, add what the input gate let in. Ct is ft element-wise mixing Ct-1, the cell state's previous value, plus it element-wise mixing C̃t. Here ft is the forget gate's share and it is the input gate's share, the two gates built in the card above. These are the two streams from the dataflow figure: the forget gate scales the old balance, the input gate scales the deposit, and they add. No tanh on this line: the cell state is only scaled and added, which is why the cell state is a gradient highway.
Last the output gate and the filtered read it reveals.
ot = σ(Wo·xt + Uo·ht-1 + bo)
The output gate: decides how much of the cell state to reveal as the hidden state. ot is built from the input xt scaled by Wo and ht-1, the hidden state from the previous step, scaled by Uo, plus bo, the output gate's bias, squashed by σ to between 0 and 1.
ht = ot ⊙ tanh(Ct)
The new hidden state: the squashed cell, filtered by the output gate. Ct is the cell state built in the update line above, and ⊙ is element-wise mixing, the output gate scaling the squashed cell entry by entry. The cell state is squashed by tanh to between -1 and 1, then the output gate lets through only its share: it picks how much of the squashed cell state to show as the hidden state ht.

These six lines are the gates' doing alone, with the weights set aside. A real LSTM learns the candidate through its own weight matrices.

  • The input pours in. The input gate is open: drops pour into the tank and the level rises.
  • Sealed: the vault. The tank is sealed like a vault: nothing drains out, so the level never drops, step after step. That flat line is the whole trick.
  • The drain: the level falls. The gate keeps nothing, so the level falls and keeps falling. Erasure is permanent.
  • The output reveals. The output gate opens its shutter: the hidden state flows out, as much as the shutter lets through.

The forget gate stays open (nothing leaks out), so the write gate does all the routing alone. That gate is the LSTM's input gate. A real network learns both schedules from data.

The setup: six gated pipes pouring into a memory tank; the important signal and the loud distractor streams compete, alongside quiet steps. Gating is a routing policy you choose. Open the valve on the important signal, close it on the loud distractor, and the tank remembers exactly what you let in.

At rest: the tank starts empty with the verdict pending. The three presets are Remember everything, Block the distractor, and Amnesia. Remember everything opens all six valves (verdict: remembers the distractor); Block the distractor opens every valve except the distractor's (verdict flips to the important signal); Amnesia closes all six (the network remembers nothing).

With all gates open the loudest input wins.

The forget gate was added later

This is the paper that started all of this (Hochreiter and Schmidhuber, 1997): a memory cell with a self-loop of weight exactly 1.0, so the error can cycle forever without shrinking, plus gates that open and close access to it. Spot what is missing: there is no forget gate in this figure. The 1997 LSTM had only input and output gates; the forget gate arrived three years later, from Gers et al. ("Learning to Forget," 2000). The LSTM on this page is the post-2000 version.

Long sequences expose the vanilla RNN

One spike feeds a plain RNN, a GRU, and an LSTM. The RNN's echo decays to flat, the gated lanes hold it, and holding costs 3x to 4x the compute. The RNN trace flattens by the last step while the gated traces stay up.

In practice you never write these cells by hand. PyTorch has them built in, and the call is boring in the best way: 


# A 2-layer LSTM; batch_first matches the (batch, seq, features) convention
lstm = nn.LSTM(input_size=28, hidden_size=128, num_layers=2, batch_first=True)
output, (hn, cn) = lstm(x)  # output: every step of the last layer; hn, cn: final states per layer
# GRU is the same minus the cell state: output, hn = gru(x)

So what does gating actually buy you? The classic stress test is sequential MNIST: feed a 28x28 digit in one row at a time, so the model must remember early rows for 28 steps before it gets to classify. Here is how the three architectures compare:

GRUs typically achieve similar results to LSTMs with fewer parameters: no separate cell state, two gates instead of three. If you are not sure which to pick, start with a GRU.

Illustrative fixed weights, scalar state (a single number, not a vector), both starting at zero. The GRU hold is input-dependent: feed a different spike and it holds a different value. Costs are relative and schematic (gate-count framing), not measured.

How to play

  • The point: one spike, three memories: only the gated lanes are still holding it at the last step.
  • What you'll see: A plain RNN forgets one spike, GRU and LSTM hold it, and holding it costs 3x to 4x the compute.

What goes wrong when you actually train one

  • Vanishing gradients are real. A vanilla RNN on long sequences is a bad default. If your sequence is longer than a few dozen steps, reach for a GRU or LSTM first.
  • Exploding gradients need clipping. Even gated models can blow up. Gradient clipping (above) is cheap insurance; a threshold around 1.0 is a common starting point.
  • Long sequences cost memory. BPTT stores activations, the per step values the network produced, its hidden states and outputs, for every timestep. For very long sequences, use truncated BPTT: cut the sequence into chunks, carry the hidden state forward between chunks, and only backprop within each chunk.
  • Don't put dropout inside the recurrence. Dropout is randomly zeroing some activations during training, so the network cannot lean on any single one. Apply dropout between stacked layers, not across timesteps within a cell. PyTorch's built-in modules do this correctly with one fixed mask per layer per forward pass.
  • When do RNNs still make sense? Transformers won the long-sequence game because attention sees everything at once. But RNNs are still the right call for streaming data where you must decide now with constant memory: real-time sensor streams, on-device keyword spotting, low-latency control. If the input arrives one step at a time and you cannot wait for the full sequence, recurrence is the natural fit.

Gating fixed the memory problem, but RNNs still read one step at a time, which makes training slow on long sequences. The next idea drops recurrence entirely: let every position look directly at every other position. That is Transformers.
