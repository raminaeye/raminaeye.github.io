Gradient Descent

The Loss Landscape

You have seen what a loss function computes for one prediction. Now zoom out. The loss over all the weights is a landscape, and training is a hike through it in the dark. Gradient descent only feels the slope under its feet. Everything painful about training comes from the shape of the ground.

Here is the toy landscape:

A gentle bowl with two pits dug into it: a deep pit (the global minimum, marked gold) and a shallow pit (a local minimum, marked orange). Plain gradient descent cannot tell them apart; it just walks downhill from wherever it starts. Each start and learning rate gives a different hike: the red trail is the hike, the curve is the loss over time.

  1. Stand: start where you are.
  2. Read the slope: feel the slope where you stand.
  3. Step downhill: move against that slope. Flat slope, done.
The whole walk in one line: stand, read the slope, step downhill.
θ ← θ − α·∇L(θ)
Read it as one sentence: where you stand, θ, gets replaced by where you stand minus the step size α times the slope ∇L(θ), the loss where you stand read as a slope. The minus is the downhill part: I move against that slope. Where you start and how big you step decide which pit you end up in.

How to play

  • The point: The start and the step size are the only choices; everything after is slope-following, so the pit you get is the one your start picked.
  • What you'll see: Gradient descent feels only the local slope, so its start decides the pit it finds, a small learning rate never arrives, and a large one diverges.

Optimizers: how you walk downhill

In The Loss Landscape, you walked the hill yourself: one start, one learning rate, one red trail winding down. In a real network nobody hand-walks. The optimizer does the stepping, and the stepping strategy is half the game. So here is the same idea as a race: one hill, one start, three walkers.

SGD. Stochastic gradient descent, from Robbins et al. (1951), "A Stochastic Approximation Method", cited in the References below: the gradient is estimated on a sampled subset rather than the full dataset, and the step is the update rule from The Loss Landscape applied to that sampled gradient. The terms θ, α, and ∇L are the ones already explained there.

Adam. From Kingma et al. (2015), "Adam: A Method for Stochastic Optimization", cited in the References below: Adam keeps two running averages (the mean gradient and the mean squared gradient), corrects both for their zero start, and steps by the corrected mean divided by the square root of the corrected squared mean, so each axis gets its own step size.

Adam's two running averages: the mean gradient and the mean squared gradient.
mt = β1mt−1+(1−β1)gt
The first running average, the mean gradient: the new average mt keeps the β1 share of the old average and mixes in the 1−β1 share of today's gradient gt. The rate β1 is the memory rate, how much of the average carries over.
vt = β2vt−1+(1−β2)gt2
The second running average, the mean squared gradient: vt keeps the β2 share of its old average and mixes in the 1−β2 share of today's gradient squared, so it tracks the size of the gradient on each axis. That is what lets each axis get its own step size.

Next, both averages get corrected for their zero start:

The bias corrections: both averages, corrected for their zero start.
m̂t = mt1−β1t
The corrected mean gradient: m̂t is mt divided by 1−β1t, where β1t is the memory rate β1 raised to the power of the step count t. The hat marks the bias correction for the zero start: the average begins at zero, so its early values are dragged toward zero, and the division inflates the mean back to a realistic value early on.
v̂t = vt1−β2t
The same correction for the squared average: v̂t is vt divided by 1−β2t, with β2t the second memory rate raised to the step count. The hat marks the bias correction for the zero start: the squared average begins at zero too, so this divides out the startup drag, and as the step count grows, the correction goes away.

Then the step itself:

Adam's step: the corrected mean, paced per axis.
θ ← θ − α·m̂t√v̂t+ε
The weights θ get replaced by themselves minus the step: the step size α times the corrected mean m̂t divided by the square root of the corrected squared mean v̂t, with ε added after the root, a tiny constant that keeps the division safe. So each axis gets its own step size.

The constants from here on: MU = 0.9, the momentum μ, a racer's velocity keeps 0.9 of itself and adds the new gradient each step; B1 = 0.9 and B2 = 0.999, the β1 and β2 memory rates above; and EPS = 1e-8 (0.00000001), the tiny constant above.

On a valley that drops 25 times faster on one axis, the expected behaviors are these: SGD zigzags, momentum overshoots (its velocity keeps 0.9 of itself and adds the new gradient each step), Adam adapts each axis and heads straight in.

The race valley, drawn from the race's own valley function: the loss is x squared plus 25 times y squared, so the valley drops 25 times faster on one axis. One start for all three runners.

One honest footnote: this race stops every runner the moment its loss drops below 0.5, so nobody actually lands on the bottom. The orbiting-Adam phenomenon (Adam circling the minimum without settling) is real with a fixed learning rate, but this toy freezes the race before you'd see it.
