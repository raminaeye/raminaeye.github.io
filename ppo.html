1 Why one big step can wreck a good paddle

A Pong paddle that already hits back most balls can be ruined by one update that changes its move chances too much. This page shows how to learn from each stretch of play several times without that happening.

The agent is the learner that picks the moves (RL Basics).

A state is the agent’s situation right now; a move, or action, is one choice the agent can make in a state; a reward is the number the world hands back after each move, good or bad (RL Basics).

The agent sees a state, makes a move and gets a reward, over and over.

An episode is one run from the start until it ends, and a policy is the agent’s rule for choosing a move in each state; it may always pick the same move or pick by chance.

The running example is Atari Pong. Each player moves a paddle up and down to hit the ball back past the other. The state is the screen, given as a stack of the last few frames so the ball’s direction shows. The moves are up, stay and down. The paddle moves once every few frames of the game; one such move is one step. Each step shifts the paddle only a little, so it has to start moving before the ball arrives. The reward is \(+1\) for a point won, \(-1\) for a point lost and \(0\) on every other step. One episode is one full game. A point ends only when a player misses the ball, so most steps of a game pass with nothing won or lost. \(J\) is the average points per game, the quantity the policy’s weights are moved to raise (Policy Gradient Methods); it is points won minus points lost.

A policy network is a network that looks at the state and outputs the chance of each move (Policy Gradient Methods).

The policy gradient pushes each move the agent played by a score \(\Psi_t\) of how well it turned out, up when the score is positive and down when it is negative (Policy Gradient Methods).

A batch is the group of steps an update learns from; PPO collects each batch from fresh play, a stretch of steps from several games played at once.

PPO is the policy-gradient method that makes small, limited updates so one batch cannot wreck the policy.

Playing a batch takes a long stretch of steps, so it pays to learn from each batch more than once. Yet many gradient steps on the same batch keep pushing the same moves, and the paddle’s chances can swing far past what the batch supports. The PPO paper calls these “destructively large policy updates”.

A smaller step for every weight does not fix this, because it cannot see how far each move’s chance has moved, so it must suit the worst case and wastes most of the batch.

The return is all the reward still to come, with later rewards counted a little less (RL Basics).

A state’s value \(V(s)\) is the return the agent expects from state \(s\) (RL Basics).

In Pong, a screen with the ball heading past the other paddle has a high value, and one with the ball slipping past this paddle a low one.

On-policy means learning the values of the same policy that is playing (SARSA).

Policy gradients are on-policy, because each push counts a move as often as the current policy plays it, so play by an older or different policy would push by the wrong amounts (Actor-Critic: A2C and A3C).

The batch in hand was played by the policy as it was before the update.

Below, PPO corrects for this with a ratio of new to old chances, so the batch can be used a few times.

\(\theta_{\text{old}}\) is the frozen copy of the weights that played the batch; \(\theta\) is the copy being updated.

\(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s)\) means the whole set of move chances on screen \(s\), up, stay and down together, rather than the chance of one move.

So while the paddle learns, the batch still shows the chances it played with, and the update shows the chances it has now.

1.1 An earlier hard limit and its cost

Trust Region Policy Optimization (TRPO), an earlier method, keeps each update inside a trust region, a limit on how far the new chances may move from the old ones in one update.

PPO keeps the same aim for the Pong paddle, chances that stay near the ones that played the batch, but reaches it another way.

How far the chances move is measured by the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence, how different two sets of chances are.

TRPO’s limit needs an expensive extra calculation at every update, and it is awkward with networks that share one body between the policy and other outputs, or that use dropout.

PPO uses only ordinary gradient steps, the same kind that train any network.

\(\displaystyle \begin{aligned}&\hat{\mathbb{E}}_t\Big[\mathrm{KL}\big(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#1f4e9c}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}}(\cdot\mid s_t)\,\Vert\,\\&\quad\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#1f4e9c}{\pi_\theta}}(\cdot\mid s_t)\big)\Big]\le\Delta\end{aligned}\)
TRPO’s hard limit on each update: averaged over the batch’s screens, the new chances may differ from the old only a little. \(\hat{\mathbb{E}}_t\) is the average over the batch; \(\theta_{\text{old}}\) is the frozen weights that played the batch; \(\pi(\cdot\mid s_t)\) is the whole set of move chances on screen \(s_t\), as defined above; \(\mathrm{KL}\) is the difference between the old and new chance sets; \(\Delta\) is the allowed size of the change. The TRPO and PPO papers write \(\delta\) for the limit, but this site uses \(\delta\) for a different quantity on RL Basics. It builds on the KL Divergence.

Inside the limit, TRPO raises a stand-in objective, the surrogate, that rewards making good moves more likely, weighted by how good they were.

A version with a penalty in place of the limit needs a penalty weight that is hard to choose.

PPO replaces both the limit and the penalty with a cap on how far each move’s chance may change.

2 Capping how far each move’s chance can move

Each move in the batch needs a score of how well it turned out.

A move’s advantage is how much better it is expected to do than the usual result from that state (Dueling DQN).

\(\hat A_t\) is an estimate of the advantage of the move at step \(t\), built from real rewards and a guess of the usual result (REINFORCE and the Baseline).

That usual result has to be guessed.

A critic is a second network that guesses each state’s value, the return expected from it (Actor-Critic: A2C and A3C).

In PPO the critic’s guess of a screen’s value is the usual result each move is compared with.

The network being trained is the actor.

An actor is the policy network that picks the action; the critic’s guesses are used to score how good it was (Actor-Critic: A2C and A3C).

Actor-Critic’s actor push raises moves the critic scored above usual and lowers the rest.

PPO reuses each batch for a few passes, so it must track how far each move’s chance has moved since the batch was played.

The ratio \(\rho_t\) compares the new policy’s chance of the move taken with the old policy’s chance; at one, nothing has changed.

Clipping holds the ratio between two clip edges set by \(\epsilon_{\text{clip}}\), so an update stops rewarding a helpful change once it passes an edge.

For a move that scored well, once the new policy already makes it noticeably more likely than the old one did, stop rewarding further increases. For a move that scored badly, once it is noticeably less likely, stop pushing it down. A change in the harmful direction, a bad move becoming more likely or a good move becoming less likely, always keeps its full penalty.

\(\epsilon_{\text{clip}}\) is unrelated to the \(\epsilon\) of epsilon-greedy exploration on the value-based pages (RL Basics).

  1. Workers, copies of the agent each playing its own game, collect a batch with the old policy (Actor-Critic: A2C and A3C).
  2. The critic scores every move: the critic’s guesses and the real rewards are blended into each move’s score.
  3. A few passes of minibatch updates on the clipped objective.
  4. The new policy becomes the old one.

One round runs after each batch, with a few passes over it in minibatches.

GAE (generalized advantage estimation) builds the advantage estimate \(\hat A_t\) from the real rewards and the critic’s guesses, blending short and long stretches of real reward into one (Actor-Critic: A2C and A3C).

\(\lambda\) sets how quickly the long stretches fade.

So in Pong a point won many steps later still raises the scores of the moves that led to it, less the further back they are.

3 The ratio, the surrogate and the clipped objective

\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#6c3483}{\rho_t}}(\theta)=\frac{\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#1f4e9c}{\pi_\theta}}(a_t\mid s_t)}{\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#1f4e9c}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}}(a_t\mid s_t)}\)
The ratio: how much more or less likely a move is on this screen now than when the batch was played. At one, nothing has changed; above one, the move is more likely; below one, less likely. \(\theta\) is the weights being updated; \(\theta_{\text{old}}\) is the frozen weights that played the batch. The paper writes \(r_t\) for the ratio, and it is not a reward; from here the page writes it \(\rho_t\). It builds on the softmax line on Policy Gradient Methods and on the limit above, the same two policies compared on the one move taken.
\(\displaystyle L^{\text{CPI}}(\theta)=\hat{\mathbb{E}}_t\big[\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#6c3483}{\rho_t}}\,\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#196f3d}{\hat{A}_t}}\big]\)
The surrogate: reward making good moves more likely than they were, weighted by how good they were. It grows without limit as the ratio grows. \(\hat A_t\) is the move’s score, built by GAE as taught in Capping how far each move’s chance can move. CPI stands for conservative policy iteration, the earlier method this line comes from (Kakade and Langford 2002). The batch was played by the old policy, so each move is counted again by how much more or less often the new policy would play it; this is the same divide-by-the-chance idea as the log on Policy Gradient Methods. At ratio one it gives the same push as the actor loss on Actor-Critic: A2C and A3C.
\(\displaystyle \begin{aligned}\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#6c3483}{\bar{\rho}_t}}&=\operatorname{clip}\big(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#6c3483}{\rho_t}},\\&\quad 1-\epsilon_{\text{clip}},\;1+\epsilon_{\text{clip}}\big)\end{aligned}\)
The clipped ratio: the ratio, but held inside a narrow band around one. \(\bar\rho_t\) is the clipped ratio; \(\operatorname{clip}(\cdot)\) holds \(\rho_t\) between one minus \(\epsilon_{\text{clip}}\) and one plus \(\epsilon_{\text{clip}}\); the two edges are the clip edges of Figure 4. The real ratio can still end up past an edge, and past it the ratio simply stops counting. It builds on the ratio line above.
\(\displaystyle L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta)=\hat{\mathbb{E}}_t\big[\min\big(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#6c3483}{\rho_t}}\,\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#196f3d}{\hat{A}_t}},\;\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#6c3483}{\bar{\rho}_t}}\,\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#196f3d}{\hat{A}_t}}\big)\big]\)
The clipped objective: the surrogate with a ceiling on the helpful side only. \(\min\) takes the smaller of the plain term, from the surrogate line above, and the clipped term, from the clipped-ratio line above. For a good move (\(\hat A_t\) positive), once \(\rho_t\) passes the right clip edge the clipped term is the smaller one, so \(\min\) picks it; past the edge the clipped term no longer changes with the weights, so it has no slope and gives no push, and the push stops. For a bad move (\(\hat A_t\) negative), multiplying by a negative number flips which term is smaller, so the cap lands on the left edge, and a bad move made more likely keeps its full penalty. The two panels of Figure 4 show the two cases: the helpful side goes flat past the edge, the harmful side never does.

The full objective adds two more terms to the clipped push, one for the critic and one for staying undecided.

Entropy \(\mathcal{H}\) is how undecided the policy is; it is high when the moves are about equally likely and zero when one move is certain (REINFORCE and the Baseline).

The entropy bonus is a small extra term that rewards the policy for staying undecided between moves, so it keeps trying the others for longer (REINFORCE and the Baseline).

In PPO that bonus is the third term of the full objective, and it keeps the paddle from settling on one move while the passes run.

\(\displaystyle \begin{aligned}&L^{\text{PPO}}(\theta,\phi)=\hat{\mathbb{E}}_t\big[L^{\text{CLIP}}_t(\theta)\\&\quad -\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{c_V}}\big(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{V_\phi(s_t)}}-\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#196f3d}{\hat{R}_t}}\big)^2\\&\quad +\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6d80}{c_H}}\,\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6d80}{\mathcal{H}}}\big(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#1f4e9c}{\pi_\theta}}(\cdot\mid s_t)\big)\big]\end{aligned}\)
PPO’s full objective, which is maximised: one objective for the Pong network, a capped push, an accurate critic, and keeping the paddle undecided for a while. \(L^{\text{PPO}}\) is the full objective; the paper writes \(L^{\text{CLIP+VF+S}}\). \(L^{\text{CLIP}}_t\) is the term inside the clipped objective’s average for step \(t\), before averaging. \(c_V\) is the critic weight; the PPO paper writes \(c_1\). \(V_\phi\) is the critic, its guess of the return from screen \(s_t\), whose weights \(\phi\) often share one network body with \(\theta\), as in the Atari runs. \(\hat R_t\) is the return the critic is trained to predict: the GAE score plus the critic’s own guess. \(c_H\) is the entropy weight, from the entropy-bonus line on REINFORCE and the Baseline; the PPO paper writes \(c_2\). \(\mathcal{H}\) is the entropy; \(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s_t)\) is the whole set of move chances. It builds on the clipped objective above, the critic’s target from Actor-Critic: A2C and A3C, and the entropy line from REINFORCE and the Baseline.

The code minimises the negative of this objective, so the signs flip in code.

5.1 Settings

The discount \(\gamma\) sets how much a reward one step later still counts (RL Basics).

Bias here means the push is off from the true uphill direction of \(J\) in the same way on average, not just noisy from one update to the next, as in the bias-variance trade-off; it is not the offset weight inside a network layer (Policy Gradient Methods).

Counting later points a little less makes the push less noisy, because far-off points hang on many later chance moves and add the most noise; the price is a small bias (Schulman and colleagues).

MuJoCo is a physics simulator for robot bodies.

Spread is how much results vary from one training run to the next.

5.2 Practical tips

  • Normalize the advantages in each minibatch: shift and scale them so each minibatch averages zero with a similar size.
  • Anneal the learning rate and the clip range, lowering them step by step over training.
  • Watch how many moves hit a clip edge in each update, and the KL between the old and new policy.
  • Stop the passes early if the KL grows.
  • Scale the rewards in robot tasks.

5.3 Runs with and without the clip

On a set of robot tasks in the MuJoCo simulator, the paper compared no limit, clipping and KL penalties, rescaling each run’s average total reward so a random policy sits at the bottom of the scale and the best setting at the top.

The paper also tried a penalty on the KL whose weight is fixed, and an adaptive one whose weight is halved when the KL stays small and doubled when it grows too large.

The negative average comes from one simulated running robot, where the unclipped run ended worse than the random starting policy.

Replay keeps a memory of past steps, and training draws random batches from it (Deep Q-Networks (DQN)).

On a large set of Atari games, PPO was compared with two other actor-critic methods.

They were advantage actor-critic (A2C) and actor-critic with experience replay (ACER), an actor-critic that reuses old play by reweighting it with a ratio of new to old chances, much like PPO’s ratio, so play by an older policy no longer pushes by the wrong amounts.

Counting the games on which each method earned the most points on average over the whole training run, which rewards learning fast, PPO led; counting final points totals at the end of training, ACER led on more games.

6 A PPO update in PyTorch

A toy PPO update for Pong in PyTorch, to read rather than run; each block’s comment names the card line it computes. Its loop follows the structure of the Atari PPO script in CleanRL, a library of short single-file training scripts; the settings values are the PPO paper’s Atari settings (see References), not CleanRL’s. Pong has six buttons; we keep the three that move the paddle. The code uses gymnasium, the library that runs the games, and ale_py, the Atari game set it loads. The network outputs three logits, the raw outputs \(z\) of the softmax line on Policy Gradient Methods.

import gymnasium as gym, ale_py
import torch
from torch import nn
from torch.distributions import Categorical

gym.register_envs(ale_py)
MOVES = [2, 0, 3]   # Atari action numbers: 2 moves the paddle up, 0 stays, 3 moves it down
n_envs, n_steps = 8, 128   # eight workers, each plays 128 steps per batch

def make_pong():
    env = gym.make("ALE/Pong-v5", frameskip=1)   # the emulator itself skips no frames
    env = gym.wrappers.AtariPreprocessing(env, frame_skip=4)   # each chosen move repeats for 4 frames
    return gym.wrappers.FrameStackObservation(env, 4)
envs = gym.vector.SyncVectorEnv([make_pong] * n_envs,
    autoreset_mode=gym.vector.AutoresetMode.SAME_STEP)

class ActorCritic(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__()
        self.body = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Conv2d(4, 16, 8, stride=4), nn.ReLU(),
            nn.Conv2d(16, 32, 4, stride=2), nn.ReLU(),
            nn.Flatten(), nn.Linear(32 * 9 * 9, 256), nn.ReLU())
        self.policy_head = nn.Linear(256, 3)   # actor: up, stay, down
        self.value_head = nn.Linear(256, 1)    # critic: V_phi(s)
    def forward(self, x):
        h = self.body(x)
        return self.policy_head(h), self.value_head(h).squeeze(-1)   # squeeze drops the extra size-one axis, so each screen gets one guess

settings = {"clip_eps": 0.1, "n_epochs": 3, "minibatch_size": 256,
            "c_v": 1, "c_h": 0.01, "lr": 2.5e-4, "max_grad_norm": 0.5}
clip_eps, n_epochs, minibatch_size = settings["clip_eps"], settings["n_epochs"], settings["minibatch_size"]
c_v, c_h, max_grad_norm = settings["c_v"], settings["c_h"], settings["max_grad_norm"]

agent = ActorCritic()
opt = torch.optim.Adam(agent.parameters(), lr=settings["lr"])

obs, _ = envs.reset()
while True:
    obs_b, act_b, logp_b, rew_b, done_b, val_b = [], [], [], [], [], []
    for _ in range(n_steps):                          # each worker plays 128 steps
        x = torch.as_tensor(obs, dtype=torch.float32) / 255
        logits, v = agent(x)
        dist = Categorical(logits=logits)   # turns the raw outputs into the move chances
        a = dist.sample()
        obs, r, term, trunc, _ = envs.step([MOVES[i] for i in a.tolist()])
        obs_b.append(x); act_b.append(a); logp_b.append(dist.log_prob(a)); val_b.append(v)
        rew_b.append(torch.as_tensor(r, dtype=torch.float32))   # r_{t+1}: the reward after move t
        done_b.append(torch.as_tensor(term | trunc, dtype=torch.float32))   # dones[t]: the game ended right after move t
    with torch.no_grad():
        _, v_last = agent(torch.as_tensor(obs, dtype=torch.float32) / 255)   # the rollout's last step bootstraps from V of the next screen
    values = torch.stack(val_b)
    advantages = compute_gae(...)   # the GAE blend from Actor-Critic: A2C and A3C
    returns = advantages + values.detach()
    old_logp = torch.stack(logp_b).detach()
    actions = torch.stack(act_b)
    obs_f = torch.cat(obs_b)
    # flatten the batch, so each minibatch index picks one step
    advantages = advantages.reshape(-1)
    returns = returns.reshape(-1)
    old_logp = old_logp.reshape(-1)
    actions = actions.reshape(-1)
    for epoch in range(n_epochs):
        for mb in torch.randperm(len(obs_f)).split(minibatch_size):
            adv_mb = (advantages[mb] - advantages[mb].mean()) / (advantages[mb].std() + 1e-8)   # shift and scale the advantages so each minibatch averages zero with a similar size
            new_logits, new_value = agent(obs_f[mb])
            dist_mb = Categorical(logits=new_logits)
            new_logp = dist_mb.log_prob(actions[mb])
            ratio = (new_logp - old_logp[mb]).exp()   # line 1 of the clip card: the ratio
            clipped = ratio.clamp(1 - clip_eps, 1 + clip_eps)   # line 3 of the clip card: the clipped ratio
            pg_loss = -torch.min(ratio * adv_mb, clipped * adv_mb).mean()   # line 4 of the clip card: the clipped objective
            v_loss = (new_value - returns[mb]).pow(2).mean()
            entropy = dist_mb.entropy().mean()
            loss = pg_loss + c_v * v_loss - c_h * entropy   # the full objective card: the clipped push, the critic and the entropy bonus
            opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward()
            nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(agent.parameters(), max_grad_norm)   # a separate cap on the size of each gradient step, unrelated to the ratio clip
            opt.step()
  1. 1Eight copies of Pong in one batch, each played by the same network for 128 steps; a finished game restarts within the same step, so every step is a real move. Done marks the step at which a game ended, so nothing after it is counted.
  2. 2GAE, from Actor-Critic: A2C and A3C: the advantage estimate, built backward from the rewards, the critic’s guesses and the done flags. The blend masks with (1 - dones[t]), so a game that ended contributes nothing after it; do not use CleanRL’s shifted done index.
  3. 3The ratio, line 1 of the clip card: the new chance of each move taken, divided by its old chance.
  4. 4The clipped ratio and the clipped objective, lines 3 and 4 of the clip card: the ratio held between the two edges, and the smaller of the plain and clipped terms, averaged over the minibatch. The minus sign turns the objective the code maximises into a loss.
  5. 5The full objective card: the critic’s loss, the squared gap between its guess and the return it is trained to predict, plus the entropy bonus. The gradient-norm cap is a separate cap on the size of each gradient step, unrelated to the ratio clip.
  6. 6Epochs and minibatches: the batch tensors are flattened first, so each minibatch index picks one step; the batch is shuffled and cut into minibatches of 256, the paper’s Atari value, and the update runs over each minibatch three times. The advantages are shifted and scaled per minibatch so each averages zero with a similar size.

7 Where the clip stops the push

The learning rate \(\eta\) sets how big a step each update takes (RL Basics).

Over the ten passes, larger steps reach the clip edge in fewer passes.

With a badly scored move the picture mirrors. Pushing the move down stops at the clip edge, but a change that made it more likely keeps its full penalty.

A step can still overshoot the edge; the clip only stops further pushing.

8 When PPO fits

PPO is a robust default for games, robots and language-model fine-tuning, when fresh batches are cheap enough to collect and one set of settings should work across many tasks.

Prefer deep Q-network (DQN) family methods when steps are costly and replay is needed. For smooth moves, amounts from a range such as joint angles or steering, in robot control with few samples, see PPO for smooth moves and for language models.

9 What breaks PPO

  • The clip range or the number of passes is too large: the KL blows up.
  • The advantages are not normalized: one batch’s pushes dwarf another’s.
  • The critic is weak: its guesses are wrong, so \(\hat A_t\) is wrong too.
  • The paddle stops being undecided too early: raise \(c_H\).
  • Clipping does not strictly bound the KL: one large step can still overshoot the edge. Add an early stop on the KL.

10 PPO for smooth moves and for language models

The same capped step works when moves come from a range, and when the ‘moves’ are the words a language model writes; what changes?

Continuous Actions uses PPO with smooth moves.

Aligning Language Models with RL shows how a language model’s replies are treated as moves.

The clip never asks what a move is; it only compares the move’s new chance with its old one.

RL, short for reinforcement learning, is learning which moves to make from rewards rather than from labelled answers (RL Basics).

Reinforcement learning from human feedback uses PPO to fine-tune a language model.

The clip and the few passes over each batch stay the same whatever the moves are.

Group relative policy optimization drops the critic and scores each reply against the average result of a group of replies to the same prompt.
