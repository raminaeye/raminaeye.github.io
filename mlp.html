When a Line Isn't Enough

1. The moons

Here is a question I love. You have two crescent moons tangled together, orange and blue, like this. Your model gets one straight cut. One line. Can it separate them?

No. And that is not a trick, it is the whole point. Logistic regression draws the best straight line it can, and the moons curl right through it. The notebook this page follows trains exactly that model on exactly these moons, and the boundary comes out straight while the data laughs at it. If your data bends, your model needs bends too.

2. Line pieces with bends

The fix starts with the humblest possible bend: the ReLU.

$$ \text{relu}(z) = \max(0, z) $$

Read it: negative in, zero out. Positive in, straight through. It is a hinge. Flat on the left, a ramp on the right, one kink at zero. Check it: $\text{relu}(-2) = 0$ and $\text{relu}(3) = 3$.

Now here is the move. A sum of hinges is a piecewise line, and you get to choose where every kink goes. The other notebook this page follows fits $y = 2x^2 + 3x + 1$ three ways: plain linear regression stalls with the train loss stuck near 8.0, two stacked linear layers with no bend do no better (more on why in a minute), and then one ReLU layer drops the loss to 0.17. Same data. The only difference is the bends.

Play with it. Each slider stop adds one more hinge, and the blue polyline is the best fit those hinges can manage:

How to play

  • The point: A curve is just line pieces with bends. Every ReLU is one bend, and stacking more bends lets the fit hug $y = x^2$ tighter.
  • What you'll see: The hinge count marches from 1 to 8; the blue polyline starts as a shallow V, grows kinks at the red ticks, and settles onto the dashed parabola while the MSE readout collapses.
  • Hinges: Sets the bend budget by hand; try 1 and watch the best possible fit still miss badly, then drag to 8 and watch the kinks multiply until the blue line and the dashed curve are barely distinguishable.

That is the whole idea of a neural network, minus the buzzwords. One hinge bends once. A layer of hinges bends everywhere at once, each in its own direction. Stack layers and the bends compose into curves, curls, and eventually crescent moons.

3. How it works

A one-hidden-layer MLP is just hinges with learned positions and learned weights. Written out:

$$ h = W_2 \cdot \text{relu}(W_1 x + b_1) + b_2 $$

Read it inside out. $W_1 x + b_1$ draws a pile of straight lines, one per hidden unit. $\text{relu}$ bends each one at zero. $W_2$ and $b_2$ mix the bent pieces into the final answer. Every symbol earns its keep, so here is a tiny one you can check by hand, two hidden units, input $x = [1, 2]$:

  1. First the lines: $W_1 x + b_1$ with $W_1 = [[1, -1], [0.5, 0.5]]$ and $b_1 = [0, -0.5]$. Unit one: $1\cdot1 + (-1)\cdot2 + 0 = $ $-1$. Unit two: $0.5\cdot1 + 0.5\cdot2 - 0.5 = $ $1$.
  2. Then the bends: $\text{relu}([-1, 1]) = $ $[0, 1]$. The first hinge went flat, the second stayed a ramp.
  3. Then the mix: $W_2 = [2, -1]$, $b_2 = 0.5$. Output $h = 2\cdot0 + (-1)\cdot1 + 0.5 = $ $-0.5$.

One gotcha before the fun part, and it is the reason the notebook's two-linear-layers model flopped. Stack two linear layers with no ReLU between them and watch what happens:

$$ W_2(W_1 x + b_1) + b_2 = (W_2 W_1) x + (W_2 b_1 + b_2) $$

It collapses into one matrix and one bias. Linear of linear is still linear, a fancier straight line, still useless on the moons. The bend is not decoration. It is the entire source of power.

5. Taming the crazy

So the overkill model memorized the moons and drew a boundary that wiggles around individual points. That wiggle is the whole disease: the weights grew huge so the model could bend sharply around every noisy point. Regularization is a tax on weights, and there are two classic tax codes:

$$ L = L_{\text{data}} + \lambda \sum_i |w_i| \quad \text{(L1)} $$
$$ L = L_{\text{data}} + \lambda \sum_i w_i^2 \quad \text{(L2)} $$

L1 is a flat tax per unit of weight. Small weights cost more than they are worth, so they get fired to exactly zero. That is why L1 gives you sparse models. L2 is a progressive tax: it punishes big weights quadratically, so everything shrinks but nothing quite dies. Check it on $w = [2, -3]$ with $\lambda = 0.1$: L1 adds $0.1 \times (2 + 3) = $ $0.5$, L2 adds $0.1 \times (4 + 9) = $ $1.3$.

The notebook's fourth model is the overkill net retrained with L2 ($\lambda = 0.001$), and that is the "tamed" stop on the dial: same giant architecture, but the weights are no longer allowed to go wild, so the boundary relaxes back into a smooth crescent hug. Dropout is the other trick in the notebook: randomly switch off neurons during training (a 0.7 dropout rate after every layer), so the net can never lean on one hero neuron and has to learn redundant paths. Same spirit, different tax.

6. What is next

You now have the full MLP story: bends from ReLUs, power from stacking, and a dial that runs from too simple to too crazy to just right. But notice what every model here assumed. The boundary was always some smooth function of the inputs, a curve drawn through space.

What if the model is not a line at all, but a pile of yes-or-no questions? Is petal width above some threshold? Is x1 to the left of some cut? Ask enough questions and you can carve up any space, no curves required. That is a decision tree, and a crowd of them voting is a random forest. Next: twenty questions, then a forest.
