Multitask Learning Field Guide

Which method for which problem, and what it costs.

On this page

Diagnose first The flowchart Pitfalls Real deployments Batching Which losses

Eleven pages in, the question turns practical: which method do I reach for, and what does it cost me? This page is the field guide. It starts with diagnosis, because medicating before you diagnose is how you end up running PCGrad on a scale problem, and it ends with the two decisions every deployment faces: how to batch the data, and which losses earn a place in shared training.

Diagnose before you medicate

Three lines of logging tell you which of the three diseases you actually have. Only then do you pick the medicine.

  1. Each task's loss, compared with training that task alone. A gap here is negative transfer, by definition. The other two measurements explain why the gap is there.
  2. Each task's gradient norm at a shared layer (the size of that task's gradient on the shared trunk). If one is orders of magnitude bigger than the others, you have a scale problem, and the fix is GradNorm or uncertainty weighting.
  3. The cosine between each pair of task gradients. Often negative means a direction problem, and the fix is PCGrad or CAGrad. In the toy landscape from the CAGrad page, once plain gradient descent was trapped in the ravine, the two gradients kept fighting for the rest of the run. The conflict never resolves itself.

Both measurements are one line of arithmetic. Here is the scale check. Say task A's gradient norm at the shared layer is 12.4 and task B's is 0.31. The ratio:

$$\frac{\|g_A\|}{\|g_B\|} = \frac{12.4}{0.31} = 40$$

Task A shouts forty times louder than task B. In the plain sum, B's gradient is a rounding error.

And here is the direction check, the cosine of the angle \(\phi\) between two gradients:

$$\cos \phi = \frac{\langle g_1, g_2 \rangle}{\|g_1\| \, \|g_2\|}$$

Read it aloud with real numbers, one computation per line. Take \(g_1 = (3, 1)\) and \(g_2 = (-2, -0.5)\). The dot product: \(3 \times (-2) + 1 \times (-0.5) = -6 - 0.5 = -6.5\). The norms: \(\|g_1\| = \sqrt{10} \approx 3.16\), \(\|g_2\| = \sqrt{4.25} \approx 2.06\). Their product: \(3.16 \times 2.06 \approx 6.52\). The cosine: \(-6.5 / 6.52 \approx -1.00\). Nearly exactly opposed: these two gradients pull in opposite directions, and their average is a coin flip decided by whoever is louder.

The same three diseases show up as symptoms first. This is the table I keep taped to the wall:

SymptomLikely causeFirst remedy

Pitfalls worth avoiding

Five mistakes I have watched teams make, including mine:

  1. Watching only the average loss. A great average can hide a task that was quietly sacrificed. I always report per-task numbers too, ideally the average per-task drop against single-task baselines.
  2. Cutting gradients in the task-specific heads. Heads share no parameters, so there is nothing to fight over. Combine gradients on the shared trunk only.
  3. Forgetting GradNorm's renormalization. Without it, the weights slowly change the overall learning speed, and the comparison against baselines stops meaning anything.
  4. Running full K-task surgery on a 50-task problem. K backward passes per step is the real price of PCGrad and CAGrad. Use task subsampling (CAGrad-Fast) or a fast method.
  5. Treating every conflict as poison. The tragic triad says conflict mostly hurts when it comes with a loudness gap and high curvature. Mild conflict between equals is often harmless.

Questions from real deployments

"Our tasks have disjoint datasets. Does this still apply?" Yes, and this is the most common misconception I run into. Every task can have its own dataset and its own head; the gradients still meet in one place, the shared backbone. Conflict is a property of gradients on shared parameters, not of shared data. The per-task dataset, per-task head, shared-trunk setup is the standard case these methods were built on. They would only become irrelevant if the tasks shared nothing at all, and then it would not be multitask learning. To convince a skeptic, log the cosine similarity between pairs of task gradients on the shared trunk for a few hundred steps. If the cosines go negative, and they almost always do, the conflict is real.

"What if one task's batch is much smaller?" A small batch makes a task's gradient jittery: averaged over 8 examples instead of 128, the direction swings step to step, even though it is honest on average. Plain averaging just absorbs the noise. GradNorm occasionally turns a knob on a bad reading but self-corrects. PCGrad sometimes cuts against a fight that was not real, which is wasteful, not dangerous. CAGrad is the most sensitive: its whole decision is "who is worst off right now," and a jittery signal sometimes makes the small-batch task look worst-off when it simply had an unlucky batch. The leash caps how far one noisy step can pull the update. The fixes, in rough order of importance:

  1. Oversample the small task. Going from 8 to 32 samples per step already helps a lot. No algorithmic patch beats a steadier signal.
  2. Smooth the decision signal. Keep an exponential moving average of each task's gradient (decay 0.9 to 0.99), decide on the smoothed gradients, step with the real ones. This composes cleanly with CAGrad: it keeps the protection and the guarantee but stops reacting to one unlucky batch.
  3. Tighten the leash. If you cannot oversample, use a smaller c (0.3 to 0.5). Every step stays closer to the plain average.
  4. Log the variance, not just the average. If the small task's gradient norm swings wildly over a few dozen steps, you have confirmed a shaky signal before it confuses the algorithm.

One more note on this one: GradVac versus CAGrad are not competitors here. GradVac is a direction-fixer with a memory. It smooths the conflict signal but has no opinion about which task deserves help, and it carries no guarantee. CAGrad actively protects the worst-off task and proves it makes progress. If your small-batch task is also your struggling task, which is common, CAGrad addresses the real problem. The best arrangement I know combines them: CAGrad as the decision-maker, GradVac-style averaging inside the measurements it decides on.

How to batch the data

Three layouts, and only one of them lets the methods do their job:

  1. Joint batch: all tasks every step. This is the standard, and my recommendation. Each task produces a loss and a gradient on the shared trunk at the same weights at the same moment, which is exactly what "do these gradients fight?" and "which task is worst off?" require.
  2. Round robin: one task per step. The methods become no-ops: no pair to de-conflict, no worst-off task to find. Worse, round robin does not remove the conflict. It spreads it across time, as the backbone zigzags between tasks, the same pacing that traps plain gradient descent. It removes the diagnostic, not the disease.
  3. Random task per step. Same problem as round robin, shuffled. Incompatible with gradient-combining methods.

Within a joint batch, I prefer equal shares per task, oversampling small datasets so every gradient is measured with equal reliability. The whole point is to let the gradients decide the weighting, which only works if every gradient is trustworthy. If repeating a small dataset worries you, cap the repetition (for example, no task repeats its data more than three times per epoch of the largest) rather than shrinking its share. And if a joint batch does not fit in memory, accumulate: run each task's sub-batch, store its gradient, and combine once all tasks have reported. PCGrad and CAGrad need all task gradients before combining anyway, so accumulation changes where you pay the memory cost, not whether you pay it.

Which losses to bring

A common situation: each team arrives with its own mature single-task model, trained with half a dozen losses. Should all of them come into multitask training? No. The backbone is the only shared, one-shot resource in the whole system. After multitask training, a team takes the backbone, freezes it, and fine-tunes its own head privately, where it can use whatever losses it wants. So each loss faces one question: does this loss need to shape the shared features, or can it do its job later, on the frozen backbone, inside my own head?

Extra losses are expensive: every added loss is another gradient pulling the trunk; the cost is paid by every team, not just the one who brought it; and the loss weights a team tuned for its single-task model no longer mean anything once GradNorm's knobs or CAGrad's leash are involved. The practical filter is the frozen-head test:

  1. Baseline. Multitask-train with each task contributing exactly one loss, its main one. Measure every task's metric.
  2. Frozen-head check. Freeze the backbone; each team fine-tunes its head with its full private stack. Measure again.
  3. Gap analysis. If frozen-head fine-tuning reaches the target, the extra losses were head-level and stay private. If the metric is stuck no matter what the head does, the backbone never learned something the task needs, and that loss belongs in multitask training.
  4. Admit one loss at a time, and re-run. Confirm it closes the gap, and check the other tasks' metrics did not degrade.

With 4 losses there are 6 pairwise relationships to manage; with 8 there are 28. The thin red average is usually dragged toward the loudest few. This is the cost the whole page has been warning about: each extra loss is paid by every team's trunk. Bring fewer, better losses.

Tips that save real time: start with one loss per task, no exceptions. The minimal baseline is the control group that makes every later decision measurable. Freeze the backbone before you argue. Keep the receipt when a loss joins: the other tasks' metrics and the conflict rate, before and after. Pre-combine a task's main and auxiliary losses with fixed weights from its single-task tuning history, so the multitask trainer sees one voice per task. And let the conflict measurements referee team debates. Re-run the frozen-head check after big changes. The verdict on a loss is not permanent.
