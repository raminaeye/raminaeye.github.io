1 What a schedule fixes

A single learning rate has to do two jobs that pull in opposite directions. Early in training a large rate moves fast across the loss surface; near a minimum the same rate keeps bouncing around the bottom, because each minibatch gradient is noisy and every step is full size (Gradient Descent, section 1.2). A schedule changes the rate as training goes: start gently, move fast, then settle.

Every large model is trained with one. ResNets used step decay (He and others 2016); the original Transformer warmed up and then decayed (Vaswani and others 2017); LLaMA warmed up for 2,000 steps and followed a cosine to a tenth of its peak (Touvron and others 2023).

3 Warmup

Warmup: start small and ramp up
\({\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{\alpha_t} = \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{\alpha_{\text{peak}}}\,\frac{t}{W}\qquad (t \le W)}\)
For the first \(W\) steps the rate climbs in a straight line from almost nothing to its peak \(\alpha_{\text{peak}}\). Early on the weights are random and the optimizer’s averages are still filling (Adam, section 5), so a full-size step can throw the model somewhere it cannot recover from; small steps first let it settle into a sensible region.
\({\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{\alpha_{\text{peak}}} = k\,\htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{\alpha_{\text{base}}}}\)
The linear scaling rule from Goyal and others (2017), for a batch \(k\) times larger. One large-batch step at \(k\) times the rate covers about the same ground as \(k\) small-batch steps at the base rate, as long as the gradient barely changes over those \(k\) steps. Early in training the weights change fast and that assumption fails, which is why such large peaks need warmup.
\({\displaystyle \begin{aligned} \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{\alpha_t} &= d_{\text{model}}^{-1/2} \\ &\quad \cdot \min\!\big(t^{-1/2},\; t\,W^{-3/2}\big) \end{aligned}}\)
The original Transformer’s schedule (Vaswani and others 2017, section 5.3): linear warmup for \(W\) steps, then decay with one over the square root of the step. \(d_{\text{model}}\) is the model width, so wider models get smaller rates. The minimum picks whichever branch is smaller: the ramp early, the decay late.

Goyal and others (2017) trained a ResNet-50 on ImageNet with a very large batch by combining the linear scaling rule with a gradual warmup. They found the result robust to the exact warmup length, and the rule broke down only beyond batches of about 8k. Transformers that normalize each sublayer’s input (pre-LN) need less warmup than ones that normalize its output (post-LN) (The Transformer Block).

4 Step and exponential decay

Step and exponential decay
\({\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{\alpha_t} = \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{\alpha_0}\,\gamma^{\lfloor t/s \rfloor}}\)
Step decay: keep the rate flat, then multiply it by \(\gamma\) (gamma, below one) every \(s\) steps or epochs. The brackets round down, so the rate drops in stairs. This was the classic ResNet recipe.
\({\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{\alpha_t} = \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{\alpha_0}\,\gamma^{\,t}}\)
Exponential decay: multiply by \(\gamma\) every step, a smooth version of the stairs. The rate never stops falling, so \(\gamma\) must be close to one for long runs.

5 Cosine annealing

Cosine annealing
\({\displaystyle \begin{aligned} \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{\alpha_t} &= \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{\alpha_{\min}} \\ &\quad + \tfrac{1}{2}\big(\htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{\alpha_{\max}} - \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{\alpha_{\min}}\big) \\ &\quad \cdot \big(1 + \cos\tfrac{\pi t}{T}\big) \end{aligned}}\)
Loshchilov and Hutter’s cosine (2017) follows half a cosine wave from \(\alpha_{\max}\) at the start to \(\alpha_{\min}\) at step \(T\). It falls slowly at first, fastest in the middle, and slowly again at the end, so training spends a long time at high rates and still lands gently.
\({\displaystyle \begin{aligned} \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{\alpha_t} &= \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{\alpha_{\min}} \\ &\quad + \big(\htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{\alpha_{\text{peak}}} - \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{\alpha_{\min}}\big) \\ &\quad \cdot \tfrac{1}{2}\big(1 + \cos\tfrac{\pi\,(t-W)}{T-W}\big) \end{aligned}}\)
The large-model form: warm up for \(W\) steps, then, for \(t > W\), run the cosine over the remaining steps down to a floor. LLaMA warmed up for 2,000 steps and ended at a tenth of its peak (Touvron and others 2023).

6 Warm restarts

Warm restarts
\({\displaystyle \begin{aligned} \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{\alpha_t} &= \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{\alpha_{\min}} \\ &\quad + \tfrac{1}{2}\big(\htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{\alpha_{\max}} - \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{\alpha_{\min}}\big) \\ &\quad \cdot \Big(1 + \cos\frac{\pi\,T_{\text{cur}}}{T_i}\Big) \end{aligned}}\)
SGDR (Loshchilov and Hutter 2017) runs a cosine over a cycle of \(T_i\) epochs, then jumps back to \(\alpha_{\max}\). \(T_{\text{cur}}\) counts epochs since the last restart; it is updated every batch, so it takes fractional values. The jump shakes the model out of the basin it settled in, and each cycle’s end gives a usable snapshot.
\({\displaystyle T_i = T_0\, T_{\text{mult}}^{\,i}}\)
Cycles can grow: each is \(T_{\text{mult}}\) times longer than the last, so later cycles explore less and settle more.

7 One-cycle

One-cycle: a rise, then a long fall
\({\displaystyle \begin{aligned} \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{\alpha_t} &= \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{\alpha_{\text{lo}}} \\ &\quad + \big(\htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{\alpha_{\max}} - \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{\alpha_{\text{lo}}}\big) \\ &\quad \cdot \tfrac{1}{2}\big(1-\cos\tfrac{\pi t}{pT}\big) \end{aligned}}\)
The rise, for the first fraction \(p\) of the \(T\) steps (\(t \le pT\)): from a low starting rate \(\alpha_{\text{lo}}\) up to the peak \(\alpha_{\max}\) along half a cosine. It is a built-in warmup.
\({\displaystyle \begin{aligned} \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{\alpha_t} &= \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{\alpha_{\text{end}}} \\ &\quad + \big(\htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{\alpha_{\max}} - \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{\alpha_{\text{end}}}\big) \\ &\quad \cdot \tfrac{1}{2}\big(1+\cos\tfrac{\pi (t-pT)}{(1-p)T}\big) \end{aligned}}\)
The fall, for the rest of training: from the peak down to a final rate \(\alpha_{\text{end}}\) far below \(\alpha_{\text{lo}}\). The long high stretch trains fast and the deep fall settles the weights. This cosine form is how PyTorch’s OneCycleLR implements the policy by default; Smith and Topin’s original (2019) used straight lines. It steps once per batch.

11 Schedules in PyTorch

A scheduler keeps one counter and sets the optimizer’s rate from it. Step, exponential and cosine usually step once per epoch; one-cycle steps once per batch.

import torch
import torch.optim as optim
from torch.optim import lr_scheduler
net = torch.nn.Linear(4, 1)
ps = net.parameters()
opt = optim.SGD(ps, lr=0.1)
# pick one, stepped per epoch
sched = lr_scheduler.StepLR(opt, 30, 0.1)
sched = lr_scheduler.ExponentialLR(opt, 0.95)
sched = lr_scheduler.CosineAnnealingLR(
    opt, T_max=100)
  1. 1A model and an SGD optimizer to schedule.
  2. 2Step decay, the stairs of the step-decay line.
  3. 3Exponential decay, the smooth line.
  4. 4Cosine annealing over \(T\) epochs, the cosine line.
sched = lr_scheduler.CosineAnnealingWarmRestarts(
    opt, T_0=10)
sched = lr_scheduler.OneCycleLR(
    opt, max_lr=0.1, total_steps=1000)
for step in range(1000):
    opt.step()
    sched.step()  # per batch
  1. 1Warm restarts with a first cycle of \(T_0\) epochs; to update every batch as the paper does, pass the fractional epoch to sched.step().
  2. 2One-cycle up to its peak and back down over the whole run.
  3. 3Steps the optimizer, then the one-cycle schedule, once per batch.

The large-model recipe chains a linear warmup into a cosine. For the Transformer’s inverse square root, write the warmup card’s last line as a function and pass it to LambdaLR.

import torch
from torch.optim.lr_scheduler import LinearLR, CosineAnnealingLR, SequentialLR
model = torch.nn.Linear(512, 512)
opt = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=3e-4, weight_decay=0.1)
warm = LinearLR(opt, start_factor=0.01, total_iters=2_000)
cos = CosineAnnealingLR(opt, T_max=98_000, eta_min=3e-5)   # floor: a tenth of the peak
sched = SequentialLR(opt, [warm, cos], milestones=[2_000])
for step in range(100_000):
    opt.step()      # after backward() in a real loop
    sched.step()    # per batch
  1. 1AdamW with a LLaMA-like peak rate and decay.
  2. 2Linear warmup over \(W\) steps, then a cosine to a floor of a tenth of the peak.
  3. 3Both stepped once per batch, after the optimizer.

12 Failure modes and tips

Most schedule bugs are about when and how often the scheduler steps.
symptomcausefix
the first rate is skipped and PyTorch warnssched.step() called before opt.step()step the optimizer first (Optimizers in Practice: failures in the loop)
the rate reaches its floor in the first epocha per-epoch scheduler stepped every batchset T_max in batches, or step once per epoch
the rate never gets low\(T\) longer than the runset T_max to the real number of steps
loss spikes in the first steps, worst with large batches or Adamno warmupadd a linear warmup
a resumed run warms up againscheduler state not savedsave sched.state_dict() with the optimizer
