Recommender Systems

People who liked this.

What does "people who liked this also liked that" actually mean? It means taste has a shape. Put every movie as a point in user space: two movies are close if the same users rated them the same way. Or put every movie as a point in genre space: two movies are close if they share genres. Either way, "recommend" becomes "find the nearest neighbors." That is all a recommender is: geometry plus a similarity score.

The notebook tries three geometries on the MovieLens data: similarity from ratings, similarity from genres, and learned embeddings. They do not always agree, and the disagreements are the interesting part.

Three ways to say "similar"

Way one: similar raters. Take the user-movie rating matrix, one row per user, one column per movie, zeros where nobody rated. Take two movie columns and compute their cosine similarity. If the same users gave both movies high ratings, the columns point the same way and the cosine is near 1. One honest footnote: the notebook calls this recommender "content based," but look at what it actually does. No content is involved, just who rated what. It is collaborative filtering wearing a content-based costume.

Way two: similar content. Forget the ratings. Turn each movie's genre list into a binary vector (1 if the movie has the genre, 0 if not) and compute cosine similarity between those. Toy Story and Toy Story 2 share all five genres, so their similarity is exactly 1. This one is the real content-based method.

Way three: learn the geometry. Instead of hand-picking the vectors, learn them. Give every user and every movie a 20-number embedding, plus a bias each, and train the whole thing to predict ratings with gradient descent. The prediction for user $u$ and movie $i$ is a dot product plus biases. Then a hybrid adds the genre similarity on top. The notebook's scoreboard: collaborative filtering alone gets test RMSE 2.54, the hybrid gets 2.47.

The machinery, step by step

Step 1: cosine similarity

$$ \cos(u, v) = \frac{u \cdot v}{\|u\| \, \|v\|} $$

Term by term: $u$ and $v$ are two item vectors, either two columns of the rating matrix or two genre vectors. $u \cdot v$ is the dot product, the sum of pairwise products, large when the same positions are large in both. $\|u\|$ and $\|v\|$ are the lengths. Dividing by the lengths turns the dot product into the cosine of the angle between the vectors: 1 means identical direction, 0 means no overlap. With nonnegative ratings and binary genres, the score always lands between 0 and 1.

Step 2: the learned prediction

$$ \hat{r}_{ui} = p_u \cdot q_i + b_u + b_i $$

Term by term: $p_u$ is user $u$'s learned embedding (20 numbers), $q_i$ is movie $i$'s learned embedding. Their dot product is the affinity: high when the user's taste vector lines up with the movie's vibe vector. $b_u$ is the user bias: some people just rate everything high. $b_i$ is the item bias: some movies are just widely loved. This is exactly the notebook's forward():

def forward(self, user_ids, item_ids):
    user_embeds = self.user_embed(user_ids)
    item_embeds = self.item_embed(item_ids)
    user_bias = self.user_bias(user_ids)
    item_bias = self.item_bias(item_ids)
    dot_product = (user_embeds * item_embeds).sum(dim=1)
    return dot_product + user_bias.squeeze() + item_bias.squeeze()

Four learned numbers combine into one prediction: the dot product says how well this user's taste lines up with this movie, and the two biases correct for generous raters and beloved movies.

Step 3: RMSE, the scoreboard

$$ \text{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum (r - \hat{r})^2} $$

Term by term: $r$ is the true rating, $\hat{r}$ is the prediction. Square the error so big misses hurt more than small ones, average over all test ratings, take the square root to get back to rating units. RMSE of 2.54 means the typical prediction is off by about two and a half stars. Lower is better, and the hybrid's 2.47 beats the pure collaborative filter's 2.54.

How to play

  • The point: Two honest ways to say "similar movies" give different answers. Rating-cosine finds movies the same users rated alike; genre-cosine finds movies with the same genres. The only-here markers show where they part ways.
  • What you will see: Play steps through all 8 movies, about 2.2 seconds each. Both top-5 lists rebuild live every step, and the only-here markers jump to whichever entries the two methods disagree on.
  • Play: Tours all 8 movies hands-free, about 2.2 seconds each, rebuilding both top-5 lists at every stop while the only-here markers jump to the disagreements.
  • Movie: pick each movie and compare its two lists. Try Toy Story: rating-cosine leads with Toy Story 2, while genre-cosine ties two movies at a perfect 1.0 (Toy Story 2, Monsters Inc.).
  • Left list (rating-cosine vs genre-cosine): flip it and watch the two panels trade sides. The only-here markers stay glued to the same movies, because the disagreement is in the data, not the layout.
  • Reset: Puts the movie back at Toy Story and rating-cosine back on the left, the opening frame. Try it after a tour and watch the lists snap back to the start.

The classical toolkit, complete

The classical toolkit is complete. You can fit a line, squash it into a probability, stack it into a network, ask twenty questions, maximize the margin, ask the neighbors, count words, find clumps, compress faces, and now recommend movies. Every one of these is a small idea with a clear equation and a toy you can turn. The deep pages take it from here: the same ideas, with more layers and more data.
