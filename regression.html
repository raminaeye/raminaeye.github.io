Linear Regression

Fitting a line, walking downhill.

You know me, I am going to start the classical machine learning tour with the humblest model of them all: a straight line. Linear regression. The "hello world" of learning from data, and I mean that as a compliment.

Why fit a line at all?

Here is the question I love: why does the oldest trick in machine learning still work? You collected all this data, and the best you can do is draw a line through it?

Here is the thing. A line is the simplest machine that can still learn. One weight per feature, one bias, and you are done. It trains in milliseconds. You can read its mind: every weight tells you exactly how much its feature matters. And the punchline: half of deep learning is lines stacked on lines with bends in between. Master the line, and the fancy stuff gets a lot less scary.

Every feature gets a vote

Forget the jargon for a second. Linear regression says: every feature gets a vote, the votes add up, and a bias shifts the total. That is the entire model.

$$ h(x) = w \cdot x + b $$

Term by term, in plain words:

  • $x$: the features. Plain numbers. For a house: size, bedrooms, age.
  • $w$: the weights, one per feature. How loudly each feature votes. Big positive weight: this feature pushes the prediction up.
  • $b$: the bias, also called the intercept. The prediction when every feature is zero. It slides the whole line up or down.
  • $h(x)$: the hypothesis. A fancy word for "our prediction".

Concrete numbers, because abstract is boring. Say $w = [5, 2]$, $b = 2$, and one sample $x = [1, 3]$:

  1. Multiply: $5 \times 1 = 5$, $2 \times 3 = 6$.
  2. Add the bias: $5 + 6 + 2 =$ 13.

That is it. Multiply, add, done. The notebook I learned this from fits exactly this shape: $y = 5x_1 + 2x_2 + 2$, two features, 2000 samples. The tool below re-enacts that same fit, live.

How it learns: walk downhill on the cost

A line has knobs ($w$ and $b$). We need a score that says how wrong the current knob settings are. That score is the cost, and for regression it is the mean squared error:

$$ J(w, b) = \frac{1}{2m} \sum_{i=1}^{m} (h(x_i) - y_i)^2 $$

Term by term:

  • $h(x_i) - y_i$: the residual. Prediction minus truth. How far off we are on sample $i$.
  • Square it: big misses hurt more than small ones, and the sign stops mattering. Missing by 4 costs 16 times missing by 1, not 4 times.
  • $\frac{1}{m}\sum$: average over all $m$ samples. One bad sample should not run the show.
  • The $\frac{1}{2}$: pure bookkeeping. It cancels the 2 that falls out when you differentiate. You will thank it in one paragraph.

Let me make it concrete with three tiny samples, worked by hand so you can check me. Samples: $x=[1,0], y=3$; $x=[0,1], y=4$; $x=[1,1], y=5$. Start with $w=[0,0]$, $b=0$, so every prediction is 0:

  1. Residuals: $0-3 = -3$, $0-4 = -4$, $0-5 = -5$.
  2. Squares: $9$, $16$, $25$. Sum $= 50$.
  3. $J = 50 / (2 \times 3) =$ 8.3333. That is our starting score. Only downhill from here.

The gradient: which way is downhill?

Now the clever part. This cost is a bowl: convex, one bottom, no traps. To walk downhill you need the slope under your feet, which is the gradient:

$$ \frac{\partial J}{\partial w_j} = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^{m} (h(x_i) - y_i)\, x_{ij} $$
$$ \frac{\partial J}{\partial b} = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^{m} (h(x_i) - y_i) $$

Read it in plain language: average over samples of (how wrong we are) times (the feature value). If the residual is negative (we predicted too low) and the feature is positive, the gradient is negative, so stepping against it pushes the weight up. Makes sense: raise the weight, raise the prediction, shrink the miss.

Same toy, same zeros, checked by hand. Residuals $(-3, -4, -5)$; multiply by each knob's features and average:

  1. Weight $w_1$, features $(1, 0, 1)$:
    $$ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial J}{\partial w_1} &= \frac{(-3)(1) + (-4)(0) + (-5)(1)}{3} \\ &= -8/3 = \mathbf{-2.6667} \end{aligned} $$
  2. Weight $w_2$, features $(0, 1, 1)$:
    $$ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial J}{\partial w_2} &= \frac{(-3)(0) + (-4)(1) + (-5)(1)}{3} \\ &= -9/3 = \mathbf{-3} \end{aligned} $$
  3. Bias $b$:
    $$ \frac{\partial J}{\partial b} = \frac{-3 - 4 - 5}{3} = \mathbf{-4} $$

All negative: everything needs to go up. Obviously. We predicted zero for everything.

Gradient descent: the whole algorithm in two lines

$$ w_j := w_j - \alpha \frac{\partial J}{\partial w_j} $$
$$ b := b - \alpha \frac{\partial J}{\partial b} $$

That is it. $\alpha$ is the learning rate, your step size. Step against the gradient (downhill, not up), update every knob at the same time, repeat a few hundred times. The tool below does exactly this in front of you.

Skip the walk: the closed form

You do not have to walk. For squared error you can solve for the bottom of the bowl directly:

$$ W = (X^T X)^{-1} X^T y $$

$X$ is the whole dataset as a matrix (one row per sample), $y$ the targets. One matrix inverse and you teleport to the answer. The notebook ran this on $y = 5x$ (one feature, a whisper of regularization) and got 4.9999. Basically 5; the 0.0001 is the regularization's fault. On clean data it recovers the truth exactly: 5, 2, 2. Try the closed-form button below and watch it.

How to play

  • The point: Gradient descent walks the line onto the data one small step at a time, while the closed form teleports to the exact answer. Two routes, same destination.
  • What you'll see: The blue prediction dots slide onto the gray ground-truth crosses in both panels while the cost curve dives; at the end the red line reads y = 5x1 + 2x2 + 2, with the weights sitting at [5, 2] and the bias at 2.
  • Learning rate: Sets the step size. Try 0.01 and watch the dots crawl; try 1.0 and watch them sprint; try past 1.9 and watch them bounce off the rails while the diverged badge lights up. Drag the slider and the dashed line previews where the walk ends before anything moves; push it to 2.0 and the would-diverge badge lights up at rest.
  • Iterations: How many gradient steps each run takes. Try 50 at learning rate 0.01 and watch it stall halfway; try 400 and watch it finish the job. Drag it and watch the dashed end-of-walk line slide: at 50 it stops short of the data, at 400 it sits on it.
  • Snap to closed form: Skips the walk and solves (X^T X)^-1 X^T y directly. Try it and watch the weights land on exactly [5.0000, 2.0000] with bias 2.0000 while the cost collapses to about 1e-30.
  • Reset: Throws the weights back to a random start and puts both sliders back at their defaults (learning rate 0.102, 200 iterations). Try it after a run and watch the dots scatter so you can walk them home again.

Same trick, bent

Polynomials: same trick, bent. Square your features and a line in the squared features is a curve in the originals. The notebook fits $y = 2x_1^2 - x_2^2 + 4x_3^2 + 1$ with the exact same gradient descent code, because as far as the math is concerned, $x^2$ is just another feature. Lines all the way down.

Classification needs a squashed line. A line predicts numbers, but yes-or-no needs a probability. So next we take this exact line, squash it through a curve that pins it between 0 and 1, and teach it to say yes or no. That is logistic regression.
