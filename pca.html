Principal Component Analysis (PCA)

Faces from parts.

What if I told you a face is 10 numbers? Not a metaphor. The notebook loads 400 faces, each one 64 by 64 pixels, which is 4096 numbers per face. Then it rebuilds each face from just 10 numbers, and you can still tell who it is. That is PCA: find the directions where the data actually varies, keep those, throw away the rest.

Think of it like describing a person to a sketch artist. You do not list all 4096 pixels. You say how much of each part this face has: a bit more of part 1, a bit less of part 2. PCA finds the parts automatically, from the data alone, no labels. The parts look like ghostly faces, and the paper that started it all literally calls them eigenfaces.

Which way does the cloud stretch?

Every face in the dataset is a point in 4096-dimensional space. One coordinate per pixel. Those points are not scattered randomly: faces all have eyes above a nose above a mouth, so the cloud of points is stretched along some directions and flat along others. The background pixels barely change from face to face. The pixels around the eyes and mouth change a lot.

PCA asks one question: along which directions does this cloud vary the most? Those directions are the principal components. The first one points along the biggest stretch of the cloud, the second along the next biggest (at a right angle to the first), and so on. Keep the top k directions and you keep most of the variation while describing each face with k numbers instead of 4096.

Because each direction is itself a 4096-dimensional vector, you can reshape it into a 64 by 64 image and look at it. What you see is a ghostly face: bright where faces differ a lot along that direction, dark where they differ the other way. Turk and Pentland wrote it plainly: the eigenvector can be displayed "as a sort of ghostly face which we call an eigenface."

The pipeline, one equation at a time

The notebook implements PCA from scratch in numpy. Here is the whole pipeline, one equation at a time.

Step 1: center the data

Subtract the mean face from every face. The mean face $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ is just the average of all 400 faces, pixel by pixel. (The paper shows it as its Figure 1b, the average face $\Psi$.) Centering matters because variance is measured around the mean: without this step, the first component would just point at the average face instead of pointing along the variation.

$$ \mathbf{x}_{centered} = \mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu} $$

Read it term by term: $\mathbf{x}$ is one face as a 4096-long vector, $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ is the mean face, and the result is that face with the average removed. A pixel that is brighter than average becomes positive, darker than average becomes negative.

Step 2: the covariance matrix

$$ C = \frac{X^T X}{n-1} $$

Term by term: $X$ is the centered data matrix, $n$ faces by $d$ pixels (400 by 4096 in the notebook). $X^T X$ multiplies it by its transpose, giving a $d$ by $d$ matrix. Entry $C_{ij}$ answers: when pixel $i$ is brighter than average, is pixel $j$ also brighter than average? Positive means they move together, negative means they move opposite. Dividing by $n-1$ turns the sums into the unbiased sample covariance. The diagonal entries are just each pixel's own variance.

The notebook computes it exactly like this:

covariance_matrix = np.dot(centered_data.T, centered_data) / (num_observations - 1)

Step 3: eigenvectors and eigenvalues

$$ C\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v} $$

Term by term: $\mathbf{v}$ is an eigenvector, a direction in pixel space. $\lambda$ is its eigenvalue, a single number. The equation says: multiplying the covariance matrix by $\mathbf{v}$ just stretches $\mathbf{v}$ by $\lambda$, it does not rotate it. That only happens for special directions, and those special directions are exactly the directions of pure variation. The stretch factor $\lambda$ tells you how much the data varies along that direction. Big $\lambda$: faces differ a lot along $\mathbf{v}$. Small $\lambda$: nearly constant, safe to drop.

The eigenvectors come out sorted by eigenvalue, biggest first. Reshape $\mathbf{v}_1$ into 64 by 64 and you are looking at the first eigenface.

Step 4: project (encode)

$$ z_i = \mathbf{v}_i^T(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}) $$

Term by term: take a centered face and dot it with eigenface $i$. The dot product measures alignment: $z_i$ is large when this face has a lot of eigenface $i$ in it. The $k$ numbers $z_1 \ldots z_k$ are the compressed code for the face. This is the encoding half: 4096 numbers in, $k$ numbers out.

Step 5: reconstruct (decode)

$$ \hat{\mathbf{x}} = \boldsymbol{\mu} + \sum_{i=1}^{k} z_i \mathbf{v}_i $$

Term by term: start from the mean face $\boldsymbol{\mu}$, then add back each eigenface $\mathbf{v}_i$ scaled by how much of it this face had ($z_i$). With $k$ small you get a ghost: the right person, blurry. With $k$ bigger the details fill in. This sum is exactly what the faceToy below computes live, in your browser, from the notebook's own eigenfaces.

Step 6: how much did we keep?

The share of the dataset's total variation explained by component $i$ is the ratio:

$$ \frac{\lambda_i}{\sum_j \lambda_j} $$

Term by term: $\lambda_i$ is the variance along component $i$, the denominator is the total variance across all components. Add up the first $k$ ratios and you get the cumulative energy kept by $k$ components. The notebook asks: how many components to keep 90% of the energy?

cumsum = np.cumsum(eigenvalues)
percent_energy = np.sum(eigenvalues) * 0.9
for idx, csum in enumerate(cumsum[1:]):
    if csum > percent_energy:
        print(idx)
        break

It prints 64. But look closely: the loop iterates over cumsum[1:], which drops the first element. Index 64 in that sliced array is index 65 in the real cumulative sum, and cumsum[65] adds up eigenvalues 0 through 65, which is 66 components. The printed 64 is off by one from the slice. The true answer on the notebook's 64 by 64 grid: 66 components hold 90% of the energy (cumulative 0.9002 at 66, still 0.8988 at 65).

How to play

  • The point: A face is a weighted sum of eigenfaces. The k slider chooses how many parts go into the sum, and the reconstruction sharpens from a ghost into a recognizable person as k grows.
  • What you will see: The canvas opens on the mean face, the ghost every rebuild starts from, with the eigenface strip unlit. Press Play and k sweeps 1 to 24: at k = 1 the rebuild is a blurry ghost with the right outline, by k = 10, the notebook's own setting, the person is recognizable, and past 10 details keep filling in while each new eigenface adds less.
  • Components k: draws the rebuild for that k by hand; drag it slowly from 1 to 24 and watch the reconstruction sharpen while the eigenface strip lights up one more part at a time. Park at 10, the red tick, to see exactly what the notebook showed.
  • Face picker: switch between the four people and watch the same k rebuild each one. Some faces need more parts than others: compare how ghostly Person 3 still looks at k = 5 versus Person 21.
  • Reset: Puts k back at 10, the face back at Person 20, and clears the canvas back to the mean-face start. Try it after a sweep and watch the person melt back to the ghost so you can rebuild again.

How to play

  • The point: rank is a quantity, not a label. The notebook's eigenface 1 explains 25.39% of the variance and eigenface 7 only 2.49%, a ten-to-one drop. That steep fall is why Turk and Pentland's recognizer kept just the top 7 eigenfaces from its 16 training images.
  • What you will see: seven bars under the 1991 figure, in the paper's display order, each bar's height the eigenface's share of variance. The slider fades every face past the top k; Play walks k from 7 down to 1, 2.2 seconds per step, with the kept-variance readout.
  • Bars: try clicking bar 1, then bar 7, and compare 25.39% against 2.49%. The readout always names the notebook's eigenface, not the paper's.
  • Keep top k slider (1 to 7): try dragging it to 1 and watch six faces fade while the kept variance falls to 25.39%. The two black cells never fade; the paper left them empty.
  • Reset: Puts k back at 7 with all faces unfaded and the tour readout back at eigenface 1. Try it after a walk and watch the faded faces come back.

From faces to taste

PCA treats a face as a point and finds the geometry of the cloud. The same trick works on taste: treat each movie as a point in user space, each user as a point in movie space, and suddenly "people who liked this" becomes a geometry question. That is recommendation: taste as geometry.
