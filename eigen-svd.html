Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors

The directions a matrix only stretches, and the long and short axes of a data cloud.

Before you start Matrix times a vector The multivariate Gaussian Covariance

A matrix turns most arrows it multiplies. A few directions only get longer or shorter. These are its eigenvectors, and the stretch of each is its eigenvalue. For face photos, the eigenvectors of the pixels’ covariance (Section 3) are the directions the pixels vary along most. PCA turns them into face-shaped directions, the eigenfaces. Collapse checks test that a model’s output vectors have not squeezed onto a few directions. They list the eigenvalues, the spectrum. LoRA fine-tunes a model with a low-rank update, one built from a few directions.

1 The few directions that matter

Eigenvectors are computed once from a covariance, or once per check on a model’s outputs. Prediction and PCA then reuse the fixed directions, and nothing is carried between faces. The cost grows with the cube of the feature count, so the code takes a small patch and whole photos go through the SVD.

2 Directions a matrix only stretches

PCA’s eigenfaces and the axes of GMM’s ellipses are these directions. The example is \(R\), the correlation table of two face pixels across PCA’s 400 photos: its diagonal holds each pixel’s own correlation, 1, and its off-diagonal says how strongly the two pixels brighten together.

A
A direction the matrix only stretches is an eigenvector; the stretch is its eigenvalue.
\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{M} \htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{v} = \htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\lambda } \htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{v}\)
\(M\) is any matrix and \(v\) a direction, an arrow from the origin. \(Mv\) is the matrix’s output for it (Vectors and Matrices, section 5). \(\lambda\) is the stretch: the output points along \(v\), only \(\lambda\) times as long. A negative \(\lambda\) flips the direction, and a \(\lambda\) of zero flattens it.
\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{R} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \rho \\ \rho & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\lambda_{1}} = 1 + \rho, \quad \htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\lambda_{2}} = 1 - \rho\)
For two pixels that brighten together, the both-pixels-brighter direction is an eigenvector stretched by one plus their correlation, and the one-up-one-down direction is an eigenvector stretched by one minus it. A correlation near one makes the first stretch large and the second near zero: a long thin cloud. Terms: \(\rho\) is the correlation; the ones on the diagonal say each pixel is scaled to spread one.

Section 4 finds every such pair for a covariance at once.

3 Covariance from data

PCA and collapse checks build this matrix, the covariance, here written \(C\). Figure 3 shows a few faces at the same two pixels: the covariance measures how their cloud spreads and tilts around its mean.

Builds on entrywise and outer products (Vectors and Matrices, section 6.3) and covariance (Probability and Bayes Rule, section 5)

B
The covariance averages, over faces, each centred face times itself as an outer product.
\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{C} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{j} \htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{(x_{j} - \bar{x})(x_{j} - \bar{x})^{T}}\)
\(x_{j}\) holds face \(j\)’s two pixel values and \(\bar{x}\) (x bar) their mean over the \(N\) faces, so \(x_{j} - \bar{x}\) is face \(j\) centred, the green arrow in Figure 3. Times its own transpose it makes a two-by-two table, the outer product (Vectors and Matrices, section 6.3). The sum adds the \(N\) tables and \(\frac{1}{N}\) averages them. The diagonal of \(C\) holds each pixel’s variance; the off-diagonal says how the two move together. With every feature scaled to spread one, \(C\) is a correlation table like card A’s \(R\).

4 Every covariance splits into its directions

\(R\) of Section 2 is itself a covariance: the covariance of the two pixels after each is scaled to variance 1. One direction, both pixels brighter together, carries most of its variance; the other, one pixel brighter and one darker, carries the rest; and the eigenvalues add up to the total variance, the sum of the diagonal, because turning the axes onto the eigen-directions moves variance between directions but never creates or loses any.

C
A covariance splits into its eigenvectors and eigenvalues: turn to the eigen-directions, stretch each, turn back.
\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{C} = \htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{V} \htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\Lambda } \htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{V^{T}}\)
\(V\) holds the eigenvectors \(v_{1}\) and \(v_{2}\) as columns; \(\Lambda\) (capital lambda) holds the eigenvalues \(\lambda_{1} \ge \lambda_{2}\) on its diagonal, 0 elsewhere. Reading right to left, \(V^{T}\) turns a point onto the eigen-directions, \(\Lambda\) stretches each by its \(\lambda\), and \(V\) turns it back. The transpose, glossed here at first use: a transpose lays a column flat as a row, and here it turns onto the eigen-directions.
\(\displaystyle e^{T} \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{C} e \le \htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\lambda _{1}}\)
\(e\) is any direction of length 1 and \(e^{T} C e\) the variance along it. Any direction is a mix of the eigen-directions, so its variance is a weighted average of the eigenvalues and can never beat the largest. It peaks at \(\lambda_{1}\) when \(e\) is \(v_{1}\), so PCA’s first eigenface is the direction of most variance. A diagonal \(C\) has the axes as eigenvectors; equal eigenvalues make a round cloud, the diag and spherical shapes of GMM.
\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{V^{T}} \htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{V} = I\)
\(I\) is the identity, 1 on the diagonal and 0 elsewhere. So \(V\) is orthogonal: its columns have length 1 and meet at right angles, and it keeps every length, only turning or flipping. Such turns mark word positions in attention without changing vector lengths (the RoPE section of Positional Encoding and Masking). Dividing each point’s coordinate along each eigen-direction by the square root of that direction’s \(\lambda\) gives variance 1 in every direction, which is whitening.

Turn a round cloud onto the eigen-directions, stretch each axis by the square root of its eigenvalue (that axis’s spread), turn back, then slide the cloud to the data’s mean. Its covariance is \(V \Lambda V^{T} = C\), line 4, because stretching by the square root multiplies the variance by \(\lambda\), and sliding by \(\bar{x}\) moves the centre without changing the spread.
\(\displaystyle w = \htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\Lambda^{-1/2}} \htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{V^{T}} (\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{x} - \htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\bar{x}})\)
Whitening undoes the slide, the stretch and the turn: centre, turn onto the axes, divide each by the square root of its \(\lambda\). The last turn back is optional, since a round cloud looks the same turned, so the line stops there. The cloud comes out round, spread one in every direction, which is where ICA starts.

Whitening undoes that split, and PCA and ICA both start from whitened data.

5 The spectrum

PCA’s kept share reads the same spectrum: the share the top directions hold. When trailing eigenvalues sit at zero, the embeddings use fewer directions than they have numbers, which is collapse.

D
\(\displaystyle \text{kept}(k) = \frac{\sum_{i\,=\,1}^{k} \htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\lambda_{i}}}{\sum_{i} \htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\lambda_{i}}}\)
The share of total spread the top \(k\) directions hold. A spectrum that falls to zero says the data lives in fewer directions than it has numbers.

6 Eigenvectors in NumPy

eigh finds a symmetric matrix’s eigen-pairs. Centre first, then build \(C\). A whole photo has many more pixels than there are photos, which makes \(C\) huge (the last failure row), so this snippet takes a small patch of pixels; for whole photos, take the SVD of the centred data instead (Singular Value Decomposition (SVD), section 3). Faces are one photo per row; the patch is a small square at the centre of the face; the covariance is square over the patch pixels.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import (
    fetch_olivetti_faces)

images = fetch_olivetti_faces().images
# (n_faces, height, width)
patch = slice(28, 36)
# a small square at the centre
pixels = (images[:, patch, patch]
    .reshape(len(images), -1))
# (n_faces, n_patch_pixels)
centred = pixels - pixels.mean(axis=0)
cov = centred.T @ centred / len(pixels)
eigenvalues, eigenvectors = \
    np.linalg.eigh(cov)  # ascending order
order = np.argsort(eigenvalues)[::-1]
eigenvalues = eigenvalues[order]
eigenvectors = eigenvectors[:, order]
kept = np.cumsum(eigenvalues) / \
    eigenvalues.sum()
  1. 1card B, centre and build \(C\) divided by \(N\);
  2. 2card C, eigen-pairs sorted largest first, since eigh lists smallest first;
  3. 3card D, the kept share for every \(k\).

The covariance has one eigenvalue per feature, and the SVD of the same centred data has one non-zero singular value per non-zero eigenvalue, in a list that runs to the smaller of the two matrix dimensions, including zeros; the two lists match on the non-zero entries, and the remaining eigenvalues are zero, with no non-zero singular value. On this page a feature is a patch pixel, so eigh returns one eigenvalue per patch pixel.

7 Most matrices are not covariances

A covariance splits into its own directions, but a general matrix has no covariance to split, so what splits every matrix? The SVD, which turns, stretches and turns, splits every matrix.
