Non-Contrastive Self-Supervised Learning

On this page

This page follows Contrastive Learning.

A quick recap. An encoder is the network that turns an input, here an image, into a vector called its embedding. Take one photo and edit it twice at random (crop it, change its colors); each edited copy is a view. Both views go through the same encoder, and their two embeddings form a positive pair that training pulls together. Contrastive learning also pushes every other image away; those other images are the negatives.

Everything on this page works with no negatives at all. That brings one danger, collapse: the encoder maps every input to the same vector, so every pair matches perfectly and the model has learned nothing.

Pulling pairs together with no negatives should collapse

If the only signal is make the two views of each image match, the cheapest answer is to output the same vector for every image. Every pair then matches, the loss hits its minimum, and the embedding says nothing about the image. In contrastive learning the negatives stop this, because a constant output cannot push other images away. Take the negatives out and nothing in the loss forbids the constant answer.

A subtler form, dimensional collapse, where embeddings still differ but only along a few directions, is in Collapse squeezes every embedding to one point or a thin slice, on Self-Supervised Learning Failure Modes and Checks. The three methods on this page avoid complete collapse without negatives, each with a different mix of parts.

BYOL predicts the output of a slowly updated copy of itself

Okay, here is where it gets weird. BYOL (Bootstrap Your Own Latent, where latent means the learned embedding space) drops the negatives entirely: just pull the two views together. It sounds like it should collapse instantly. It does not.

BYOL runs two networks. The online network is the one being trained. It has an encoder, then a projector, then a predictor. The projector is a small MLP (a multilayer perceptron: a few fully connected layers with a nonlinearity between them) that maps the encoder's output to the vector the loss sees. The predictor is a second small MLP of the same shape. The online network turns view 1 into a prediction p1. The target network has only an encoder and a projector, and turns view 2 into a target z2. The target network is never trained by gradients. It is a slow moving average of the online network.

The loss asks the prediction to point the same way as the target. Both vectors are first scaled to length 1 (a bar over a letter means scaled to unit length), so they sit on a sphere, and the loss is the squared distance between them. BYOL computes it in both directions and adds the two (BYOL Eq. 2): view 1's prediction against view 2's target, and view 2's prediction against view 1's target. BYOL's paper writes q for the prediction; this page writes p, as in the SimSiam section.

BYOL's loss: the squared distance between the unit-length prediction and the unit-length target, added up over both directions.
L = ‖p̄1 − z̄2‖2 = 2 − 2·⟨p̄1, z̄2⟩
Read it as: the loss L for one direction is the squared length, ‖ ‖2, of the gap between p̄1, view 1's prediction scaled to unit length, and z̄2, view 2's target scaled to unit length. The angle brackets ⟨ , ⟩ are the dot product, which for two unit vectors is the cosine of the angle between them: 1 when they point the same way. So the loss is 0 when prediction and target agree and grows to 4 when they point in opposite directions. Only the prediction gets gradients; the target is a fixed answer for this step.
LBYOL = L + L̃
L̃, L with a tilde, is the same loss with the views swapped: view 2's prediction against view 1's target. BYOL adds the two, so the total runs from 0 to 8, and every image teaches the online network twice per step.

The second rule says how the target follows. After every training step its weights move a small step toward the online network's weights.

The slow copy: after each step the target keeps most of its old weights and takes a sliver of the online weights.
ξ ← m·ξ + (1 − m)·θ
ξ (xi) are the target network's weights and θ (theta) the online network's weights; the arrow ← means replace with. The new target keeps the share m, the decay, of its old self and takes the rest, 1 − m, from the online network. This is an exponential moving average (EMA): old weights fade out a little every step, so the target is a smoothed history of the online network rather than a copy of it.

The target barely moves: m starts at 0.996, so 99.6% of the target stays each step, and it rises to 1 by the end of training (BYOL §3.3). MoCo, the contrastive method that keeps a queue of old embeddings, updates its key encoder with the same rule, also written with m, in A queue of old embeddings replaces the giant batch, on Contrastive Learning.

In code, the loss is a few lines. In byol_loss, the cosine is computed by normalizing both vectors and taking their dot product, and the last line adds the two directions, as in the card's second line.

def byol_loss(p, z):                             # p, z: [N, D]
    p, z = F.normalize(p, dim=1), F.normalize(z, dim=1)
    return (2 - 2 * (p * z).sum(dim=1)).mean()   # squared distance on the sphere

p1, p2 = predictor(online(x1)), predictor(online(x2))  # online: encoder + projector
with torch.no_grad():
    z1, z2 = target(x1), target(x2)              # target: encoder + projector, no gradient
loss = byol_loss(p1, z2) + byol_loss(p2, z1)     # sum of both directions, as in BYOL

After the optimizer has updated the online network, the target follows with the moving average, in place and with no gradients:

@torch.no_grad()
def ema_update(online, target, m=0.996):         # m rises to 1 during training
    for p_o, p_t in zip(online.parameters(), target.parameters()):
        p_t.mul_(m).add_(p_o, alpha=1 - m)

No negatives also means no hunger for a huge batch. BYOL's batch-size experiment trains a ResNet-50 (a 50-layer image network) for 300 epochs (an epoch is one pass over the data) on ImageNet (the standard 1000-class photo benchmark), then scores it with a linear probe: a single linear classifier trained on the frozen embeddings, reporting top-1 accuracy, the share of images whose top guess is right. BYOL's accuracy holds from batch 4096 down to 256 and drops only below that, which the paper puts down to the batch normalization layers in the encoder (layers that normalize with statistics of the current batch). SimCLR, the contrastive method whose negatives come from the batch, degrades faster as the batch shrinks (BYOL §5, Fig 3a).

Three parts hold collapse off

Now the real question. Why does this not collapse? With no negatives, why does the model not output the same vector for everything and get zero loss? Three parts work together, and BYOL's ablations remove them one at a time. Setup: the same ResNet-50, 300 epochs on ImageNet, linear-probe top-1 accuracy (BYOL Table 5).

  1. Stop-gradient. The target network never receives gradients: its output is treated as a fixed number when the loss is differentiated. If both networks trained, they could walk together to a constant output; the anchored target makes the online network chase a moving but real target. SimSiam shows this part can be enough on its own.
  2. The predictor. The online path has an extra MLP the target path lacks. It must map one view's projection to the other view's target, which a constant output cannot do well. Remove the predictor, or the target network, and accuracy falls to 0.3% or less: collapse (Table 5b).
  3. The EMA target. The target changes slower than the online network: the online network improves, the target absorbs it slowly, the online network chases again. Bootstrap your own latent; the name is literal. Never updating the target (m = 1, so it stays a random network) gives 18.8%; replacing it with the online network every step (m = 0) gives 0.3%; m = 0.99 gives 72.5% (Table 5a).

Here is what that setup prevents, on twenty toy embeddings over one hundred training steps. With gradients flowing everywhere, every dot drains into one, zero loss and nothing learned; BYOL's stop-gradient plus predictor keeps them spread.

Low loss is not success: both runs end near zero loss, and only the spread shows the collapsed one learned nothing.

SimSiam shows stop-gradient alone prevents collapse

BYOL needs three parts. SimSiam (Simple Siamese networks; a Siamese network runs two inputs through branches that share one set of weights) asks which of them really matter. Setup: one network for both views, so there is no slow copy; no negatives; a batch of 512; and plain SGD, stochastic gradient descent on mini-batches with no special large-batch optimizer (SimSiam §3, §4). An encoder f, here the image network plus a projector, turns each view into z, and a predictor MLP h turns z into a prediction p. Each view's prediction is compared with the other view's z, and that z sits behind a stop-gradient, written sg.

SimSiam's loss: each view's prediction should point the same way as the other view's output, with that output held fixed.
L = 12D(p1, sg(z2)) + 12D(p2, sg(z1))
Read it as: the loss L is half of D for view 1's prediction p1 against view 2's output z2, plus half of the same with the views swapped. sg( ) is the stop-gradient: whatever is inside it counts as a constant, so gradients flow only through the prediction. Averaging the two directions keeps the loss symmetric, where BYOL adds them.
D(p, z) = −p‖p‖2·z‖z‖2
D is the negative cosine similarity. ‖p‖2 is the length of p, so p divided by its length is p scaled to unit length, and the same for z. The dot · between two unit vectors is their cosine, and the minus sign turns agree into a low loss. D runs from −1, same direction, to 1, so the smallest possible L is −1 (SimSiam §3).

In code (SimSiam Algorithm 1), the stop-gradient is the detach call, which hands back z with its gradient path cut, and the cosine comes from F.cosine_similarity.

def D(p, z):                                    # negative cosine, stop-gradient on z
    return -F.cosine_similarity(p, z.detach(), dim=-1).mean()
z1, z2 = f(x1), f(x2)                           # f: encoder + projector, [N, 2048]
p1, p2 = h(z1), h(z2)                           # h: predictor MLP
loss = D(p1, z2) / 2 + D(p2, z1) / 2            # minimum is -1

Setup for the finding: ResNet-50 on ImageNet, 100 epochs of pre-training, scored by linear probe. Without the stop-gradient the loss falls straight to −1, its minimum, and accuracy is 0.1%, chance level for 1000 classes; with it, 67.7% (SimSiam §4.1, Fig 2). Without the predictor, accuracy is also 0.1% (Table 1). So the slow copy is not what prevents collapse here: a stop-gradient plus a predictor is enough.

DINO matches a student to a centered, sharpened teacher

DINO (self-distillation with no labels) borrows the language of knowledge distillation, where a student network learns to copy the outputs of a teacher network. Here there is no trained teacher to start from; the teacher is built from the student itself, which is what self-distillation means. Setup: student and teacher share one architecture, and the teacher is an EMA of the student, the same rule as BYOL's target with m rising from 0.996 to 1 (DINO §3.1, Fig 2, Algorithm 1). Each network outputs K numbers per image. A softmax turns those K numbers into K positive shares that add up to 1, and dividing the numbers by a temperature before the softmax sets how peaked the shares are: a low temperature lets one share dominate, a high one flattens them. The student is trained so its shares match the teacher's, scored by cross entropy, which is low when the student puts its probability where the teacher puts its own.

DINO has no predictor and no negatives. It stops collapse with two edits to the teacher's output, made before the teacher's softmax.

DINO's teacher: subtract a running center, then sharpen with a low temperature.
Pt(x) = softmax(gt(x) − cτt)
Read it as: the teacher's shares Pt for an image x come from its K raw outputs gt(x), minus the center c, divided by the teacher temperature τt, then passed through the softmax. Subtracting c is centering; dividing by a small τt is sharpening. The student's shares use the same softmax on its own outputs, divided by its own temperature τs, with no centering.
c ← β·c + (1 − β)·1BΣi=1B gt(xi)
The center c is a moving average of the teacher's mean output. Σi=1B adds up the teacher outputs gt(xi) for the B images xi in the batch, and 1 over B turns the sum into a mean. The new c keeps the share β of the old one, the same moving-average shape as the slow copy (DINO Eq. 4). DINO's paper writes m here; this page uses β so that m keeps meaning the slow-copy decay.
gt: the teacher's K raw outputs; Pt: its K sharesc: the center, K numbersτt: teacher temperature, 0.04 rising to 0.07 over the first 30 epochsτs: student temperature, 0.1β: how much of the old center staysB: the batch size

Each edit alone would collapse, in opposite directions. Centering stops one output from dominating for every image, but on its own pushes the teacher toward a flat output, the same even spread for every image. Sharpening does the opposite and lets one output dominate. Together they balance, and with the slowly updated teacher that is enough to avoid collapse (DINO §3.1, §5.3).

In code (DINO Algorithm 1), H is the cross entropy between the centered, sharpened teacher and the student, taken in both view orders, and the last line updates the center. tps and tpt are τs and τt, and beta is β.

def H(t, s, c, tps=0.1, tpt=0.04):                # t, s: [N, K] teacher and student outputs
    t = F.softmax((t.detach() - c) / tpt, dim=1)  # center, then sharpen
    s = F.log_softmax(s / tps, dim=1)
    return -(t * s).sum(dim=1).mean()
loss = H(t1, s2, c) / 2 + H(t2, s1, c) / 2        # teacher on one view, student on the other
c = beta * c + (1 - beta) * torch.cat([t1, t2]).mean(dim=0)

DINO describes itself as self-distillation with no labels (DINO abstract). Distillation with a trained teacher, including self-distillation, is on Knowledge Distillation.

The three methods side by side. Each one puts a stop-gradient on the target side; what differs is the rest.
