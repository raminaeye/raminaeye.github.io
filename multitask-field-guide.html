Multitask Learning Field Guide

Which method for which problem, what it costs, and where the field goes next.

On this page

This page is where the series turns practical: which method do I reach for, and what does it cost me? It starts with diagnosis, because medicating before you diagnose is how you end up running PCGrad on a scale problem, and it ends with the two decisions every deployment faces: how to batch the data, and which losses earn a place in shared training. Then it steps back one last time: the one-page map of everything the series taught, the newer methods worth knowing by name, what no optimizer can fix, and where the field goes next.

Diagnose before you medicate

Three lines of logging tell you which of the three diseases you actually have. Only then do you pick the medicine.

  1. Each task's loss, compared with training that task alone. A gap here is negative transfer, by definition. The other two measurements explain why the gap is there.
  2. Each task's gradient norm at a shared layer (the size of that task's gradient on the shared trunk). If one is orders of magnitude bigger than the others, you have a scale problem, and the fix is GradNorm or uncertainty weighting.
  3. The cosine between each pair of task gradients. Often negative means a direction problem, and the fix is PCGrad or CAGrad. In the toy landscape from the CAGrad page, once plain gradient descent was trapped in the ravine, the two gradients kept fighting for the rest of the run. The conflict never resolves itself.

Both measurements are one line of arithmetic, but the intuition comes first. A gradient norm is just the length of a task's arrow on the shared trunk: how loudly that task speaks in the update. If one task is far louder than the rest, its arrow decides every step. Here is the check, worked with real numbers:

  1. Measure the norms at the shared layer: task A's is \(12.4\), task B's is \(0.31\).
  2. Divide: \(\frac{\|g_A\|}{\|g_B\|} = \frac{12.4}{0.31} = 40\).
  3. Read it: task A shouts forty times louder than task B, so in the plain sum B's gradient is a rounding error. That is the scale disease, and the fix is GradNorm or uncertainty weighting.

For direction, I want one number that says whether two arrows pull together or fight. That number is the cosine of the angle \(\phi\) between them: \(1\) means the same direction, \(0\) means perpendicular, \(-1\) means exactly opposed. The formula divides their agreement (the dot product) by their lengths:

$$\cos \phi = \frac{\langle g_1, g_2 \rangle}{\|g_1\| \, \|g_2\|}$$

One worked example, one computation per line:

  1. Write the gradients: \(g_1 = (3, 1)\), \(g_2 = (-2, -0.5)\).
  2. Dot product: \(3 \times (-2) + 1 \times (-0.5) = -6 - 0.5 = -6.5\).
  3. Norms: \(\|g_1\| = \sqrt{9 + 1} = \sqrt{10} \approx 3.16\); \(\|g_2\| = \sqrt{4 + 0.25} = \sqrt{4.25} \approx 2.06\).
  4. Multiply the norms: \(3.16 \times 2.06 \approx 6.52\).
  5. Divide: \(\frac{-6.5}{6.52} \approx -1.00\).
  6. Read it: nearly exactly opposed. Their average is a coin flip decided by whoever is louder. That is the direction disease, and the fix is PCGrad or CAGrad.

The same three diseases show up as symptoms first. This is the table I keep taped to the wall:

SymptomLikely causeFirst remedy

Pitfalls worth avoiding

Five mistakes I have watched teams make, including mine:

  1. Watching only the average loss. A great average can hide a task that was quietly sacrificed. I always report per-task numbers too, ideally the average per-task drop against single-task baselines.
  2. Cutting gradients in the task-specific heads. Heads share no parameters, so there is nothing to fight over. Combine gradients on the shared trunk only.
  3. Forgetting GradNorm's renormalization. Without it, the weights slowly change the overall learning speed, and the comparison against baselines stops meaning anything.
  4. Running full K-task surgery on a 50-task problem. K backward passes per step is the real price of PCGrad and CAGrad. Use task subsampling (CAGrad-Fast) or a fast method.
  5. Treating every conflict as poison. The tragic triad says conflict mostly hurts when it comes with a loudness gap and high curvature. Mild conflict between equals is often harmless.

Questions from real deployments

"Our tasks have disjoint datasets. Does this still apply?" Yes, and this is the most common misconception I run into. Every task can have its own dataset and its own head; the gradients still meet in one place, the shared backbone. Conflict is a property of gradients on shared parameters, not of shared data. The per-task dataset, per-task head, shared-trunk setup is the standard case these methods were built on. They would only become irrelevant if the tasks shared nothing at all, and then it would not be multitask learning. To convince a skeptic, log the cosine similarity between pairs of task gradients on the shared trunk for a few hundred steps. If the cosines go negative, and they almost always do, the conflict is real.

"What if one task's batch is much smaller?" A small batch makes a task's gradient jittery: averaged over 8 examples instead of 128, the direction swings step to step, even though it is honest on average. Plain averaging just absorbs the noise. GradNorm occasionally turns a knob on a bad reading but self-corrects. PCGrad sometimes cuts against a fight that was not real, which is wasteful, not dangerous. CAGrad is the most sensitive: its whole decision is "who is worst off right now," and a jittery signal sometimes makes the small-batch task look worst-off when it simply had an unlucky batch. The leash caps how far one noisy step can pull the update. The fixes, in rough order of importance:

  1. Oversample the small task. Going from 8 to 32 samples per step already helps a lot. No algorithmic patch beats a steadier signal.
  2. Smooth the decision signal. Keep an exponential moving average of each task's gradient (decay 0.9 to 0.99), decide on the smoothed gradients, step with the real ones. This composes cleanly with CAGrad: it keeps the protection and the guarantee but stops reacting to one unlucky batch.
  3. Tighten the leash. If you cannot oversample, use a smaller c (0.3 to 0.5). Every step stays closer to the plain average.
  4. Log the variance, not just the average. If the small task's gradient norm swings wildly over a few dozen steps, you have confirmed a shaky signal before it confuses the algorithm.

One more note on this one: GradVac versus CAGrad are not competitors here. GradVac is a direction-fixer with a memory. It smooths the conflict signal but has no opinion about which task deserves help, and it carries no guarantee. CAGrad actively protects the worst-off task and proves it makes progress. If your small-batch task is also your struggling task, which is common, CAGrad addresses the real problem. The best arrangement I know combines them: CAGrad as the decision-maker, GradVac-style averaging inside the measurements it decides on.

How to batch the data

Three layouts, and only one of them lets the methods do their job:

  1. Joint batch: all tasks every step. This is the standard, and my recommendation. Each task produces a loss and a gradient on the shared trunk at the same weights at the same moment, which is exactly what "do these gradients fight?" and "which task is worst off?" require.
  2. Round robin: one task per step. The methods become no-ops: no pair to de-conflict, no worst-off task to find. Worse, round robin does not remove the conflict. It spreads it across time, as the backbone zigzags between tasks, the same pacing that traps plain gradient descent. It removes the diagnostic, not the disease.
  3. Random task per step. Same problem as round robin, shuffled. Incompatible with gradient-combining methods.

Within a joint batch, I prefer equal shares per task, oversampling small datasets so every gradient is measured with equal reliability. The whole point is to let the gradients decide the weighting, which only works if every gradient is trustworthy. If repeating a small dataset worries you, cap the repetition (for example, no task repeats its data more than three times per epoch of the largest) rather than shrinking its share. And if a joint batch does not fit in memory, accumulate: run each task's sub-batch, store its gradient, and combine once all tasks have reported. PCGrad and CAGrad need all task gradients before combining anyway, so accumulation changes where you pay the memory cost, not whether you pay it.

Which losses to bring

A common situation: each team arrives with its own mature single-task model, trained with half a dozen losses. Should all of them come into multitask training? No. The backbone is the only shared, one-shot resource in the whole system. After multitask training, a team takes the backbone, freezes it, and fine-tunes its own head privately, where it can use whatever losses it wants. So each loss faces one question: does this loss need to shape the shared features, or can it do its job later, on the frozen backbone, inside my own head?

Extra losses are expensive: every added loss is another gradient pulling the trunk; the cost is paid by every team, not just the one who brought it; and the loss weights a team tuned for its single-task model no longer mean anything once GradNorm's knobs or CAGrad's leash are involved. The practical filter is the frozen-head test:

  1. Baseline. Multitask-train with each task contributing exactly one loss, its main one. Measure every task's metric.
  2. Frozen-head check. Freeze the backbone; each team fine-tunes its head with its full private stack. Measure again.
  3. Gap analysis. If frozen-head fine-tuning reaches the target, the extra losses were head-level and stay private. If the metric is stuck no matter what the head does, the backbone never learned something the task needs, and that loss belongs in multitask training.
  4. Admit one loss at a time, and re-run. Confirm it closes the gap, and check the other tasks' metrics did not degrade.

With 4 losses there are 6 pairwise relationships to manage; with 8 there are 28. The thin red average is usually dragged toward the loudest few. This is the cost the whole page has been warning about: each extra loss is paid by every team's trunk. Bring fewer, better losses.

Tips that save real time: start with one loss per task, no exceptions. The minimal baseline is the control group that makes every later decision measurable. Freeze the backbone before you argue. Keep the receipt when a loss joins: the other tasks' metrics and the conflict rate, before and after. Pre-combine a task's main and auxiliary losses with fixed weights from its single-task tuning history, so the multitask trainer sees one voice per task. And let the conflict measurements referee team debates. Re-run the frozen-head check after big changes. The verdict on a loss is not permanent.

The practical half is done: three diseases, five methods, one flowchart, a batching rule, and a test for which losses earn their place. Now the view from above. Every method in this series makes one of two moves. It either changes how the losses are mixed before any gradient is computed, or it operates on the gradient vectors themselves. Nothing else. Uncertainty weighting and GradNorm live in the first camp; MGDA, PCGrad, GradVac, and CAGrad live in the second. That split is the whole map of the field's optimization half, and the map below is how I keep it straight. It is the last tool of the series, and it is a directory: every method links back to its own page.

Newer methods: a one-line tour

Research has kept moving since the core methods in this series were published, and the newer arrivals mostly remix the same two families with different machinery. Here is my one-line tour of the ones I think are worth knowing by name:

MethodIdea in one line
GradDropRandomly drops gradient entries where tasks disagree about the sign.
IMTL-GLooks for an update that helps every task by the same amount.
RotoGradRotates and rescales task gradients to make them more alike.
Nash-MTLTreats the update as a bargaining game among the tasks.
FAMOBalances tasks with almost no extra cost per step, which makes many-task problems cheap.

Different machinery, same core ideas as this series: scales, speeds, and directions. None of them escape the two-family map above; they just pick different spots inside it.

What none of them can fix

This is the part I consider just as important as the methods. Gradient-level fixes redistribute a shortage; they do not remove it. If the shared trunk is simply too small to hold all the tasks, or the tasks genuinely work against each other, no optimizer can fix that. The limit is then set by the architecture (wider trunks, softer sharing, task-specific modules) and by task grouping (which tasks should share a trunk at all).

Optimization methods decide how well I use the sharing I chose; they cannot choose it for me. When a multitask setup is failing, my first question is no longer which optimizer to try. It is whether the tasks belong together and whether the trunk has room for them. The optimizer is the second question, not the first.

Where it goes from here

The open problems I take seriously are the ones this series does not solve. First, objectives beyond the plain average: letting users set priorities instead of pretending every task matters equally. Second, guarantees that hold with noisy gradients and many tasks, not just the clean two-task toy setups. Third, principled ways to combine weighting, surgery, and architecture choices instead of treating them as three separate literatures that never talk to each other.

That is where I would put my effort if I were starting a project in this space today. The series ends here; the field does not.

The whole series in one paragraph

The one-paragraph summary. Multitask training fails quietly, in three ways: one task can be so loud it drowns the rest (scale), tasks can learn at different speeds so a fixed balance is always wrong for someone (speed), and tasks can pull in opposite directions (direction). GradNorm fixes the first two by learning a volume knob per task. PCGrad fixes the third by cutting the part of each gradient that fights another task. CAGrad fixes the third with a guarantee: help the worst-off task as much as possible without straying far from the average, and you provably keep improving the loss you set out to minimize. Diagnose with gradient norms and cosines, then pick your medicine.

Glossary

Argmin / argmaxThe knob position that gives the best score, not the score itself. min of f(x) is the lowest value f ever reaches; argmin of f(x) is the x that gets you there.
Conflicting gradientsTwo task gradients that point against each other (angle wider than 90 degrees, cosine negative). Following their sum can push one task's loss up.
Convex combination / simplexA blend where every weight is at least 0 and the weights sum to 1. Each task gets a percentage of the mix. With two tasks: w times g1 + (1-w) times g2. The simplex is the set of all legal weight lists.
Dot productMultiply matching entries and add: the dot product of (3,1) and (-1,2) is -3+2 = -1. Its sign is an agreement meter: positive means pulling together, zero means perpendicular, negative means opposed.
GradientThe arrow that points straight uphill on the loss landscape; training follows its negative downhill.
Gradient normThe length of a gradient: how "loudly" a task speaks in the shared update. A task with a 300x larger norm gets 300x more say.
Gradient Vaccine (GradVac)A generalization of PCGrad: instead of pushing every conflicting pair to cosine zero, each task pair gets its own target cosine (a running average of its historical alignment), and gradients are nudged back to that target whenever they sag below it.
Hard parameter sharingOne shared trunk, small task-specific heads. The trunk's parameters receive every task's gradient, which is where conflicts happen.
Inverse training rateGradNorm's progress meter: the fraction of the way a task has come down, current loss divided by initial loss. A value of 0.25 means three quarters of the journey is done.
Linear scalarizationThe obvious multitask recipe: squash all the losses into one number, a weighted sum, and run ordinary gradient descent on it.
Lipschitz smoothness (H)A budget on curvature: the gradient changes at a rate of at most H as you move, so step sizes up to 1/H are provably safe. Used by CAGrad's guarantee.
MGDAMultiple-gradient descent algorithm: each step descends along the shortest legal blend of the task gradients. Guaranteed to converge to some Pareto-stationary point, but which one depends on where you started.
Negative transferWhen training tasks together makes them worse than training them separately. The disease this whole series is about.
NormThe length of a vector: the norm of (3,1) is the square root of (9+1), about 3.16.
Normal planeAll the directions perpendicular to one gradient. Moving along them neither helps nor hurts that task, which is why PCGrad projects conflicting gradients onto it.
Pareto-optimal / Pareto setA point where no task can improve without another getting worse. The set of all such points: the menu of all fair trade-offs.
Pareto-stationaryA point where some blend of the task gradients cancels out to exactly zero, the multitask version of "gradient equals zero." Every Pareto-optimal point is Pareto-stationary, but not the other way around.
ProjectionThe shadow one vector casts on another's line. Subtracting the shadow leaves the perpendicular part.
Tragic triadThe PCGrad paper's name for the three ingredients that make conflict truly harmful: gradients that fight, one gradient much louder than the other, and a sharply curved landscape.
Trust region (CAGrad's ball)The set of update directions within distance c times the average gradient's norm of the average gradient. The leash CAGrad puts on its correction.
VectorA list of numbers, pictured as an arrow. A model's parameters form one very long vector (millions of entries), and a gradient is an arrow of the same length.
