k-means gives every point one cluster and every cluster a circle. A Gaussian mixture gives each point a share in every cluster and lets clusters be tilted ellipses.

1 Hard circles or soft ellipses

A Gaussian mixture model (GMM) describes the data as a weighted sum of Gaussian bumps, gives each point a share in every bump, fits the bumps with the EM loop from Maximum Likelihood and EM, and picks the number of bumps with a score that charges for extra parameters.

2 A mixture adds weighted bumps

A GMM describes data as a weighted sum of Gaussians. Each Gaussian, a component, has a center, a covariance and a weight, and EM picks the values that maximize the likelihood of the data. EM runs once over the training set; after that, scoring a new point is one pass over the K components.

The mixture’s density at a point x is a weighted sum of bell curves, one per component. K is the number of components.
p(x) = Σk wk N(x | μk, Σk)
k numbers the components, and the plain Σk adds over all K of them. The bold Σk is component k’s covariance, its width along each axis and its tilt (see Probability and Bayes Rule). N(x | μk, Σk) is a bell centered at μk. wk is component k’s mixing weight, the fraction of the data component k explains. The weights add to 1, so the sum still has area 1. A larger wk makes its bump taller without moving it, and where bumps overlap their heights add, which is how two humps form one curve.

3 Each point gets a share, by Bayes rule

Which component made a point? Bayes rule answers with shares: each bump’s height at the point, times its weight, rescaled to add to 1.

Bayes rule splits each point among the components, in proportion to how well each one explains the point and how common it is.
aik = wk N(xi | μk, Σk)
i numbers the points. aik is the height of bump k at point xi, times how common k is: the likelihood times the prior, the top of Bayes rule.
rik = aikΣj aij
rik, the responsibility, is the share of point i given to component k. The bottom adds the same score over every component j, so each point’s shares add to 1. A point deep inside one bump goes almost wholly to it; a point between two bumps is split, which lets the ellipses overlap.

4 Fitting the mixture with EM

The shares need the bumps and the bumps need the shares, the loop EM solves. The E step computes every rik; the M step refits each component from the points counted at their shares.

Figure 4. Toy data like the Old Faithful geyser, eruption length against waiting time, in minutes.

One EM round computes every point’s shares from the current ellipses, then refits each ellipse from the points counted at those shares.
rik = aikΣj aij
E step. The shares are recomputed from this round’s centers, ellipses and weights; the M step reads nothing else.
μk = 1Nk Σi rik xi
M step. Nk is the sum of every point’s share in k, the number of points component k holds. The new center is the share-weighted average of the points, the plain average of a bell fit with each point counted at its share, so a point that k barely claims barely pulls its center.
Σk = 1Nk Σi rik
(xi − μk)(xi − μk)T
The new ellipse averages each point’s gap from the center times that same gap, at the point’s share. The raised T is the transpose, which makes that product a 2 by 2 table of variances and tilt. Points that line up along a diagonal have gaps of the same sign on both axes, and that tilts the ellipse along them.
wk = NkN
The new weight is the average share over all N points, so a component that holds more of the data gets a taller bump in the next E step.

This snippet runs one EM round in NumPy on two toy blobs and prints the new weights and means; after the starting guess, its lines follow the card from top to bottom.

import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import multivariate_normal as mvn
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
blob_a = rng.normal([-1, -1], 0.5, (100, 2))
blob_b = rng.normal([1, 1], 0.5, (150, 2))
X = np.vstack([blob_a, blob_b])
w = np.array([0.5, 0.5])
mu = np.array([[-0.5, 0.0], [0.5, 0.0]])
Sigma = np.array([np.eye(2), np.eye(2)])
K = len(w)
bells = [mvn(mu[k], Sigma[k]) for k in range(K)]
a = np.column_stack([w[k] * bells[k].pdf(X) for k in range(K)])
r = a / a.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)
N_k = r.sum(axis=0)
mu = r.T @ X / N_k[:, None]
covs = [np.cov(X.T, aweights=r[:, k], bias=True) for k in range(K)]
Sigma = np.stack(covs)
w = N_k / len(X)
print(w.round(2), mu.round(2))

  3. 3Computes the scores aik, eq card in Section 3, line 1, then the E-step shares rik, eq card in Section 4, line 1.
  4. 4Moves each mean μk, eq card in Section 4, line 2.
  5. 5Refits each Σk, eq card in Section 4, lines 3 and 4.
  6. 6Resets the weights wk, eq card in Section 4, line 5.

5 k-means is the hard-label limit

Give every component the same round covariance, with its standard deviation shrinking toward zero. Each share becomes 0 or 1 for the nearest center, and the center update becomes k-means’. k-means never estimates a covariance, hence its circles.

Figure 5. Narrow bumps give the dark point to the nearer center, like k-means.

6 Covariance shapes trade flexibility for parameters

A full covariance lets every component tilt and stretch freely, at three numbers per component in 2D. Cheaper options constrain it; scikit-learn names them with covariance_type.

The long axes of each ellipse are the directions PCA (principal component analysis) would find inside that cluster.

7 How many components? Let BIC decide

More components always raise the likelihood, so the likelihood alone cannot pick K. The Bayesian information criterion (BIC) can.

BIC adds a penalty for every parameter to minus twice the log-likelihood, and the number of components with the lowest score wins.
BIC = −2 log L + m log N
L is the fit’s likelihood, so −2 log L falls as the fit improves. m is the number of free parameters (weights, centers and covariance entries). Each parameter costs log N, so the penalty grows slowly with the data. A new component always lowers −2 log L a little but raises m by a whole block, so past the real number of clumps the penalty wins and BIC climbs again.

This snippet fits 1 to 6 components to the same two blobs and prints each BIC; look for the smallest.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.mixture import GaussianMixture
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
blob_a = rng.normal([-1, -1], 0.5, (100, 2))
blob_b = rng.normal([1, 1], 0.5, (150, 2))
X = np.vstack([blob_a, blob_b])
for K in range(1, 7):
    gm = GaussianMixture(n_components=K, random_state=0).fit(X)
    print(K, gm.bic(X))  # lowest wins

With two separate blobs, expect the smallest BIC at 2.

8 Gaussian mixtures in practice

In scikit-learn a mixture sits after a scaler; fit runs EM once on the training points, and predict_proba or score reads new points.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.mixture import GaussianMixture
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
short = rng.normal([2.0, 55], [0.3, 6], (100, 2))
long_ = rng.normal([4.3, 80], [0.4, 6], (172, 2))
X = np.vstack([short, long_])
gm = GaussianMixture(n_components=2, covariance_type="full", random_state=0)
model = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), gm)
model.fit(X)
new = [[2.1, 54.0], [4.0, 79.0]]
print(model.predict_proba(new).round(2))  # shares r_ik
print(model.score(new), gm.n_iter_)  # mean log-likelihood, rounds

9 Where GMMs are used

In speaker verification, the classic use, Reynolds et al. (2000) fit a large background GMM to speech from many people and adapt each speaker’s model from it. Each short frame becomes a vector of MFCCs, numbers describing its spectrum shape (see Audio Features), and both models score the frames.

A fitted mixture scores every new point (score_samples); a very low score flags an anomaly. Classic speech recognizers used a mixture per hidden state to score each frame (Rabiner 1989, eq. 49), as in Hidden Markov Models.

10 Common traps

  • A component can collapse onto one point and send the likelihood to infinity; reg_covar prevents it.
  • EM finds a local peak; raise n_init to try several starts.
  • Unscaled features bend every ellipse; standardize first, shifting each feature to mean 0 and scaling it to standard deviation 1.
  • Component names are arbitrary, so K components give K! (K factorial) equal answers (label switching).
  • Too many components overfit; let BIC choose.

11 What if the hidden cluster changes over time?

When the hidden cluster follows a chain over time, as words in speech or a casino’s dice do, the model is a hidden Markov model.
