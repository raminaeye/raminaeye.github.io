Introduction to Multitask Learning

One model, several tasks sharing one trunk of layers: three ways training it fails and the geometry that tells them apart.

One model learning several tasks should be a bargain: shared features, less compute, each task teaching the others. The obvious way to train it breaks in three different ways that look alike from the outside, and the geometry of the task gradients (each task's uphill direction on its loss) tells you which one you have.

One model, many tasks

What if the best way to learn one thing is to learn several things at once?

Most machine learning does one thing at a time: one dataset, one loss, one skill. Multitask learning does the opposite on purpose. One model, several tasks, and the bet is that each task helps the others learn.

The bet is old by deep learning standards. Back in 1997, Rich Caruana showed something striking with a robot's camera. A network learning two jobs from images of doors, finding the doorknob and telling a single door from a double door, made about 24 percent fewer errors on the doorknob and 31 percent fewer on the door type when the same network was also trained to locate the door's edges and frame. The extra tasks pushed the network toward the features that really matter, edges and frames and handles, and away from the noise. Thirty years of vision, language, and robotics work has kept rediscovering the same lesson.

Hard parameter sharing

The workhorse design has a name: hard parameter sharing. One shared trunk learns to make sense of the input, and a small task-specific head per task turns that understanding into an answer. Ruder's 2017 overview calls it the most common way to do multitask learning in neural networks.

In symbols, the trunk's parameters are θsh, shared by everyone. Each head gets its own private parameters θi. With K tasks (three in the diagram in “Where each task pulls the shared trunk”), each task reports its own loss Li on one training batch, the handful of examples the model sees in one step. The optimizer, the routine that updates the parameters, works on one number built from them: the losses added up. The equation for that sum is in “Add the weighted losses”.

One number is all the optimizer sees. And that single number's gradient, the direction in which it rises fastest, pushes on every head plus the one shared trunk. That shared push is the whole idea. It is also, as you are about to see, the whole problem.

Why sharing one trunk helps

  1. It is cheaper. One trunk instead of K separate models means less memory, less compute, and one model to deploy. A self-driving car's vision system cannot afford ten networks per frame.
  2. Tasks teach each other. Labels for one task quietly act as training signal for the others. Knowing the depth of a scene, how far away each part of it is, helps with segmentation, labeling each part of an image by what it shows; knowing the segmentation helps with surface orientation. Vandenhende and coauthors (2022) survey multitask networks built for exactly these per-pixel tasks.
  3. The model learns better features. A trunk that must please many tasks is pushed toward features that are useful in general. It is less likely to overfit, memorizing one dataset's quirks instead of learning patterns that still hold on new data. The extra tasks act like a built-in safety net.

Once you know what to look for, this design is everywhere: scene understanding (one network predicts segmentation, depth, and surface normals, the direction each point on a surface faces), robot control (one policy, the rule the robot uses to pick its actions, handles 10 to 50 tasks), multilingual language models (dozens of languages), and recommendation systems that must care about clicks, watch time, and satisfaction all at once. The surveys by Crawshaw (2020) and by Zhang and Yang (2022) cover many more of these uses.

Tasks fight over the shared trunk

Train the tasks together in the obvious way, just add the losses and go, and you will often find that every task does worse than it would have on its own. The shared trunk is not a free lunch. It is a shared resource, and the tasks fight over it. Maninis and coauthors (2019) call this task interference, and one of their fixes keeps any single task's gradients from dominating the shared trunk.

Add the weighted losses

The naive recipe takes two lines, the total loss every task shares and the step that trains every parameter on it.
L(θ) = Σi wi Li(θ)
The total loss L(θ) is every task's loss added up, each one scaled by its volume knob wi, the task's loss weight: setting a knob to 2 makes that task count double, and with every knob at 1 this is the plain sum from “Hard parameter sharing”. Σi is the sum over every task i, and θ is all of the model's parameters, the shared trunk θsh plus every head θi.
θ ← θ − η ∇L(θ)
Read ← as “gets replaced by”. Each training step replaces the model's parameters θ with themselves minus a scaled copy of the gradient. η, the Greek letter eta, is the step size (also called the learning rate), a small positive number that sets how far each step moves, and ∇L(θ) is the gradient, the arrow pointing straight uphill on the total loss. It points uphill however steep the slope is; the steepness only sets how long it is. Subtracting it heads the step downhill.

Everything on this page works on this one sum, and the step in the second line is the one piece of the recipe every later method keeps. The catch sits inside the first line. If one task's loss towers over the other's, the sum listens to the loud task almost exclusively, and the quiet one is a rounding error. Here a loud task is one whose loss is large.

That is not a bug in the arithmetic; it is the naive recipe working exactly as written. And the fix is not obvious. To give the quiet task an equal vote you have to raise its weight by however many times quieter it is, and the right number changes as training proceeds, because the losses themselves move.

Three different failures, one shared symptom

With equal weights, “train on the sum” is what most codebases do first. Sometimes it works. Often it quietly fails. One task stalls far behind what it could do alone, or all of them get worse. When the multitask model loses to separate single-task models, researchers call it negative transfer. The failure is usually diagnosed from a loss curve, but the loss curve hides which of three different failures you actually have. They look alike from the outside and demand different fixes.

Scale: the loud task wins every vote

A depth loss near 10³ (a thousand) and a classification loss near 1 are just numbers to gradient descent, the routine that trains the model by stepping it downhill, against the gradient. The task with big numbers produces the big gradient, which swamps the others. Hand-tuning weights means searching K knobs, and doing it again every time the task set changes.

That is the stacked bar in “In the naive sum, the larger loss is almost the whole total”, where the loud task owns almost the whole sum at equal weights. Nothing about the loud task is more important here. It is just louder.

Tasks learn at different speeds

One task may be nearly solved in the first 10% of training while another improves until the end. The fast task's gradients shrink as it finishes, so the weighting that was fair at step 100 is unfair at step 10,000. The balance must move with training.

Picture two tasks whose gradients start the same size. The fast task's gradient shrinks quickly as it learns, while the slow task's barely shrinks, so a little way into training the slow task's gradient is several times larger. Weights that gave the two tasks equal shares at the start now hand most of every update to the slow task. That is why a weighting tuned early in a run can be wrong by the end of it.

Direction: the arrows fight

Two gradients can point against each other, so the average update contains a piece that actively raises one task's loss. The angle between the two arrows measures this, through its cosine: 1 when they point the same way, 0 at right angles, −1 when they point opposite ways. “The math kit: norms, dot products, gradients” builds this score properly.

At 0 degrees the arrows agree fully. Past 90 degrees the cosine turns negative and the arrows fight. At 180 degrees they point opposite ways and the cosine is −1. If task 1's gradient is the longer one, the sum then points task 1's way and pushes task 2's loss up. This is the failure no volume knob can fix. Scaling a gradient changes its length, never its direction.

The math kit: norms, dot products, gradients

All of this was geometry: arrows with sizes and directions, added together, sometimes fighting. The conflict is geometry, and the fixes are geometry too. Every fix does one of three things to gradient arrows: it measures how loud they are, checks how much they agree, or cuts out the part that fights. Those three moves rest on five ideas, each simple once seen the right way: the norm, an arrow's length; the dot product, how much two arrows agree; the projection, one arrow's shadow on another; the gradient, the pull of a loss; and the convex blend, a way to split 100% between arrows.

A vector is just an arrow on a map: (3, 1) means walk 3 east, 1 north. A model's parameters are a vector too, one with millions of entries; I cannot draw that map, but any two arrows in it, and every blend of them, lie in one flat plane. So every fact about two arrows, their lengths and the angle between them, stays true there, which is why every picture here gets away with flat arrows.

The norm is the arrow's length

The norm, written ‖v‖, is the arrow's length. To compute it, square every entry, add the squares up, and take the square root. That is Pythagoras's theorem, applied to as many entries as the arrow has.

A large loss usually comes with a long gradient. Whenever I call a task's gradient “loud”, I mean exactly this number is large. Loudness is a norm.

The dot product is an agreement meter

The dot product, written ⟨u, v⟩, measures how much two arrows agree. To compute it, multiply matching entries and add the results.

The dot product also equals the two lengths times the cosine of the angle between the arrows, so its sign is the verdict: positive means roughly the same direction, zero means exactly perpendicular, negative means they partly oppose each other.

Dividing by the lengths gives the cosine similarity, a scale-free agreement score between −1 and 1. Scale-free because a loud arrow and a quiet arrow pointing the same way score the same. This sign check is the standard conflict test for two task gradients, written g1 and g2. A negative result means the two pulls partly oppose each other, which is not yet the same as a step against their average hurting a task.

Projection is one arrow's shadow on another

The projection of one arrow onto another is the shadow the first arrow casts on the second arrow's line: the part of the first arrow that lies along the second.

To compute it, take the dot product of the two arrows and divide it by the second arrow's squared length. That number says how many copies of the second arrow the shadow holds, so scaling the second arrow by it gives the shadow. Subtract the shadow from the first arrow and the leftover has no part along the second arrow at all. The leftover's dot product with the second arrow is zero for every pair of arrows, so the leftover always sits at right angles to it.

This subtraction is the core move of the direction-fixing methods later in the series. PCGrad, which has its own page, uses it when two task gradients point against each other: it subtracts from one gradient its projection onto the other, so what is left of that gradient no longer pushes against the other task.

A gradient is a pull

The gradient, written ∇L(θ), is the same uphill arrow as in the weighted-losses equation in “Add the weighted losses”. Picture the loss landscape: the loss drawn as hills and valleys over the model's parameters, with height showing how bad the loss is. A map can draw such a hill as contours, lines that join points sitting at the same loss height, so stepping across them is what climbing or descending means. The gradient always points across the contours, straight uphill.

I treat each task's gradient as a pull on the shared parameters. Training follows the opposite pull, straight downhill. Each task has its own landscape and therefore its own pull, and those pulls need not point the same way. That sentence is the seed of every problem in this series.

A convex blend is a way to split 100%

A convex combination is a blend where every weight is at least zero and the weights add to one, so each task gets a percentage of the mix.

Blends matter because blending is how tasks compromise, and one fact makes them useful: a step that lowers every task's loss also lowers every blend of those losses. The pull of a blended loss is the same blend of the task pulls. Turned around, if some blend of the pulls cancels to zero, no step lowers that blend, so no step lowers every task's loss. So to answer “is there a step that lowers every task's loss?”, you do not have to try every possible step. You can search over the few blend weights instead. In the MGDA page I search over these blends, with weights zero or more that add to one, for the shortest arrow.

The dot-product test: does this step help or hurt each task

Picture two tasks sharing one set of parameters. You pick a direction, and training takes one small step against it. You want to know, task by task, whether that step helps or hurts. The answer is one dot product.

Why a dot product? The step is the direction reversed and scaled by the step size, which is positive. Each task's loss falls by roughly the step size times the dot product of that task's gradient with the direction (a negative dot product means it rises): slope times move, coordinate by coordinate. Since the step size is positive, the sign of the dot product is the whole verdict. A positive dot product means the direction points along the task's uphill arrow, so the actual move goes downhill for that task. A negative dot product means the move goes uphill: the task gets worse.

Call that dot product the task's score for the step. The whole test fits in one sentence: whenever some task's dot product with the chosen direction comes out negative, the step makes that task worse. Keep your eye on this dot product. Every method in this series is really just a different way of picking a direction so that no task's score comes out too negative.

Opposing pulls do not always mean a hurt task. Task 2's score for the average of the two pulls is half of ⟨g1, g2⟩ plus half of g2's squared length. So a step against the average raises task 2's loss only when ⟨g1, g2⟩ falls below minus g2's squared length, where the opposing part outweighs task 2's own pull. The same holds for task 1 with g1's squared length.

The average step helps one task, hurts the other

Here is the dot-product test on one concrete pair. Take two tasks that share two parameters, so each gradient is an arrow with two entries; the numbers are made up for the example. Task 1's gradient g1 = (2, 0) points east with length 2. Task 2's gradient g2 = (−1, 0.5) points west and slightly north, with length about 1.1.

Plain training steps against the average of the two arrows, which is (0.5, 0.25). It leans east, toward task 1, because task 1's arrow is the longer one. Scored with the dot product, the average gets +1.00 from task 1 and about −0.38 from task 2. The positive score means the step lowers task 1's loss. The negative score means the very same step raises task 2's loss.

The average follows the longer arrow and pushes the other task's loss up. The MGDA page draws this same pair in “Two tasks, two parameters, one conflict”.

The conflict has a principled answer

When the arrows fight, the plain average just picks a side. The classical answer is MGDA (Multiple Gradient Descent Algorithm), which blends the task gradients into the shortest combination whose weights are all zero or more and add to one, and steps against it. Each of its steps either lowers every task's loss, for a small enough step, or comes out as zero, and it comes out as zero only at a Pareto-stationary point, a spot where some blend of the task pulls cancels, so no single small step lowers every task's loss at once. Every model whose losses sit on the Pareto front, the curve of trade-offs where no task can improve without hurting another, is such a spot. But which spot a run stops at is decided by where you started, not by anything you chose.

The full treatment, the Pareto front, the blend tool, and that catch, is on the MGDA page.

What this series does not solve: priorities, guarantees, and combining the fixes

The open problems I take seriously are the ones this series does not solve. First, objectives beyond the plain average: letting users set priorities instead of pretending every task matters equally. Second, guarantees that hold with noisy gradients and many tasks, not just the clean two-task toy setups. Third, principled ways to combine weighting, gradient surgery (editing the task gradients themselves, as PCGrad does), and architecture choices instead of treating them as three separate literatures that never talk to each other.

The series has answers for each failure; the field still has these three open.

Three failures, and the pages that fix them

Multitask training fails quietly, in three ways: scale, speed, and direction.

Diagnose with gradient norms and cosines, then pick your fix. Norms that differ, or drift apart as training goes on, call for a learned loss weight per task. A negative cosine calls for a method that edits the directions, because no weight can turn an arrow.

Whichever method you pick, the loss scale still shapes the gradients it receives, and the optimizer and gradient clipping, which caps a gradient's length, still act on the combined gradient it produces, as Gradient Balancing Inside the Training Loop shows.

The two weighting methods, the volume knobs that learn each task's loss weight (GradNorm and Uncertainty Weighting), the three steering methods (PCGrad, GradVac, and CAGrad), and MGDA, which lands on the Pareto front, side by side:

MethodThe question it answers
GradNormHow loud should each task be, to keep progress balanced? (learned, driven by training dynamics)
Uncertainty WeightingHow loud should each task be, given how noisy it inherently is? (learned, driven by the data)
PCGradHow should the directions be edited so tasks stop fighting?
GradVacHow should each pair of tasks be nudged back to the agreement level where it learns best?
CAGradHow do we protect the worst-off task and stay near the average, with a guarantee?
MGDAWhat direction helps every task at once, and where does the run land on the Pareto front?
