Decision Trees, Random Forests, and Boosting

Twenty questions, then a forest.

A decision tree splits the data with yes-or-no questions

You know twenty questions. I think of something, you ask yes-or-no questions, and each answer cuts the possibilities down until one is left. A decision tree is that game played by a machine, except the machine picks the questions with math instead of gut feeling.

Each question is a threshold on one feature: is petal width above 1.3? Is petal length to the left of 0.5? Answer yes, go right. Answer no, go left. At the bottom of every path sits a leaf, and the leaf just votes for whatever class showed up most in its corner of the data. Here is the shape of it, using the Iris root split:

Every internal node is one yes-or-no question about a single feature. Every leaf is a majority vote of the training points that landed there. Deeper tree, more questions, finer carving.

How trees pick splits

So how does the machine pick the questions? It tries every feature and every threshold, and keeps the split that makes the children purest. "Purest" needs a number, and there are two classics.

Entropy is surprise, measured in bits. A 50/50 node has 1 bit of entropy, maximum surprise; a pure node has 0, no surprise at all. Information gain is just surprise destroyed: the parent's entropy minus the size-weighted entropy of the children. Splits that separate the classes destroy a lot of surprise and score high; splits that do nothing score zero.

Gini impurity is the lazy cousin, no logarithms. Read it as: pick two points from the node at random, and gini is the chance they disagree. For a 50/50 node that chance is 0.5; for a pure node it is 0. The notebook this page follows computes both by hand on Iris: petal width wins the root split, and the size-weighted gini of its two children comes out to 0.443. Same idea as entropy, cheaper arithmetic, and in practice the trees come out nearly identical.

Then the tree recurses: split the children, split the grandchildren, until a max depth or until every leaf is pure. And here is the gotcha the notebook demonstrates next. A tree with max depth 125 on 2D data keeps splitting until it has memorized the training set, drawing a jagged boundary that tiptoes around every noisy point. The notebook's tree lands at 87.6% test accuracy. It learned the noise. Watch one tree grow below, then the two cures after it.

Watch one tree grow

The forest and the boosting loop are just ways of combining trees. One tree is the whole algorithm, so here it is growing on the same four-blob data the tools below use, one move per step.

Growing one node in one line: pick the split that purifies the most, measured as the parent's impurity minus each side's weighted share.
ΔI = I(parent) − Σc(Nc/N)·I(c)
Score the split: take the parent's impurity and subtract the weighted impurity of the children. Bigger drop means a purer split.

What this shows

  • The point: Growing a tree is four moves: keep the split that purifies the most, cut the data there, repeat on each side, and let pure-enough leaves vote for their majority class.

Forests vote

Cure one: do not trust one tree. Grow twenty, each on a bootstrap resample of the data (same size, drawn with replacement, so each tree sees a slightly different dataset), each capped at a max depth. Then let them vote. One tree's jagged memorization is random; the average of twenty jagged memorizations is smooth. The notebook's forest of 20 trees at depth 5 beats its single deep tree on test data, 89.0% vs 87.6%, and the boundary visibly relaxes.

Boosting corrects

Cure two is the opposite philosophy. A forest grows its trees independently and averages them. Boosting grows its trees in sequence, and each new tree is hired for one job: fix the last round's mistakes.

The recipe, straight from the notebook. Start with the dumbest possible prediction, the mean of the labels. Compute the residuals, truth minus prediction, the signed mistakes. Fit a small tree (depth 3) to the residuals, not the labels. Nudge the prediction toward the tree's output with a small learning rate, and repeat.

Each round the boundary corrects itself exactly where it is still wrong, and the residual field shrinks. The notebook runs 100 rounds at a learning rate of 0.01 with depth-3 trees and lands at 82.7% test accuracy. Below, the same algorithm trains live: the halos are the current residuals, red where the model under-predicts, blue where it over-predicts, fading as each round eats them.

Out-of-bag votes make a free test set

Each tree in the forest sat out about a third of the data: its bootstrap sample never included those points. Every tree therefore comes with a free test set: the points it never saw. The forest's vote on those held-out points is an honest error estimate that costs nothing extra. Breiman (2001) showed it tracks a real held-out test set almost exactly. When trees help and when they do not

The forest on this page votes; its cousins differ in how they combine trees. Extra trees randomize the split thresholds too, which is faster and often just as good. AdaBoost reweights the points the last tree got wrong instead of fitting residuals. Gradient boosting (above) fits the residuals.

XGBoost and LightGBM are the production versions of that idea: histogram splits, regularization on the leaves, and column sampling, which is why they win most tabular competitions.

Ensembles are not the only answer to memorization. Pruning grows the tree deep and then cuts the branches that do not pay for themselves, trading a little training accuracy for a calmer boundary.

The same machine does regression: a leaf predicts the mean of its points instead of a vote.

Reach for trees when the data is tabular with mixed types, when values are missing, or when you need to read the model. They struggle when the true boundary is a smooth diagonal (every question is axis-aligned, so diagonals come out as staircases), when you need predictions past the training range (a leaf can only repeat what it saw), or when you need calibrated probabilities (a leaf is a vote count, not a confidence).
