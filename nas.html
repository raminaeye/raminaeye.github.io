Neural Architecture Search Basics

Search spaces, random search, evolution and reinforcement-learning controllers

  2. One-Shot NAS: Weight-Sharing Supernets

Designing architectures by hand is slow and mostly trial and error: a layer made wider here, a skip connection (a path that carries a layer's input past other layers) added there.

Neural architecture search (NAS) hands this job to an automated search.

NAS runs once, before the final model is trained; the search is its own job (Section 3 has its cost), and its output architecture is then trained from scratch. During the search the strategy keeps a memory: a population for evolution, a controller network for reinforcement learning, or architecture weights for differentiable search (DARTS: Differentiable Architecture Search).

1 Search space, search strategy and evaluation

This page builds on convolutional networks: kernels and feature maps from CNN Basics: Convolution, Stride and Pooling, and the skip connections of ResNet from ResNet and Residual Connections. Every NAS method has the same three pieces (Figure 1).

  • The search space sets what may change: kernel sizes (a kernel is the window of weights a convolution slides over the input), depths, widths, block types, skip connections.
  • The search strategy sets how to explore it: random sampling, evolution, reinforcement learning, or gradients.
  • The evaluation strategy sets how to score a candidate cheaply, without training it until its accuracy stops improving.

Get these wrong and thousands of GPU hours rediscover ResNet, a hand-designed network.

2 How the search works

2.1 The search space

Say each of \(L\) layers picks one op out of \(k\) candidates, from a small set like a \(3\times3\) convolution, a \(5\times5\) convolution, a \(3\times3\) separable convolution, max pooling and a skip that passes its input through, the one Listing A searches. Every added layer multiplies the count by \(k\) again, so even a small space holds far more architectures than any search can visit. The card states that count \(N\) for any \(k\) and \(L\).

The number of architectures is the choices per layer raised to the number of layers.
\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{N} = \htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{k}^{\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{L}}\)
Each of the \(L\) layers chooses independently among \(k\) ops, so every added layer multiplies the count by \(k\). Repeated multiplication is a power, which is why \(N\) grows exponentially with depth: one more layer is \(k\) times more work for brute force.

At that size, brute force is not a plan. So what matters is the search strategy, not the size of the space.

2.2 Four search strategies

  1. Random search samples architectures and keeps the best, the baseline every NAS paper should beat.
  2. Evolution keeps a population, drops the worst and adds a mutated copy of the best; AmoebaNet's aging evolution removes the oldest member instead.
  3. Reinforcement learning trains a controller that proposes architectures and learns from their scores (Section 2.3).
  4. Differentiable search turns the choice into weights that gradient descent can tune (DARTS: Differentiable Architecture Search).

Random search and evolution both fit in a few lines on a toy space of architectures with fixed accuracies; score is a lookup standing in for training, and mutate moves a short step.

import random

def run_search(n_architectures, budget, max_step):
    acc = [random.random() for _ in range(n_architectures)]

    # stands in for training
    def score(a):
        return acc[a]

    def mutate(a):
        step = random.randint(-max_step, max_step)
        # stay inside the space
        return min(n_architectures - 1, max(0, a + step))

    samples = random.choices(range(n_architectures), k=budget)
    return max(samples, key=score)
  1. 1A toy space of architectures; score looks up a stored accuracy instead of training.
  2. 2mutate moves to a nearby architecture, at most a short step away (this page's choice, not a published setting). A larger step explores more but can jump off a good peak; a smaller one refines a peak but can stay stuck on a small one.
  3. 3Random search: score a budget of random picks and keep the best. A bigger budget gets every strategy closer to the best architecture at proportionally more GPU time.

Evolution spends the same budget of architectures to train and score on a population, replacing the worst with a mutated copy of the best. Hill climbing is a population of one that keeps a mutation only if it scores higher.

def run_evolution(score, mutate, n_architectures, budget, population):
    pop = random.choices(range(n_architectures), k=population)
    for _ in range(budget - population):
        # worst first
        pop.sort(key=score)
        pop[0] = mutate(pop[-1])
    return max(pop, key=score)
  1. 1Starts a population of random architectures, which count toward the budget. A larger population explores more peaks for the same budget but improves each one more slowly.
  2. 2Each round replaces the worst member with a mutated copy of the best.
  3. 3Keeps the best architecture found.

2.3 Reinforcement-learning controllers

Reinforcement learning is the NASNet approach. A controller, usually a small RNN (recurrent neural network, which emits its choices one at a time; taught in Recurrent Neural Networks: RNN, GRU, and LSTM), samples architectures; each is trained, and its validation accuracy (on held-out data) comes back as a reward.

The controller is a policy: for each choice it gives every option a probability, written \(\pi(a)\) for the option \(a\) it actually picked, and the reward \(R\) is the child's validation accuracy. The first NAS updates it with REINFORCE against a baseline (step 4 of Figure 5); NASNet uses PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization, which limits how far one update can move the policy). REINFORCE, the log-probability it raises and the baseline it subtracts are taught, with PPO, in Policy-Based and Actor-Critic Methods: REINFORCE, PPO and SAC.

A pick whose child beats the usual reward becomes more likely; one that falls short becomes less likely.
\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=nJ}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{\nabla J}}} \approx \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=R}{\htmlStyle{color:#555}{(R - b)}}} \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=lp}{\htmlStyle{color:#555}{\nabla \log \pi (a)}}}\)
\(J\) is the expected reward, the average accuracy of the children it samples, and \(\nabla J\) is the weight change that raises it fastest. The reward \(R\) minus the running average \(b\) says how much better than usual this child did, and \(\nabla \log \pi(a)\) points toward making the picked op \(a\) more likely. Here the child beats the running average, so every pick goes up. The listing below runs this line as loss = -logp * reward.
\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=lp}{\htmlStyle{color:#555}{\nabla \log \pi (a)}}} = \frac{\nabla \pi (a)}{\pi (a)}\)
The log brings this division. A common op is sampled often, and dividing its push by \(\pi(a)\) stops it gaining just for turning up, so on average the pushes follow \(\nabla J\).
\(\approx\) approximately; \(\nabla\) gradient; \(\pi(a)\) probability of op \(a\)

The listing runs that update without a baseline on a one-layer toy: logits choose among kernels, and a lookup table perf stands in for training.

import torch
from torch.distributions import Categorical

def train_controller(kernel_sizes, rewards, learning_rate, n_steps):
    perf = torch.tensor(rewards)  # stored reward per kernel
    logits = torch.nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(len(kernel_sizes)))
    opt = torch.optim.Adam([logits], lr=learning_rate)
    for step in range(n_steps):
        dist = Categorical(logits=logits)
        action = dist.sample()
        reward = perf[action]
        logp = dist.log_prob(action)
        loss = -logp * reward
        opt.zero_grad()
        loss.backward()
        opt.step()
  1. 1Stored rewards, one per kernel, and the controller's logits over them.
  2. 2The controller samples a kernel and receives its reward.
  3. 3REINFORCE update: raise the log-probability of the sampled kernel in proportion to its reward.

The probability mass drifts toward the best kernel; without a baseline some runs wander first. With real training in place of the lookup, this is the first NAS controller's REINFORCE update in miniature, minus its baseline and its RNN. NASNet trained the same kind of RNN controller with PPO instead.

NASNet (Zoph et al. 2018) cut the cost of the first NAS by searching for small cells instead of whole networks (Figure 6).

NASNet searches for two cells on CIFAR-10 (60,000 tiny labeled images), a cheap stand-in for ImageNet (the large standard image-classification dataset): a normal cell keeps the feature map (a layer's output grid) the same size and a reduction cell halves it. The same cell design scales by construction.

2.4 EfficientNet, found by RL search

EfficientNet-B0, the best-known result of RL search, is a small mobile model. The controller searched the MnasNet mobile space with a reward trading accuracy against FLOPs (the count of arithmetic operations). The authors then scaled B0 up with compound scaling, one coefficient growing depth, width and resolution (the card below).

EfficientNet's own ratios (Tan and Le 2019) are 1.2 for depth, 1.1 for width and 1.15 for resolution, applied once per step of the knob \(\phi\) (B0 is \(\phi = 0\)); the card names them \(\alpha\), \(\beta\) and \(\gamma\).

One knob, \(\phi\), grows depth, width and resolution together, and each step of it about doubles the compute.
\(\displaystyle d = \alpha ^{\phi },\, w = \beta ^{\phi },\, r = \gamma ^{\phi }\)
The multipliers \(d\), \(w\) and \(r\) scale depth (layers), width (channels) and input resolution (pixels per side). The ratios \(\alpha\), \(\beta\), \(\gamma\) come from a small grid search on B0 and stay fixed; raising them to one power \(\phi\) (phi) grows all three in balance, so one step of \(\phi\) scales all three by their ratios once.
\(\displaystyle \alpha \cdot \beta ^{2} \cdot \gamma ^{2} \approx 2\)
Compute grows with depth and with the square of width and of resolution, so this product is the FLOPs growth per step of \(\phi\); with EfficientNet's ratios it lands near 2. Capping it near 2 means each step of \(\phi\) about doubles the compute, B1 through B7. Efficient CNNs: MobileNet and EfficientNet works through it in full.

3 Running a sampling-based search

The strategy is an outer loop; each child is an ordinary short training job on a proxy, and its validation score goes back to the strategy. The evolution loop of Section 2 cannot be reused: it rescores the whole population every round, so the scoring bill runs far past the budget, and its mutate moves an index along a line, not a choice inside an architecture.

Listing A trains one child: an architecture is a list of \(L\) choices from the five ops of Section 2.1, and score trains it briefly on proxy_loader (a training subset) and returns accuracy on the held-out val_loader.

import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F

def make_ops(c):
    return {
        "conv3": lambda: nn.Sequential(nn.Conv2d(c, c, 3, padding=1), nn.ReLU()),
        "conv5": lambda: nn.Sequential(nn.Conv2d(c, c, 5, padding=2), nn.ReLU()),
        "sep3": lambda: nn.Sequential(nn.Conv2d(c, c, 3, padding=1, groups=c),
                                      nn.Conv2d(c, c, 1), nn.ReLU()),
        "pool": lambda: nn.MaxPool2d(3, stride=1, padding=1),
        "skip": lambda: nn.Identity(),
    }

def build(arch, c):
    ops = make_ops(c)
    layers = [ops[op]() for op in arch]
    return nn.Sequential(nn.Conv2d(3, c, 3, padding=1), *layers,
                         nn.AdaptiveAvgPool2d(1), nn.Flatten(), nn.Linear(c, 10))

def score(arch, c, epochs, learning_rate, momentum):
    net = build(arch, c)
    opt = torch.optim.SGD(net.parameters(), lr=learning_rate, momentum=momentum)
    net.train()
    for _ in range(epochs):
        for x, y in proxy_loader:
            loss = F.cross_entropy(net(x), y)
            opt.zero_grad()
            loss.backward()
            opt.step()
    net.eval()
    with torch.no_grad():
        hits = sum((net(x).argmax(1) == y).sum().item() for x, y in val_loader)
    return hits / len(val_loader.dataset)
  1. 1The five ops and build, which stacks one op per layer between a first convolution and a classifier. Convolution and max pooling are taught in CNN Basics: Convolution, Stride and Pooling; sep3 is a depthwise-separable convolution (groups=c), taught in Efficient CNNs: MobileNet and EfficientNet.
  2. 2Trains the child briefly, on a small per-child budget; the optimizer is SGD with momentum (taught in Optimizers from SGD to Adam), and the learning rate and momentum are the run's settings.
  3. 3Returns validation accuracy, the only signal the strategy sees.

Listing B is aging evolution (Algorithm 1 of Real et al. 2019, the AmoebaNet search). Members keep their scores, so each child trains once; each cycle mutates the best of \(S\) sampled members and drops the oldest.

import random, collections

OPS = ["conv3", "conv5", "sep3", "pool", "skip"]

def aging_evolution(score, n_layers, population, sample_size, budget):
    def mutate(arch):
        child = list(arch)
        i = random.randrange(n_layers)
        others = [op for op in OPS if op != arch[i]]
        child[i] = random.choice(others)
        return child

    # each member is (arch, acc): trained once, kept
    pop = collections.deque()
    history = []
    while len(pop) < population:
        a = [random.choice(OPS) for _ in range(n_layers)]
        pop.append((a, score(a)))
        history.append(pop[-1])
    while len(history) < budget:
        sample = random.choices(list(pop), k=sample_size)
        parent = max(sample, key=lambda m: m[1])[0]
        child = mutate(parent)
        pop.append((child, score(child)))
        history.append(pop[-1])
        # drop the oldest
        pop.popleft()
    return max(history, key=lambda m: m[1])[0]
  1. 1Layers over the five ops, a population, samples and a budget, sized for a toy run; mutate changes one layer to a different op.
  2. 2Fills the population with random architectures, each scored once.
  3. 3Each cycle mutates the best of the sampled members, scores the child and drops the oldest member, so score runs once per child.

NNI's RegularizedEvolution defaults to the paper's population and sample sizes. A larger \(S\) picks parents more greedily (\(S = 1\) is random search); a larger \(P\) remembers more but adapts more slowly. Repeat architectures are retrained on purpose, so only those that retrain well survive.

The per-child budget sets the bill. The first NAS (Zoph and Le 2017) trained every child for many epochs. MnasNet trained each sampled model briefly on ImageNet, with a reward that also charged for latency, the time one prediction takes on the target hardware.

Implementations include MnasNet's code (tensorflow/tpu) and NNI's ready-made random, evolution and RL (PolicyBasedRL) strategies.

  • A short proxy run ranks children roughly. MnasNet retrained only its shortlist on full ImageNet.
  • Latency needs the target device. MnasNet timed every sampled model on a Pixel 1 phone.
  • Retrain the winner from scratch at full size.

4 Pitfalls of sampling-based search

  • Random search is a strong baseline. Li and Talwalkar (2019) found that plain random search with early stopping (scoring after a short run) did at least as well as ENAS, a search that shares weights across candidates, on CIFAR-10, at about the compute of one DARTS search, a gradient-based one (ENAS in One-Shot NAS: Weight-Sharing Supernets, DARTS in DARTS: Differentiable Architecture Search). Averaged over seeds, DARTS still did better.
  • Reproducibility is shaky. Two runs with different seeds (a seed is the fixed random starting setting) can give two different architectures, so report the seed and the variance.
  • Hand design still wins sometimes, with a small budget, unusual hardware or a new domain.

Each method here trains every candidate separately, thousands of GPU-days per search. Can the choice itself be trained by gradient descent, inside one network? DARTS: Differentiable Architecture Search does that.
