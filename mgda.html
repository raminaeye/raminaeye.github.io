MGDA (Multiple Gradient Descent Algorithm)

MGDA steps against the shortest blend of the task gradients, the directions in which each task's loss rises fastest. It stops at a Pareto-stationary point, where no small step lowers every task's loss at once, but it does not pick which one.

The intro left us with two tasks pulling the shared trunk, the shared part of the model that both tasks train, in different directions, and a plain average that just picks a side. This page is the classical answer to that fight. It needs one new idea first, what “best” even means when two tasks trade wins.

When “best” stops meaning one thing

With one loss, the one number training tries to push down, comparing two models is easy: lower wins. With two losses, model A can beat model B on task 1 while losing on task 2. Neither is “better,” full stop. So “optimal” needs a new meaning.

The replacement idea is to throw out any model that has a free upgrade available, some other model at least as good on every task and strictly better on one. Why stay where a free upgrade exists? What is left after all the free upgrades are used up is the set of defensible trade-offs.

Two tasks, two parameters, one conflict

Here is the conflict, measured, on the same two invented arrows the intro used in “The average step helps one task, hurts the other”. Two tasks share two parameters, the shared numbers both losses depend on, so every gradient, the direction in which that task's loss rises fastest (training steps the other way), is an arrow with two entries, small enough to draw. The first arrow points east with length 2. The second points west and slightly north, length about 1.1.

Plain training steps against the average of the two arrows.

How do you tell whether a step helps a task? Training steps against a direction d built from the gradients, so give d a score for that task.

Two lines set up the conflict. The first scores a direction for one task, and the second is the plain average direction training uses by default.
⟨gi, d⟩ > 0 ⇒ task i improves
The score for task i is the dot product of its gradient gi with the direction d, held in the angle brackets: multiply matching entries and add them up. A small step against d lowers task i's loss in proportion to the score, so a positive score helps that task and a negative one hurts it. The arrow ⇒ reads as implies.
d = 12(g1 + g2)
Plain training uses half the sum of the two task gradients as its direction and steps against it. The half gives each task an equal vote in the sum. But the longer arrow pulls the average toward itself, so the average follows the louder task.

As the intro found, stepping against the average helps task 1 and pushes task 2's loss up. Their scores are +1.00 and −0.38.

The average is just one arbitrary blend of many. There is a whole line of blends between the two arrows, and nothing about the plain average makes it the right one.

The Pareto front is a whole curve of trade-offs

  1. A point, one setting of the model, is Pareto-dominated when it has a free upgrade.
  2. A point is Pareto-optimal when nothing dominates it. You cannot improve any task without hurting another. All the free upgrades are used up. Plot the task losses of all such points against each other and they trace the Pareto front.
  3. A point is Pareto-stationary when, judged by the slope alone, no single small step from it lowers every task's loss at once. Equivalently, the shortest blend of the task gradients is zero there. Every Pareto-optimal point is Pareto-stationary, just as every single-task minimum has zero gradient.

The target is a Pareto-stationary point, and that condition can be written as one equation.

The Pareto-stationary condition says the pulls can cancel exactly.
minw∈Δ ‖Σi wi gi‖ = 0
Inside the paired bars, Σi wi gi is a blend, each task's gradient gi scaled by its weight wi and added over every task i, and the bars take its length. The subscript w ∈ Δ says to try every legal weight list (not negative, adding to one) and keep the smallest length. The line says that smallest length is zero.

The catch is that the Pareto front is not one point. It is a whole curve of trade-offs.

Lower loss is better, so in the figure the front bows toward the corner where both losses are zero. A point above and to the right of the front is dominated. Some point on the front has lower loss on both tasks, so moving to it is a free upgrade. A point on the front has no free upgrade.

Moving along the curve helps one task and hurts the other. Picking between the green and blue points is a choice, not a theorem.

Step against the shortest blend

So how do you actually reach a point with no free upgrade left? MGDA steps against the shortest legal blend, a weighted mix of the task gradients whose weights are not negative and add to one, instead of the fixed average, and what it reaches is a Pareto-stationary point. If the shortest blend has length zero, the pulls cancel and you are Pareto-stationary: stop.

Why does a step against the shortest blend help every task? For two tasks, every legal blend has its tip on the straight line between the two arrowheads. The shortest blend meets that line at a right angle, because tilting it toward either arrowhead would make it longer. A blend that meets the line at a right angle has the same score against both pulls, and that shared score is its own squared length, which is positive whenever the blend is not zero. So a small step against it lowers both losses. If the right-angle point falls past an arrowhead, the shortest blend is that task's pull itself, and both scores still come out positive.

The MGDA step in three lines. The first finds the shortest blend, the second stops when it is nearly zero, and the third gives the one form every legal blend takes for two tasks.
minw∈Δ ‖Σi wi gi‖2
Over every legal list of blend weights w, choose the one that makes the squared length of the blend, each task's pull gi scaled by its weight wi and added up, as small as it can go. The symbols are the same as in the Pareto-stationary condition, and the small 2 squares the length.
stop when ‖d*‖ ≈ 0 (Pareto-stationary)
Stop when the shortest blend d* is nearly zero, below a small tolerance set in advance. The run is then at or very near a Pareto-stationary point.
blend = w1 g1 + (1 − w1) g2
For two tasks the weight list has two entries, and they add to one, so task 2's weight w2 is always 1 − w1. Every legal blend is then task 1's weight w1 times its pull g1, plus 1 − w1 times task 2's pull g2. That is why a blend tip rides the line between the two arrowheads.

Textbook MGDA (Désidéri, 2012) is proven to reach a Pareto-stationary point when its step size is well chosen, but on deep networks, the neural networks stacked many layers deep that these tasks use, it is costly in two ways, and Sener and Koltun (2018) fix both.

  • Finding the shortest blend exactly gets slow with many tasks, so they use a Frank-Wolfe solver, a cheap solver that works in rounds. Each round mixes in more of the task whose pull points most against the current blend, until the blend stops getting shorter.
  • Plain MGDA needs every task's gradient on all the shared parameters, one backward pass (the reverse run through the network that computes gradients) per task. MGDA-UB (UB for upper bound) instead blends the gradients at the shared representation, the output of the shared trunk that every task reads. That length bounds the one MGDA minimizes, and one backward pass gives it for every task, so the extra cost is negligible. Each task head, the small part only one task trains, steps with its own gradient.

Their guarantee rests on one assumption: every change of the shared representation can be reached by some change of the shared parameters. Under it, the MGDA-UB direction either lowers every task's loss for a small enough step, or is zero, and the point is Pareto-stationary.

Where the shortest blend sits

Same two arrows as round 1 of “One MGDA step, then repeat”, now with task 1's blend weight w1 in your hands; task 2 gets the rest, 1 − w1. Every legal blend's tip rides the dashed line between the two arrowheads. At w1 = 0 the blend is all of task 2's arrow, and task 1's score reads −2.00. At w1 = 1 it is all of task 1's arrow, and task 2's score reads −2.00.

In between, the length falls to its smallest value near w1 = 0.35, about a third of the way along the dashed line from task 2's arrowhead, where a gold-outlined diamond appears once the blend reaches it. This is MGDA's min-norm point, the shortest legal blend. Only in a narrow window around it are both scores positive.

At the min-norm point here, a genuine mix strictly between the two tasks, the blend tool shows the two scores are equal, about 0.11 each. Neither task is hurt. So one small min-norm solve per step finds a step that lowers both losses, with no hand-tuned weights.

MGDA reaches a Pareto-stationary point, but not one you chose

MGDA is proven to reach some Pareto-stationary point. But “some” hides a problem: which point you reach is decided by where you started, not by anything you chose. The average loss, the thing you actually wanted to minimize, is left to luck.

Closing that gap, reaching a trade-off you chose instead of one the starting point chose, is the job of two methods that build on MGDA. Pareto MTL (Lin et al., 2019; MTL stands for multi-task learning) lets you name several preferred trade-offs up front and steers one run toward each, giving a spread of solutions across the front to pick from. CAGrad, short for Conflict-Averse Gradient Descent, favors the worst-off task in each step while keeping the average loss in view.

One caveat remains. Pareto-stationary is a weaker promise than Pareto-optimal. A blend can cancel to zero at a point that is only a standoff, not a true optimum, the way a zero gradient also holds at a single-task saddle, a flat point that rises in some directions and falls in others. MGDA promises a point where the pulls cancel, not a point where no better trade-off exists.

A loss's scale still turns MGDA's step

MGDA acts after the backward pass, so anything that scaled a loss has already scaled what it blends.

# flat makes one vector; write_grads
# sets the shared layers' .grad
grads = [flat(torch.autograd.grad(
             L, shared,
             retain_graph=True))
         for L in losses]
# shortest blend: a 10 times longer
# gradient gets LESS weight
alpha = min_norm_weights(grads)
g = sum(a * gk for a, gk
        in zip(alpha, grads))
write_grads(shared, g)
# with mini-batches g.norm() levels
# off at the noise: no clean stop
opt.step()

Gradient Balancing Inside the Training Loop covers these in its sections “The loss sets the scale every balancer sees” and “MGDA's shrinking gradient is not a clean stop signal”.

MGDA leans away from a loud task, but never sets its loudness

So there is a principled way to reach a Pareto-stationary point, and when every task's loss is strictly convex, shaped like a single bowl, that point sits on the front. But MGDA never sets how loud each task is. Its blend leans away from the louder task by an amount the arrow lengths decide, not you. Setting each task's loudness on purpose is the question the GradNorm page, next, takes on: how do we fix the loudness first?

The two weighting methods, the volume knobs that learn each task's loss weight (GradNorm and Uncertainty Weighting), and the four gradient methods, PCGrad, GradVac, CAGrad, and MGDA, which stops at a Pareto-stationary point (where no small step lowers every task's loss at once), side by side:
