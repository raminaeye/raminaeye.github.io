Pruning in one paragraph:

  1. Take a ResNet-18 that hits 92.58% accuracy on STL-10.
  2. Delete half of its weights. Accuracy collapses to 66.25%.
  3. Fine-tune for a few epochs. It climbs back to 92.74%.
  4. End result: 5.6 million weights no longer doing any work, and accuracy back where it started.

That is pruning in one paragraph. Neural networks are overparameterized: most weights are just along for the ride. If you can figure out which weights barely matter and remove them, you get a smaller, faster, cheaper-to-deploy model without giving up what the network learned.

The authors were trying to solve a deployment problem. Neural networks are both computationally and memory intensive, which makes them hard to run on embedded systems with limited hardware. Their answer was "deep compression", a three-stage pipeline: prune the network by learning only the important connections, then quantize the weights to force weight sharing, then apply Huffman coding on top. After the first two stages they retrain the network to fine-tune the remaining connections and the quantized centroids.

The numbers are what made the paper famous. Pruning alone cut the connections 9x to 13x; quantization cut each connection from 32 bits to 5. Together with Huffman coding, AlexNet shrank 35x, from 240MB to 6.9MB, and VGG-16 shrank 49x, from 552MB to 11.3MB, both with no loss of accuracy. That is small enough to fit in on-chip SRAM instead of off-chip DRAM, and the compressed networks ran 3x to 4x faster per layer with 3x to 7x better energy efficiency. This page teaches the first stage of that pipeline, and the fine-tuning step that heals the damage.

Zeros are not free: two pruning flavors

There are two ways to cut. Unstructured pruning removes individual weights wherever they are, leaving a sparse Swiss cheese of a matrix. Structured pruning removes whole filters or channels at a time, which actually shrinks the layer. Drag the slider and flip between the two to feel the difference:

small weights to large weights pruned

Toy accuracy vs sparsity. Shaded bands mark the 85% and 50% thresholds the readout uses.

How to play
  • The point: Unstructured pruning just zeros weights, so the matrix stays 16 by 16 and the zeros are not free unless your backend skips them. Structured pruning deletes whole rows, so the layer physically shrinks inside its old footprint. Accuracy falls steadily as sparsity rises: 80.3% at 30%, 66.25% at 50%, 48.96% at 70%.
  • Sparsity (0 to 95%): sets how many weights go. In structured mode the readout reports the actual pruned fraction, because whole rows cannot be subdivided.
  • Unstructured / Structured: switches between scattering X's across the grid and deleting whole rows at a time.

How PyTorch prunes with masks

Here is the part that surprises people: PyTorch does not delete anything at first. When you prune a layer, it keeps the original weights as weight_orig and attaches a binary weight_mask next to them. During the forward pass the effective weight is just weight_orig * weight_mask. The zeros are a fiction maintained by the mask, which is why you can always unprune or finalize later.

Pruning every convolution and linear layer by L1 magnitude, keeping the 50% largest weights, is a few lines: 

import torch.nn.utils.prune as prune

def apply_unstructured_pruning(model, amount=0.5):
    for name, module in model.named_modules():
        if isinstance(module, (nn.Conv2d, nn.Linear)):
            prune.l1_unstructured(module, name='weight', amount=amount)
    return model

After running it, each pruned layer carries the two new attributes, and you can check they are there: 

model_pruned.layer1[0].conv1.weight_orig   # untouched original weights
model_pruned.layer1[0].conv1.weight_mask   # 1s where weights survive, 0s where pruned

If you are happy with the result and want to make it permanent, one call bakes the mask into the weights and deletes the extra bookkeeping: 

prune.remove(module, 'weight')

Getting it back with fine-tuning

The fix is to let the survivors adjust. We retrain just the final classifier layer for 15 epochs with plain SGD, so the network learns to compensate for everything that was cut: 

optimizer = torch.optim.SGD(model_pruned.fc.parameters(), lr=1e-3, momentum=0.9)

model.train()
for epoch in range(15):
    for x, y in train_loader:
        optimizer.zero_grad()
        loss = criterion(model_pruned(x), y)
        loss.backward()
        optimizer.step()

The loss falls from 0.40 to 0.23 across the 15 epochs, and when we evaluate again:

92.74%
Accuracy on STL10 test set
After 15 epochs of fine-tuning the final layer

Fully recovered, slightly above where we started, with half the weights pruned. This is the standard pruning workflow: cut aggressively, fine-tune to heal, and optionally quantize afterward to shrink the model even further.

The prune-and-finetune loop, step by step

The whole recipe on one tiny 6 by 6 grid, using this page's real accuracy numbers. Score every weight, cut the weakest half, measure the damage, then retrain the survivors:

cell shade is weight magnitude weakest half pruned

Pruning in two lines: each weight is multiplied by a keep-or-cut mask, and sparsity is the fraction of weights the mask cut.
w′ = w ⊙ m, mi ∈ {0, 1}
Apply the mask to every weight: multiply each weight by 1 to keep it or by 0 to cut it, and the cut weights become zero.
s = Σi(1 − mi) / N
Count the cut: add up one for every weight the mask cut, then divide by the total number of weights to get the fraction gone.
w one weight (the teal cells); w′ the weight after the cutm the mask: 1 keeps the weight, 0 cuts it (the orange outline marks the cut half)⊙ elementwise multiply; mi ∈ {0, 1} each mask entry is keep or cut; s sparsity, the fraction cut; Σi sum over all weights; 1 − mi 1 for a cut weight, 0 for a kept one; N total weights; / divided by
How to play
  • The point: Pruning is a loop: score every weight, cut the weakest, measure how much accuracy the cut cost, retrain the survivors until the accuracy comes back.
  • Step 1: every weight gets a magnitude score; the weakest half is outlined.
  • Step 2: the outlined half is set to zero. The cut stays reversible: the mask notes exactly which weights went, so they can be restored or baked in later.
  • Step 3: accuracy falls 92.58% to 66.25%; the survivors expected help that is gone.
  • Step 4: fifteen epochs retraining just the final layer; survivors shift, the bar climbs.
  • Step 5: 5.6 million weights doing no work, accuracy at 92.74%. Repeat the loop for more sparsity.

One big cut or many small ones

One-shot pruning is this page's recipe: cut the weights once, then heal the survivors. Iterative pruning cuts a little, heals, and repeats, and the gentler schedule usually reaches higher sparsity before accuracy starts to bend. Gradual pruning folds the same idea into training itself, raising sparsity a little at a time while the network is still learning. Whichever schedule you pick, the loop is the same: score, cut, measure, retrain.

Pruning pitfalls: batch norm, residuals, and sparse speed

  • Do not prune batch norm layers. They keep training stable and cost almost nothing.
  • ResNets need care: residual connections constrain which channels structured pruning is allowed to remove.
  • Sparse does not mean fast. Zeroed weights are still multiplied at inference time unless your backend has sparse kernels. Torch and ONNX compilers can help here.
