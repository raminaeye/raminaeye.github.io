You already know supervised learning. A model makes a prediction, a loss measures how far the prediction is from the right answer, and gradient descent nudges the model’s weights to shrink that loss (Gradient Descent). Everything there starts from a right answer for every example. This page is about learning when nobody can give you that answer.

1 Learning from rewards

Think of learning to ride a bike. Nobody can hand you the right angle for the handlebars at every instant. You wobble, you fall or you don’t, and over many tries you learn which moves keep you up. There is no right answer to copy, only a signal after the fact of how well it went.

That is the setting of reinforcement learning. RL, short for reinforcement learning, is learning which moves to make from rewards rather than from labelled answers.

Why not just collect right answers and train a model the usual way? For many tasks nobody knows them. Which move wins a game of Go from this exact board? Which motor commands make a robot walk? A person can tell you when the result is good, but not the best move at every step. RL needs only that signal. Games are where it made its name: programs learned to play Atari games from the screen and the game’s points alone (Mnih and colleagues), and to beat a top professional, and later the world’s best players, at Go (Silver and colleagues).

2 Agent, world, state, move, reward

To talk about RL we need names for its parts. One small video-game level will carry every idea on this page: a hero walks a level that has a coin near the start and a treasure chest far away.

The agent is the learner that picks the moves. Here it is the hero, the blue dot.

The world, also called the environment, is everything outside the agent: the level with its walls, coin and chest. It takes the agent’s move and decides what happens next.

A state is the agent’s situation right now. Here the state is the square the hero stands on. We write a state as \(s\).

A move, or action, is one choice the agent can make in a state. Here there are four: up, down, left and right. Walking into a wall leaves the hero where it was. We write a move as \(a\).

A reward is the number the world hands back after each move, good or bad. Here the coin pays a small reward, the chest pays a big one, and every other move pays nothing. Picking up either one ends the level. We write a reward as \(r\).

The reward is the only thing the agent is told, so it chases exactly the reward you wrote down, not the one you meant. A boat-racing game agent rewarded for hitting targets learned to circle one lagoon hitting the same targets forever, and never finished the race (Clark and Amodei).

3 Episodes and the return

One reward on its own says little. On most moves the hero earns nothing at all, yet some of those moves were what brought it to the chest. To judge a move we need to look at everything that came after it.

An episode is one run from the start until it ends. In the game, an episode starts at the start square and ends when the hero picks up the coin or the chest, or when it is cut off after a fixed number of steps without reaching either.

The return is all the reward still to come, from this step to the end of the episode. It is what the agent really cares about: not the reward of this one move, but the total its moves lead to.

\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{G_t}} = \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#7d6608}{r_{t+1}}} + \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#7d6608}{r_{t+2}}} + \cdots + \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#7d6608}{r_{T}}}\)
The return from step \(t\) is every reward from the next one to the last one, added up. \(t\) counts the steps of the episode and marks now. \(r_{t+1}\) is the reward the world hands back right after the move made at step \(t\); it carries \(t+1\) because it arrives one step later. The dots stand for every reward in between, and \(T\) is the step where the episode ends. So \(G_t\) is one number for how well the rest of the episode went from now on: for a hero that walks to the chest it is the chest’s big reward, however many empty steps came first.

This is also why RL is harder than supervised learning. The reward that matters can arrive many moves after the move that earned it, so the agent has to work out which earlier moves deserve the credit; judging every move by the return that follows it is how it links the two.

4 Discounting

Adding rewards up as they are has two problems. Some worlds never end, a robot that keeps working or a game that loops, and then the sum can grow without limit, so two different ways of acting both look infinitely good. And a reward now is surer than one promised much later, when a lot can still go wrong. So we let later rewards count a little less.

The discount \(\gamma\) (the Greek letter gamma) sets how much a reward one step later still counts. It is a number from zero up to one, usually just below one; it can be exactly one only when every episode is sure to end. Each extra step of waiting multiplies a reward by \(\gamma\) once more, so rewards fade the further away they are.

With the discount in place, the return from section 3 becomes the version every RL method uses. The return is all the reward still to come, with later rewards counted a little less.

\(\displaystyle \begin{aligned} \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{G_t}} &= \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#7d6608}{r_{t+1}}} + \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\gamma}}\, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#7d6608}{r_{t+2}}} \\ &\quad + \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\gamma^{2}}}\, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#7d6608}{r_{t+3}}} + \cdots \end{aligned}\)
The same sum as in section 3, with each reward shrunk by how long it waits. The next reward \(r_{t+1}\) counts in full. The one after, \(r_{t+2}\), is multiplied by \(\gamma\) once; \(r_{t+3}\) by \(\gamma\) twice, written \(\gamma^{2}\); and so on. A \(\gamma\) near one keeps far rewards almost whole, so the agent is far-sighted. A small \(\gamma\) makes them vanish fast, so the agent is short-sighted and cares mostly about the next few moves. Because the shrinking never stops, the sum stays finite even when the episode never ends, as long as no single reward can be endlessly large.
\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{G_t}} = \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#7d6608}{r_{t+1}}} + \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\gamma}}\, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{G_{t+1}}}\)
The first line, read one step at a time. Everything after the next reward is itself the return from the next step, \(G_{t+1}\), and it waits one step, so it is shrunk once by \(\gamma\). In words: the return from now is the next reward plus the discounted return from one step later. The code below computes the return this way, walking back from the end of an episode.

In code, given the rewards of one finished episode in order, the return from the first step comes out of a short loop that walks back from the end.

def discounted_return(rewards, gamma):
    """rewards[k] is the reward handed back after move k of one episode."""
    G = 0.0                   # the return after the last step is zero
    for r in reversed(rewards):
        G = r + gamma * G     # G_t = r_{t+1} + gamma * G_{t+1}
    return G
  1. 1The input: the list of rewards from one episode and the discount \(\gamma\).
  2. 2The second line of the card above, applied once per step from the end back to the start.

5 The policy

So far the agent has moves and a return to aim for. What it actually learns, and what it uses when it plays, is a rule for choosing moves. A policy is the agent’s rule for choosing a move in each state; it may always pick the same move or pick by chance.

You have seen this shape before. A classifier with a softmax output gives a probability for each class (Softmax, Temperature and Decoding). A policy does the same with moves: it takes a state and gives a chance for each move.

\(\displaystyle \begin{aligned} \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\pi}}(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a}} \mid \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s}}) &= \text{chance of move } \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a}} \\ &\phantom{=}\ \text{in state } \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s}} \end{aligned}\)
\(\pi\) (the Greek letter pi) is the usual name for a policy. The bar \(\mid\) reads “given”: the chance of move \(a\) given that the agent is in state \(s\). On the start square of Figure 5 the policy gives the move up the largest chance and the other moves smaller ones. A policy that always makes the same move in a state puts all of the chance on that one move.
\(\displaystyle \sum_{\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a}}} \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\pi}}(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a}} \mid \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s}}) = 1\)
The big sigma \(\Sigma\) means add up, here over every move \(a\). In every state the chances of all the moves add up to one, just like a softmax over classes: the agent always makes some move. Learning a policy means shifting these chances toward the moves that lead to a high return.

6 Value

To pick good moves, the agent needs to judge how good a square is before it knows how the episode will end. The return of one run cannot do that on its own: it depends on what happened in that run, and a policy that picks by chance may reach the chest one time and the coin the next.

6.1 The value of a state

The fix is to average. Imagine starting from the same square many times, following the same policy, and averaging the returns. An expected value is the average over everything that can happen by chance, weighted by how likely each outcome is (Probability and Bayes Rule).

A state’s value \(V(s)\) is the return the agent expects from state \(s\), if it follows its policy from there.

\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{V_{\pi}}}(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s}}) = \mathbb{E}_{\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\pi}}}\big[\, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{G_t}} \mid \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s_t}} = \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s}} \,\big]\)
The value of a state is the average return from it. \(\mathbb{E}\) means expected value, the average over runs; its small \(\pi\) says the runs follow policy \(\pi\). Inside the brackets is the return \(G_t\) from the discount card in section 4, and after the bar, “given that the state at step \(t\), \(s_t\), is the square \(s\)”. Put together: start on square \(s\), play by \(\pi\), and average the discounted returns. A square near the chest has a high value; a square from which the policy usually wanders has a low one. The value belongs to the policy too: change the policy and the values change.

6.2 The value of a move

A state’s value says how good it is to stand somewhere. To choose, the agent needs one step more: how good each of its moves is from there. Stepping up from a square that leads toward the chest is worth more than walking into a wall.

A move’s value \(Q(s,a)\) is the return the agent expects if it makes move \(a\) in state \(s\) and plays on from there.

\(\displaystyle \begin{aligned} \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{Q_{\pi}}}(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s}}, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a}}) &= \mathbb{E}_{\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\pi}}}\big[\, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{G_t}} \mid \\ &\qquad \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s_t}} = \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s}},\ \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a_t}} = \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a}} \,\big] \end{aligned}\)
The value card of section 6.1 with one more condition after the bar: the first move is fixed to \(a\), and only after it does the agent follow its policy \(\pi\). So \(Q\) rates one move in one square by the average return it leads to. In Figure 7 these are the arrow thicknesses. The letter \(Q\) is just the field’s usual name for it.
\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a^{*}}} = \arg\max_{\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a}}} \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{Q}}(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s}}, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a}})\)
Once the agent has move values, choosing is easy. \(\arg\max\) picks the move whose value is largest, and \(a^{*}\) is that best move: the thickest arrow. Here \(Q\) is written without its small \(\pi\), for short; it still means the move values of the agent’s policy. An agent that always makes its best move needs nothing but good move values, and much of RL is about learning them.

6.3 How far ahead the agent looks

Now the pieces work together. The discount decides how much the far chest counts against the near coin, the values turn that into how good each square is, and the move values decide the route. Here the agent always makes its best move.

7 Explore or exploit

There is a catch, and it is one more way RL differs from supervised learning: there is no fixed dataset. What the agent gets to see depends on the moves it makes. The agent does not start with values; it learns them from its own tries. Suppose that early on it stumbles onto the coin. The coin now looks good, every other square still looks like nothing, and an agent that always makes its best-looking move will walk to the coin forever. It never finds out the chest exists.

Making the move that looks best is called exploiting what the agent knows. Trying a move that does not look best, to find out what it leads to, is exploring. Exploring costs reward in the short run; never exploring can cost the best reward there is. Every RL method has to balance the two.

The simplest balance is epsilon-greedy. Epsilon-greedy usually makes the move with the highest value and, with a small chance \(\varepsilon\) (the Greek letter epsilon), a random one instead.

\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a_t}} = \begin{cases} \text{a random move} \\ \quad \text{with chance } \varepsilon \\[6pt] \arg\max_{\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a}}} \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{Q}}(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s_t}}, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a}}) \\ \quad \text{otherwise} \end{cases}\)
The move at step \(t\) comes from one of two rules. The brace lists them, each with when it applies. Most of the time the agent exploits: it makes the best move by its current move values, the \(\arg\max\) of section 6.2. With chance \(\varepsilon\) it explores: any move, picked at random. A larger \(\varepsilon\) means more exploring and more wasted moves; a smaller one means the agent settles sooner on what it already believes. Seen as a policy from section 5, it puts most of the chance on the best move and spreads the rest evenly over all moves.

8 The RL loop

Every piece is now in place, and they fit into one cycle that repeats at every step of every episode. The agent sees a state, its policy picks a move, the world answers with a reward and the next state, and the agent uses the reward to improve its values or its policy.

Practice worlds for RL come with a standard interface. In Gymnasium, the common Python library of them, one episode of the loop looks like this, with a random move standing in for a learned policy.

import gymnasium as gym

env = gym.make("CartPole-v1")          # a cart that must keep a pole upright
state, info = env.reset()               # info: extra detail, unused here
episode_over = False
while not episode_over:
    action = env.action_space.sample()  # a learned policy would choose here
    state, reward, terminated, truncated, info = env.step(action)
    episode_over = terminated or truncated  # ended, or cut off by the step limit
env.close()
  1. 1Make the world and get the first state of an episode.
  2. 2The loop of Figure 10: pick a move, send it with env.step, get back the next state and the reward, stop when the episode ends. terminated means the episode ended on its own, as at the coin or the chest; truncated means it was cut off by the step limit; info is extra detail we ignore.
  3. 3Close the world when done.

9 Method families

Every RL method runs the loop above. They differ in what the agent learns inside it: move values, the policy itself, both, or a model of how the world responds. Each family has its own page.

  • Learns move valuesValue-Based RL

    Learns \(Q\) for every state and move, as Q-Learning does, then makes the best move.

  • Move values with a networkDeep Q-Networks (DQN)

    A neural network learns the move values from the game screen, as in the Atari agents.

  • Learns the policyPolicy Gradients: REINFORCE, Actor-Critic and PPO

    Shifts the policy’s chances straight toward moves that earned a high return.

  • Moves that are amountsContinuous-Action Actor-Critic: TD3 and SAC

    For robots, where a move is how hard to push a motor, not one choice from a list.

  • Moves that are amounts, first methodDeep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG): Actor and Critic

    The first method for moves that are amounts, and the one TD3 and SAC grew from.

  • Uses a model of the worldModel-Based RL: Dyna, AlphaZero and MuZero

    Plans ahead in its head before moving, as the Go and chess agents do.

  • Several agentsMulti-Agent Reinforcement Learning

    Agents that learn together, including one agent playing against itself.

  • Language modelsAligning Language Models with RL

    The chat reply is the move, and a reward learned from human preferences judges it.

The natural next step is the first family: how does an agent actually learn the move values of section 6 from its own tries? Value-Based RL answers that.
