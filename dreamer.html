1 Learning to walk from camera frames

Each round of real play teaches the model, and the model supplies fresh practice, so each new real episode makes the walker better.

The running example is a walker, a two-legged robot that must learn to walk forward, seen only through a camera. The camera follows the walker from the side as it moves.

The camera shows only this picture, never the joints, so the learner must work out where the body is.

A robot that learns from camera images by trial and error needs a huge amount of real practice, and real practice is slow and costly.

Here the agent is the program that drives the walker’s joints, turning each camera frame into motor commands. In each frame the walker is in a state, its situation right now; the agent picks a move, one choice it can make in that state, and the world hands back a reward, a number, good or bad, after each move. (RL Basics)

DeepMind Control is a set of simulated robot tasks, such as this walker. An observation is what the agent senses at one moment, here the camera frame. (RL Basics)

The walker is paid for moving forward while staying upright. A real step is one move made in the real environment, with the reward and the next state it brings. On the walker each real step runs the simulated robot forward and renders a new camera frame, so real steps are the expensive part.

Each move is a set of torques, one for each joint, and there is no short list of moves to try. A torque is a turning force a motor applies at a joint. (Continuous-Action RL) A continuous action is a real number, or a list of them, that can take any value in a range, like a torque, instead of one choice from a list. (Continuous-Action RL)

1.1 World Models played games inside its own guess

The world model

What. Picture the walker seen only through its camera: a world model is the learner’s guess of how the world works, built from frames.

How. Squeeze each frame into a hidden state, a short list of numbers, guess how it changes after each torque, predict the reward, and rebuild the frame.

Why. When the guess is good, the walker practises inside the guess instead of paying for real steps.

Dreamer grew from World Models, which learned a guess of two games and played one of them entirely inside that guess. Dreamer keeps that idea and fixes two gaps: training in stages, and a controller kept tiny by learning without gradients.

Latent means hidden: learned by the network rather than observed directly.

CarRacing is a top-down driving game, and VizDoom Take Cover is a first-person game where the player must dodge fireballs. A recurrent network is a network that carries a memory from one step to the next. (RNN Basics)

World Models squeezed each frame into a short list of numbers, and used a recurrent network to learn how those numbers change. On top sat a controller, a tiny network that picks the moves from those numbers and the memory’s summary of the past. The model’s temperature sets how much randomness it adds when it imagines: hotter means more random.

In VizDoom the controller trained only inside the imagined game, and a higher temperature made tricks that only work there harder to find.

On CarRacing the full model, with vision and memory, scored 906 ± 21 over 100 trials, past 900, the score the task sets as solved, after learning from 10,000 runs of random play; the vision-only version, which keeps no memory from one frame to the next, scored only 632 ± 251. In Take Cover a temperature of 0.1 scored 2086 inside the imagined game but only 193 for real, while 1.15 scored 1092 for real.

1.2 World Models trained in stages and kept its controller tiny

World Models trained the frame-squeezing part and the predicting part first, from random play, and only then the controller on top. Dreamer keeps training its model on new real experience while its behaviour learns.

World Models trained its controller by trial and error with CMA-ES, which tries many weight settings and keeps and recombines the best. That ignores the gradients the model could offer, how the reward would change for a slightly different torque, so the controller had to stay tiny. Dreamer learns its behaviour by gradients through the model, so its networks can be large.

Dreamer’s imagined rollouts start from real moments the walker has lived. One imagined step is one move tried inside the model; an imagined rollout is a run of them, each starting where the last one ended. (Model-Based RL)

The walker needs a rule for choosing a move in each state. A policy is that rule; it may always pick the same move or pick by chance. (RL Basics) Model exploitation means the policy finds actions where the learned model is wrong and scores well only in imagination. World Models’ controller found moves that made the imagined game’s fireballs vanish, a trick that failed in the real game. Dreamer also learns its behaviour inside a model, so the walker faces the same risk.

2 The walker practises without moving

Learning in imagination

What. Picture the walker practising without moving: the actor that picks the torques and the critic that scores states train only on rollouts the world model makes up.

How. Start from a real hidden state, step the model forward without new frames, and let the actor pick torques, each step earning its reward.

Why. When a round lands, one real step buys many practice steps, and the actor never sees a real frame. Each real episode buys 100 training steps on batches of 50 stored sequences, each a run of frames, torques and rewards from real play.

Every imagined step grows from that one hidden state, so the actor and critic learn from a start the walker has actually lived.

Picture the walker mid-stride. A fall early on loses all the return it would have earned by walking on: one run from the start until it ends is an episode (RL Basics), and the return is all the reward still to come, with later rewards counted a little less (RL Basics).

From any pose, the walker still has return to earn: a state’s value is that expected return. (RL Basics) A critic is a second network that guesses each state’s value, the return expected from it. (Actor-Critic) The critic lets Dreamer value what lies past the end of a short imagined rollout.

Dreamer’s actor picks the torques; for amounts like these it gives a range of likely torques instead of a chance per move.

Latent dynamics are the world model’s rules for how the hidden state changes from one step to the next. Both learn from the same imagined rollouts.

The real world gives no gradient, but the learned model is a network, so the actor learns how a small change in its torques changes later imagined rewards.

Exploration noise is a small random nudge added to the actor’s actions while it learns, so the agent tries moves it is not sure about. (Training DDPG) The actor plays real episodes with this noise added to its torques.

2.1 Short imagined rollouts with a critic at the end

Dreamer imagines only a short way ahead from each real moment. That length, the planning horizon, also called the imagination horizon, is how many steps each imagined rollout runs before a critic values the rest. (Model-Based RL) Here the horizon \(H\) counts imagined steps, while the \(H\) on the Soft Actor-Critic page means entropy, how undecided the policy is. A short horizon is safe but short-sighted.

Each imagined step starts from the last imagined state, so a small model error grows the further ahead the agent imagines. (Model-Based RL) So the longer Dreamer imagines, the less it can trust the end, where the critic takes over.

PlaNet is a planner that plans inside a learned model: before every real step it tries many sequences of actions inside the model and plays the first move of the best one, then plans again from scratch. It never needs a critic but pays for a fresh plan at every step. Reach for it when a fresh plan at every step is affordable and no actor needs training. An actor trained on imagined rewards alone, without a critic, sees reward only inside the horizon, as PlaNet does. Dreamer imagines these short rollouts from many stored real moments at once.

3 Three networks learn inside imagination

3.1 Frames become hidden states

The world model is a recurrent state space model, an RSSM: its hidden state has two parts, a deterministic memory that carries the past forward and a random state drawn fresh at each step.

The mathematics. \(\textcolor{#117a65}{q_\omega}\) is the guess that predicts from the hidden state alone, with the small \(\textcolor{#117a65}{\omega}\) under it the model's weights. \(\textcolor{#7d6608}{r_t}\) is the reward the guess gives chances over: the points the walker earned by arriving there. The bar reads as given. The brackets hold the one input, \(\textcolor{#8e44ad}{\hat s_t}\), the hidden state. \(\textcolor{#117a65}{q_\omega}\) of \(\textcolor{#7d6608}{r_t}\) given \(\textcolor{#8e44ad}{\hat s_t}\) is the reward guess. That same guess, \(\textcolor{#117a65}{q_\omega}\) of \(\textcolor{#2471a3}{o_t}\) given \(\textcolor{#8e44ad}{\hat s_t}\), also rebuilds the frame from the hidden state.
Example. With the walker upright after a step: the reward guess predicts the points for staying upright.
4\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-model)}{q_\omega}(\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-state)}{o_t} \mid \htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-hidden)}{\hat s_t})\)
Intuition. Rebuilding the frame is what teaches the hidden state to carry what the camera shows.
The mathematics. \(\textcolor{#117a65}{q_\omega}\) is the guess that predicts from the hidden state alone, with the small \(\textcolor{#117a65}{\omega}\) under it the model's weights. \(\textcolor{#2471a3}{o_t}\) is the frame the guess gives chances over: what the walker's camera would show if the hidden state were right. The bar reads as given. The brackets hold the one input, \(\textcolor{#8e44ad}{\hat s_t}\), the hidden state. \(\textcolor{#117a65}{q_\omega}\) of \(\textcolor{#2471a3}{o_t}\) given \(\textcolor{#8e44ad}{\hat s_t}\) is the guess that rebuilds the frame. Only the guess that uses the frame sees it; every other guess predicts from the hidden state alone. The whole equation is the model: see the frame, guess ahead without it, predict the reward, and rebuild the frame to learn from.
Example. For the same mid-stride pose: that guess redraws the frame the camera should show, so a wrong hidden state is caught.
5\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-hidden)}{h_t} = \htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-model)}{f}(\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-hidden)}{h_{t-1}}, \htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-hidden)}{\hat s_{t-1}}, \htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-action)}{a_{t-1}})\)
Intuition. The memory carries the past forward: what happened stays available without a new frame.
The mathematics. \(\textcolor{#8e44ad}{h_t}\) is the deterministic memory, the part of the hidden state that is computed, not drawn. \(\textcolor{#117a65}{f}\) updates it from \(\textcolor{#8e44ad}{h_{t-1}}\), the last memory, \(\textcolor{#8e44ad}{\hat s_{t-1}}\), the last hidden state, and \(\textcolor{#a04000}{a_{t-1}}\), the last torques. Nothing random enters here, so the memory carries what happened forward step by step. The memory feeds the random state at each step and rides inside the hidden state the actor and critic read.
Example. Two steps after the push-off: the memory still holds the push-off from two steps back, while the fresh random state says only where the body is now.

Training needs one score for the whole model, one that covers everything chance can do across the stored sequences. The score keeps the guess that works without the frame close to the guess that uses the frame with a KL divergence, how far one set of chances is from another. (Probability)

Equation 2. The model objective. A world model is only useful if its guesses match what really happened, so its training needs one score to raise.
1\(\displaystyle \begin{aligned} J_\omega = \mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_t \;&\log \htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-model)}{q_\omega}(\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-state)}{o_t} \mid \htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-hidden)}{\hat s_t}) \\ &+ \log \htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-model)}{q_\omega}(\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-reward)}{r_t} \mid \htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-hidden)}{\hat s_t}) \\ &- \htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-discount)}{\beta}\,\mathrm{KL}\big(\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-model)}{p_\omega} \,\Vert\, \htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-model)}{q_\omega}\big)\Big] \end{aligned}\)
Intuition. One score: rebuild frames, predict rewards, keep the guess that works without the frame close.
The mathematics. \(J_\omega\) is the model objective. \(\mathbb{E}\) is the expected value over the stored sequences, each sequence's score times its chance. The first term, log \(\textcolor{#117a65}{q_\omega}\) of \(\textcolor{#2471a3}{o_t}\) given \(\textcolor{#8e44ad}{\hat s_t}\), is the rebuild line's score for the real frame. The second term adds log \(\textcolor{#117a65}{q_\omega}\) of \(\textcolor{#7d6608}{r_t}\) given \(\textcolor{#8e44ad}{\hat s_t}\), the reward line's score for the real reward. The third term subtracts \(\textcolor{#5d6d7e}{\beta}\) times the KL gap between \(\textcolor{#117a65}{p_\omega}\) and \(\textcolor{#117a65}{q_\omega}\), penalizing the guess that works without the frame for drifting from the guess that uses the frame. \(\textcolor{#5d6d7e}{\beta}\) weights the penalty: larger keeps the guess that works without the frame closer. \(J_\omega\) is that sum over every step t: the rebuild log plus the reward log minus \(\textcolor{#5d6d7e}{\beta}\) times the KL gap between \(\textcolor{#117a65}{p_\omega}\) and \(\textcolor{#117a65}{q_\omega}\).
Example. When the walker stumbles in one stored sequence: at each step the hidden state must rebuild the frame and predict the reward.

The rebuild term is not the only way to teach the hidden state. A contrastive objective instead drops the part that rebuilds the frame from the hidden state, the observation model, and trains a state model instead, a part that predicts the hidden state straight from the image. It learns by noise contrastive estimation: the state must pick the matching image out of a batch of wrong ones, so the states stay spread out instead of collapsing. On the 20 Control Suite tasks, the simulated robot tasks Dreamer was tested on, rebuilding solved the most, the contrastive version about half, and predicting rewards alone was not sufficient. Reach for it when rebuilding frames costs too much, or the frames carry little the task needs.

3.2 The actor picks torques and the critic scores them

The reparameterization trick writes a random draw as the centre plus the width times a standard random number, so the critic’s gradient can flow back into the actor. (Soft Actor-Critic)

The actor

What. Picture the walker mid-stride in imagination: the actor is the policy network that picks the torques in imagined states.

How. It gives one centre and one width per torque, and draws each torque from a Gaussian, a bell-shaped spread around the centre as wide as the width, as the centre plus the width times a standard random number, so gradients flow back through the model, then tanh, which squeezes any number into -1 to 1, squeezes each draw into the joints' allowed range.

Why. Gradients flow back through the model into the actor, so imagined returns improve the torques without a real step.

Equation 3. The actor and critic. The actor picks torques in imagined states and the critic scores those states, so imagination can train behaviour.
1\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-action)}{a_j} \sim \htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-policy)}{\pi}_{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-actor)}{\theta}}(\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-action)}{a_j} \mid \htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-hidden)}{\hat s_j})\)
Intuition. The actor draws torques in imagined states; the critic judges those states.
The mathematics. \(\textcolor{#a04000}{a_j}\) is the torque drawn at imagined step j, and the tilde sign reads as drawn at random from. \(\textcolor{#7d3c98}{\pi}_{\textcolor{#196f3d}{\theta}}\) of \(\textcolor{#a04000}{a_j}\) given \(\textcolor{#8e44ad}{\hat s_j}\) is the actor, a tanh-transformed Gaussian over torques, with the small \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#196f3d}{\theta}}\) under it the actor's weights; the bar reads as given. The small \(j\) under each symbol marks the imagined step, counted from the real moment \(t\). \(\textcolor{#a04000}{a_j}\) is drawn from \(\textcolor{#7d3c98}{\pi}_{\textcolor{#196f3d}{\theta}}\) of \(\textcolor{#a04000}{a_j}\) given \(\textcolor{#8e44ad}{\hat s_j}\). The actor draws with the reparameterization trick, the centre plus the width times a standard random number, so the gradient flows back into the actor, and tanh squeezes each draw into the joints' allowed range.
Example. With the walker balanced on one leg in imagination: the actor draws a torque for the ankle joint from its chances.
2\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-statevalue)}{V}_{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-statevalue)}{\phi}}(\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-hidden)}{\hat s_j})\)
Intuition. The critic reads only the hidden state, no frame and no torque.
The mathematics. \(\textcolor{#1f618d}{V_\phi}\) of \(\textcolor{#8e44ad}{\hat s_j}\) is the critic's guess of the value of the imagined hidden state \(\textcolor{#8e44ad}{\hat s_j}\). The critic's guess is the return the walker expects from there, with the small \(\textcolor{#1f618d}{\phi}\) under it the critic's weights. The critic reads only the hidden state, no frame and no torque.
Example. For the hidden state behind the balanced pose: the critic scores it, saying what return the walker expects from there.
3\(\displaystyle \begin{aligned} \htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-action)}{a_j} &= \tanh(\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-policy)}{\mu}_{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-actor)}{\theta}}(\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-hidden)}{\hat s_j}) + \htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-policy)}{\sigma}_{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-actor)}{\theta}}(\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-hidden)}{\hat s_j}) \htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-action)}{\epsilon}), \\ \htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-action)}{\epsilon} &\sim \mathcal{N}(0, I) \end{aligned}\)
Intuition. The torque is a centre plus a width times a standard random number, squeezed into the joints' allowed range.
The mathematics. \(\textcolor{#a04000}{a_j}\) is the torque at imagined step j. \(\textcolor{#7d3c98}{\mu}_{\textcolor{#196f3d}{\theta}}\) of \(\textcolor{#8e44ad}{\hat s_j}\) is its centre, \(\textcolor{#7d3c98}{\sigma}_{\textcolor{#196f3d}{\theta}}\) of \(\textcolor{#8e44ad}{\hat s_j}\) its width, and \(\textcolor{#a04000}{\epsilon}\) is a standard random number, drawn from a Gaussian, a bell centred at 0 with width 1. \(I\) means each number is drawn on its own, with width 1. tanh squeezes each draw into the joints' allowed range, so no draw can exceed it.
Example. At the ankle joint: the centre says push a little forward, the width says how unsure the actor is, and the draw picks the actual torque.

3.3 The target blends rewards with the critic's value

Lambda, the mix weight, sets how much the longer sums count. The discount sets how much a reward one step later still counts. The target is what the critic learns to predict.

The lambda-return

What. Picture the rollout ending before the payoff: the target blends the next reward with the critic's value and the next target, so it looks past the end.

How. The next reward comes first, then the next step’s blend, counted a little less by the discount: one minus lambda times the critic’s value, plus lambda times the next target.

Why. A short rollout ends before the payoff, so without this blend the actor would train only toward what pays inside the horizon.

Equation 4. The lambda-return. A short imagined rollout ends before the payoff arrives, so the critic needs a target that looks past the end of the rollout.
1\(\displaystyle \begin{aligned} \htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-statevalue)}{V}_{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-lambda)}{\lambda}}(\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-hidden)}{\hat s_j}) &= \htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-reward)}{\hat r_{j+1}} \\ &\quad + \htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-discount)}{\gamma}(1-\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-lambda)}{\lambda})\, \htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-statevalue)}{V}_{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-statevalue)}{\phi}}(\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-hidden)}{\hat s_{j+1}}) \\ &\quad + \htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-discount)}{\gamma}\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-lambda)}{\lambda}\, \htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-statevalue)}{V}_{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-lambda)}{\lambda}}(\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-hidden)}{\hat s_{j+1}}) \end{aligned}\)

The plot shows what the target leans on as the mix weight changes. At zero the target is one reward plus the critic's value; at one it is the longest reward sum plus the critic's value.

Intuition. The target is the next reward plus a blend of the critic's value and the next target, so it looks past the end of the rollout.
The mathematics. \(\textcolor{#1f618d}{V}_{\textcolor{#8e44ad}{\lambda}}\) of \(\textcolor{#8e44ad}{\hat s_j}\) is this imagined state's target. \(\textcolor{#7d6608}{\hat r_{j+1}}\) is the first term, the reward predicted one step ahead. The plus signs add the blend, discounted one step by \(\textcolor{#5d6d7e}{\gamma}\). One minus \(\textcolor{#8e44ad}{\lambda}\) weights the critic's value \(\textcolor{#1f618d}{V_\phi}\) of \(\textcolor{#8e44ad}{\hat s_{j+1}}\); \(\textcolor{#8e44ad}{\lambda}\) weights the next target, \(\textcolor{#1f618d}{V}_{\textcolor{#8e44ad}{\lambda}}\) of \(\textcolor{#8e44ad}{\hat s_{j+1}}\). Each next target unrolls the same way, so the blend mixes the one-reward target, the two-reward target, and every longer sum up to the longest. \(\textcolor{#8e44ad}{\lambda}\) sits between zero and one: at zero the target is the one-reward target, one reward plus the discounted critic value, and at one it is the longest reward sum plus the critic’s value at the end. At the final imagined state the target is the critic’s own guess, which is where the recursion stops. \(\textcolor{#1f618d}{V}_{\textcolor{#8e44ad}{\lambda}}\) of \(\textcolor{#8e44ad}{\hat s_j}\) equals \(\textcolor{#7d6608}{\hat r_{j+1}}\) plus \(\textcolor{#5d6d7e}{\gamma}\) times one minus \(\textcolor{#8e44ad}{\lambda}\) times \(\textcolor{#1f618d}{V_\phi}\) of \(\textcolor{#8e44ad}{\hat s_{j+1}}\) plus \(\textcolor{#5d6d7e}{\gamma}\) times \(\textcolor{#8e44ad}{\lambda}\) times \(\textcolor{#1f618d}{V}_{\textcolor{#8e44ad}{\lambda}}\) of \(\textcolor{#8e44ad}{\hat s_{j+1}}\). This target is what the critic learns to predict: the critic’s guess \(\textcolor{#1f618d}{V_\phi}\) moves toward it.
Example. Picture the walker two steps from a fall: at zero the target is the next reward plus the critic’s value, and at one it is the longest reward sum plus the critic’s value.

3.4 The actor climbs, the critic follows

Equation 5. Learning. The actor climbs toward higher targets and the critic moves toward them, both inside imagination.
1\(\displaystyle \max_{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-actor)}{\theta}}\; \mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{j=t}^{t+H} \htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-statevalue)}{V}_{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-lambda)}{\lambda}}(\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-hidden)}{\hat s_j})\Big]\)
Intuition. The actor changes its torques to raise the \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{V}_{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{\lambda}}}\) along its imagined futures.
The mathematics. Max over \(\textcolor{#196f3d}{\theta}\) reads as make as large as possible by choosing the actor's weights \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#196f3d}{\theta}}\). \(\mathbb{E}\) is the expected value, an average over the possible imagined rollouts, each rollout's summed \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{V}_{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{\lambda}}}\) times its chance. The sum adds \(\textcolor{#1f618d}{V}_{\textcolor{#8e44ad}{\lambda}}\) of \(\textcolor{#8e44ad}{\hat s_j}\) over the imagined steps j, from the real moment t through the final imagined state. H is the horizon, the number of imagined steps in each rollout. The line is the max over \(\textcolor{#196f3d}{\theta}\) of the expected value of the sum of \(\textcolor{#1f618d}{V}_{\textcolor{#8e44ad}{\lambda}}\) of \(\textcolor{#8e44ad}{\hat s_j}\).

2\(\displaystyle \begin{aligned} \min_{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-statevalue)}{\phi}}\; \mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{j=t}^{t+H} \tfrac{1}{2}\,\big(&\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-statevalue)}{V}_{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-statevalue)}{\phi}}(\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-hidden)}{\hat s_j}) \\ &- \htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-statevalue)}{V}_{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-lambda)}{\lambda}}(\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-hidden)}{\hat s_j})\big)^2\Big] \end{aligned}\)
Intuition. The critic learns to predict the \(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{V}_{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{\lambda}}}\), so its guesses move toward the targets.
The mathematics. Min over \(\textcolor{#1f618d}{\phi}\) reads as make as small as possible by choosing the critic's weights. \(\mathbb{E}\) is the expected value, an average over the possible imagined rollouts, each rollout's summed squared gap times its chance. The sum over j runs over the imagined steps from the real moment t through the final imagined state. The brackets hold the gap, \(\textcolor{#1f618d}{V_\phi}\) of \(\textcolor{#8e44ad}{\hat s_j}\) minus the target \(\textcolor{#1f618d}{V}_{\textcolor{#8e44ad}{\lambda}}\) of \(\textcolor{#8e44ad}{\hat s_j}\), and the square makes the gap positive, so big misses count more, and the one-half halves it. The target \(\textcolor{#1f618d}{V}_{\textcolor{#8e44ad}{\lambda}}\) of \(\textcolor{#8e44ad}{\hat s_j}\) is held fixed, so it stays still while the critic moves toward it. The line is the min over \(\textcolor{#1f618d}{\phi}\) of that expected sum.
Example. Picture a rollout where the critic scores a crouched pose too low: the gap is large, so the update moves that guess up the most there.

The sums run through the final imagined state; the actor sum ends on the critic's value there, and the critic sum ends on a gap of zero.

4 Stepping through one Dreamer cycle

The Dreamer cycle

What. Picture one round of the loop: the walker plays for real, the model learns, the walker practises in imagination, then plays again.

How. Train the model on stored sequences, imagine rollouts, learn actor and critic, then play one real episode with noise and store it.

Why. Each round feeds the model fresh real frames and feeds the behaviour fresh imagined practice, so both improve together.

One round: a stumble teaches the model, and the next rollouts start there.

5 Three short functions run one update

Three short functions run one update from a batch of real moments. The first imagines a rollout from each moment: the actor picks torques, the model steps the hidden state forward, and each step earns its predicted reward. The second walks back from the critic’s value at the end, blending each reward with the value after it into targets. The third learns from those targets: the actor climbs toward higher targets and the critic moves toward them. The model’s own training is left out. The update starts from hidden states pinned down by the guess that uses the frame.

def imagine(start, actor, transition, reward, H):
    s = start.detach()
    states, rewards = [s], []
    for _ in range(H):
        a = actor(s).rsample()
        s = transition(s, a).rsample()
        states.append(s)
        rewards.append(reward(s).mean)
    return torch.stack(states), torch.stack(rewards)
  1. The rollout keeps one more state than the horizon counts, the start first. Each reward is the reward after the move taken from the state beside it, the predicted reward one step ahead. The torque draw and the hidden-state draw both keep their gradients: the guess that works without the frame steps the hidden state forward from the last state and the last torques, the actor draws the torque from its chances, and the reward line gives its mean. The start state is cut off from the gradients, so the rollout starts from a fixed hidden state. The per-step states and rewards are gathered into one list each.
def lambda_return(rewards, values, gamma, lam):
    R = values[-1]
    out = []
    for i in reversed(range(len(rewards))):
        R = rewards[i] + gamma * ((1 - lam) * values[i + 1] + lam * R)
        out.append(R)
    return torch.stack(out[::-1])
  1. The running total starts as the critic’s value at the rollout’s end; each step pairs a reward with the value of the state after it; reversing at the end puts each target beside its state. The mix weight sets how much the longer sums count; the critic’s value of each state comes in alongside the rewards.
frozen = list(world_model.parameters()) + list(critic.parameters())
for p in frozen:
    p.requires_grad_(False)
S, Rw = imagine(start, actor, world_model.transition, world_model.reward, H)
targets = lambda_return(Rw, critic(S), gamma, lam)
actor_loss = -targets.mean()
actor_opt.zero_grad(); actor_loss.backward(); actor_opt.step()
for p in frozen:
    p.requires_grad_(True)
critic_loss = (critic(S[:-1].detach()) - targets.detach()).pow(2).mean()  # squared gap to the target
critic_opt.zero_grad(); critic_loss.backward(); critic_opt.step()
  1. The actor’s update raises the expected sum of the targets over the imagined steps, turned into a loss to minimise by the minus sign. The critic’s update shrinks the squared gap between the critic’s guess and the target, on every imagined state except the last. The actor’s loss averages one target per reward earned, while the paper’s sum runs one term further, to the final imagined state. The final imagined state earns no reward after it, so it has no target of its own; its value is only the starting point for the walk back. The critic skips the final state because there its target equals its own guess, so the gap is zero. Freezing the model and critic weights still lets the actor’s gradient flow through them, but writes nothing into them. The learned model here supplies the transition and reward lines. One updater changes the actor’s weights, another the critic’s.

6 The critic lets short rollouts train toward late payoffs

The walker is reactive: a good move pays almost at once, so it copes without a critic. A version without a critic and PlaNet succeed only on reactive tasks like it, so the tool uses Hopper Hop instead: a one-legged robot whose payoff comes many steps later. In this tool the Hopper first crouches, and only the hop after it pays. The critic has learned from past imagined rollouts that a crouched pose is worth a lot, because a hop usually follows it.

The hop pays nothing inside the horizon, yet its bar stands tallest.

With the critic off, only what pays inside the horizon counts, so the lean wins.

With the critic on, the hop wins.

7 Training settings and results on 20 tasks

A longer horizon sees further ahead, but it leans on a model that grows less accurate the further it imagines. A higher mix weight trusts longer imagined sums of reward, while a lower one trusts the critic sooner. A larger action repeat, how many frames one chosen move is held for, makes each imagined step cover more real time.

The settings that shape the behaviour:

  • Imagination horizon \(H\): 15 steps.
  • Lambda-return weight \(\lambda\): 0.95.
  • Discount \(\gamma\): 0.99.
  • KL weight \(\beta\): 1, with 3 free nats (a nat is the unit the KL term is measured in; the KL term stops pushing once it is already small).
  • Training steps per collected episode: 100.
  • Batch: 50 sequences of length 50; a larger batch steadies each update, a smaller one runs cheaper.
  • Optimizer and learning rates: Adam, an optimizer that gives each weight its own step size, with 6e-4 for the model and 8e-5 each for the actor and the critic.
  • Gradient norm clip: 100 (the norm is the gradient’s length; longer gradients are scaled down to this length); a higher clip lets longer gradients through, a lower one steadies training.
  • Random episodes to start: 5; more seed episodes give a wider start, fewer start narrower.
  • Action repeat: 2.
  • Exploration noise: Normal(0, 0.3), a bell-shaped draw centred at 0 with width 0.3; more noise tries wilder moves, less noise sticks to what works.
  • Images: 64 by 64.

Dreamer needed no slow-moving copy of the critic to build its targets (the target network of Training DDPG) and no extra reward for staying undecided. For tasks that can end early, the model also predicts whether the episode continues.

A higher learning rate takes bigger steps and can overshoot; a lower one takes smaller, steadier steps. Dreamer uses a separate learning rate for the model, the actor and the critic.

Each training run used one Nvidia V100 GPU and 10 CPU cores and took about 3 hours per million real steps. Imagining stays cheap because it runs in the compact latent space: each hidden state pairs a 200-number deterministic memory with a 30-number random state. Only the reward function is three layers of 300 units; the transition is the recurrent state space model, and frame rebuilding is a transposed convolutional decoder. The authors released the code under the name Dreamer.

7.1 Walking and balancing from pixels on DeepMind Control

Dreamer trained on 20 DeepMind Control Suite tasks from 64 by 64 camera images, with the same settings for every task: action repeat 2, episodes of 1,000 steps, actions of 1 to 12 torques, rewards from 0 to 1. The tasks include the walker, the hopper, the acrobot, a two-link arm that must swing up, the quadruped, a four-legged robot that must walk, and cup catch, a ball on a string to catch in a cup. Each seed is one full training run from a different random start, and the results average 5 seeds.

After 5 million real steps Dreamer averaged a score of 823 across the 20 tasks. The model-based agent PlaNet averaged 332 at the same 5 million steps. D4PG is a model-free method: it learns values straight from real steps, with no model of the world. It is also a distributed agent: many copies play in parallel and feed one learner. D4PG averaged 786 after 100 million steps. At a horizon of 20, Dreamer beat the alternatives on 16 of 20 tasks and tied on 4.

Dreamer tops both rivals on a twentieth of the real steps D4PG needed.

8 Control from pixels with few real steps

Dreamer fits when the actions are amounts, the observations are images, and real steps are scarce.

Dreamer also runs on discrete actions, one move from a list instead of an amount per joint: it was tried on Atari games, the arcade games of the 1980s played from the screen alone, and DeepMind Lab levels, first-person 3D maze tasks, with a horizon of 10 and a KL weight of 0.1.

Against MuZero, which searches through possible moves at decision time, Dreamer acts without searching, so acting is cheap and continuous actions work directly.

Against D4PG, a model-free agent that learns from stored steps: it fits when real steps are cheap enough for many copies to play in parallel, while Dreamer fits when they are scarce; the cost is a model to tune.

9 When the model is wrong and the horizon runs short

  • Model exploitation: the actor finds actions where the model is wrong, so imagined returns climb while real ones do not, as when a move that scores inside imagination scores nothing for real. Keeping the model training on new real episodes closes the blind spots the actor steers into, and a short horizon gives the model less room to be wrong.
  • A reward alone is too sparse to shape the hidden state, so the hidden state misses what the task needs and imagined rollouts go wrong.
