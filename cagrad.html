Spend your freedom on the worst-off task

Here is how I think about CAGrad: it is a diplomat on a leash.

The leash is a ball drawn around the average gradient, with a radius set to a chosen fraction of that gradient's own length. The update is not allowed to stray farther than that from the consensus direction.

Staying close is what keeps the convergence proof alive.

Inside the ball, the diplomat spends all of its limited freedom on a single goal: help the worst-off task as much as possible.

Turn the knob to 0 and the ball shrinks to a point, which is plain gradient descent. Let the knob grow very large and the leash comes off, which is MGDA.

The useful range is in between.

The leash: stay inside a ball around the average

Even when the average loss is exactly what you want to minimize, optimizing it directly can stall.

The cause is conflicting gradients: task gradients come in different scales, so the loudest dominates the update; they also point in different directions, so the average step can actively hurt a specific task.

The scoring rule: the dot product of a task's gradient with a candidate step scores how much that step helps the task. Positive means the task's loss goes down.

CAGrad judges a step by its worst score across all tasks, then picks the step whose worst score is the best.

The step must stay on a leash: inside a ball around the average gradient, whose radius is set by the knob.

At 0 the leash has zero length and you get plain gradient descent; a large knob setting lets the step roam, which is MGDA.

Inside a ball around the average gradient, find the direction that helps the least-helped task the most.

Here is that problem statement as equations, before any tool draws it. State the goal: pick the direction that helps the worst-off task the most, while staying on a short leash around the average gradient.

$$\max_d \min_i \langle g_i, d \rangle \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \|d - g_0\| \le c \cdot \|g_0\|$$

Here d is any candidate direction, gi is any task's gradient, g0 is the average gradient, c is the leash, how far the step may stray as a fraction of the average's length, max and min pick the largest and smallest values, ⟨⟩ are angle brackets for the alignment score of the two vectors, ∥∥ is the norm, the length of a vector, s.t. means subject to, the constraint, · is the dot product, how aligned two vectors are, and ≤ means at most.

Solve for the blend weights: choose how much of each task's gradient to mix so the blend helps the worst-off task as much as possible.

$$w^* = \operatorname{argmin}_{w \in \Delta} \left( g_w \cdot g_0 + \sqrt{\varphi} \cdot \|g_w\| \right), \qquad g_w = w_1 g_1 + w_2 g_2$$

Here w* is the solved blend weights, argmin is the choice giving the smallest value, ∈ means is a member of, Δ is every legal blend, where the weights sum to 1, gw is the blended gradient, w1 and g1 are task 1's blend weight and gradient, w2 and g2 are task 2's, and √ is the square root of.

Build the final update: start at the average gradient, then step toward the worst task's direction, limited by the leash length.

$$d^* = g_0 + c \cdot \|g_0\| \cdot \left( \frac{g_w}{\|g_w\|} \right)$$

Here d* is the final update, the direction that maximizes the worst task's score, g0 is the average gradient, and c is the leash, how far the update may stray, as a fraction of the average's length.

One symbol in the second line needs its own sentence, because the tool's legend calls φ only the confidence knob. In the app code and its worked block, φ is c squared times the squared length of the average gradient:

$$\varphi = c^2 \|g_0\|^2$$

The four moving parts inside one CAGrad step

Every piece of that problem statement is doing a job:

  1. Why judge a step by its worst task? The average score is exactly what betrays a quiet task: a loud task can outvote a hurt one and the average never notices. Taking the minimum is a weakest-link rule. Betrayal has nowhere to hide.
  2. Why the ball around the average gradient? Without it the rule turns into MGDA, which wanders as far from the average as it takes to keep the peace, with no promise about where it ends up. The ball is what buys the proof: any step within the leash of the average gradient keeps a positive fraction of that gradient's downward push on the average loss.
  3. Why a radius tied to the gradient's own length, not a fixed number? So the leash grows and shrinks with the terrain. Early in training gradients are large and a fixed radius would be uselessly tight; late in training it would be uselessly loose. A fixed fraction keeps the diplomat's freedom constant in the only units that matter, and it makes the knob unit-free, so no rescaling of the losses can game it.
  4. Why solve the dual? The primal searches over d in the full parameter space, millions of dimensions, at every step. The dual searches over K blend weights, and K is 2 to 50. Same answer, vastly less work: the solve is negligible next to a backward pass for small K.

The update: average gradient plus one bounded nudge toward the worst task

Searching over every possible update directly is hopeless: it lives in the full parameter space, millions of dimensions.

But the answer has a simple shape. The best update is the average gradient plus one bounded nudge: the consensus direction, plus one step of fixed length toward the compromise that helps the worst-off task most.

The step length is exactly the leash length, so the update always lands on the ball's boundary.

The reference implementation's toy example divides the update by one plus the knob setting: a toy-code convention, not part of the method itself.

The race below uses it so the numbers match the reference code.

The app lists the steps of one CAGrad update in order:

  1. Forward pass; compute each task loss Li.
  2. Compute each task gradient and their average (θsh is the shared parameters, the trunk every task trains):

    gi = ∇θsh Li

    g0 = 1K Σgi

  3. Solve the dual: over all blends w in the simplex, minimize F(w), where gw is the blend of the task gradients:

    F(w) = gwTg0 + c∥g0∥∥gw∥

    gw = Σwigi

  4. Form the update from the winning blend w*:

    d = 11+c (g0 + c∥g0∥·gw*∥gw*∥)

  5. Step the network with d as usual.

Read the ball geometry from the inside out. Pick any candidate d. Let every task score it and keep only the lowest score. Choose the d with the highest lowest score. The best worst-task score is found by hunting on the disc's edge. The update cannot leave the ball.

The code is the honest version:

import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize

def cagrad_combine(grads, c=0.5):
    G = np.stack(grads); g0 = G.mean(axis=0); K = len(G)
    phi = c**2 * (g0 @ g0)
    def F(w):                       # the dual objective
        gw = w @ G
        return gw @ g0 + np.sqrt(phi) * np.linalg.norm(gw)
    w = minimize(F, np.full(K, 1/K), bounds=[(0, 1)] * K,
                 constraints={'type': 'eq', 'fun': lambda w: w.sum() - 1}).x
    gw = w @ G
    # last line: the reference repo's toy.py convention (not part of the method itself)
    return (g0 + c * np.linalg.norm(g0) * gw / np.linalg.norm(gw)) / (1 + c)

The last line is the diplomat on a leash in one expression: the consensus direction, plus one bounded act of independence.

The guarantee: convergence to a stationary point of the average loss

CAGrad provably converges to a stationary point of the average loss. The squared steepness of the average loss, added over all steps, never exceeds a fixed budget, so the steepness must shrink to zero.

The budget makes sense: starting farther above the floor allows more total progress, bigger steps buy progress faster, and a bigger knob setting costs some of it.

The assumptions H-Lipschitz gradients means the landscape never curves too sharply (a smoothness budget). Bounded below means the loss has a floor it cannot fall under. Small enough steps means steps sized for that curvature.

  1. You keep your objective. Unlike MGDA or PCGrad, the proven destination is a stationary point of the average loss itself, not some arbitrary trade-off chosen by the starting point.
  2. Balancing has a price. The guarantee weakens as the knob grows: more conflict-avoidance, slower worst-case progress.
  3. The guarantee only holds while the knob stays below 1, which is why the recommended range sits comfortably below 1 (0.4 on NYUv2, a benchmark of indoor scenes where each image carries many labels at once, and 0.2 on Cityscapes, a benchmark of street scenes labeled the same way).

How to read the six panels

The setup is tiny on purpose: a model with just two knobs to turn, and two losses pulling in different directions. With only two knobs, the whole story fits in six pictures. Here is how to read them.

What the plots are: each panel is drawn in loss space, not knob space. The horizontal axis is task 1's loss, the vertical axis is task 2's loss, so every point is one whole model: bottom-left means both tasks happy, top-right means both suffering. The curve in each panel is the Pareto front, the models where one task can only improve if the other gets worse. Nothing can live below and left of it.

What the trails are: one colored trail is one training run from one starting point, painted red at the start and fading to yellow as training goes on. Five trails per panel, five different starts. Panels 1 to 4 give GD, MGDA, PCGrad, and CAGrad (c = 0.5) the same five starts; panels 5 and 6 rerun CAGrad with different leash lengths.

How to read them: a run that stalls stops up and to the right, both losses still high, going nowhere. A run that works bends down and left, hugging toward the front. Compare panel 1 with panel 4 and the difference is the whole method: GD's runs die in the valley, CAGrad's bend down and left toward the Pareto front.

CAGrad keeps descending where gradient descent stalls

  1. GD stalls. Its trail dies in the ravine on three of five starts, with final average losses of 2.49, 1.69, and 3.50, miles above the minimum -15.09. On the trapped runs the two task gradients fight on about half of all steps and never stop: conflict is not a passing storm, it is the climate of the trap.
  2. MGDA and PCGrad escape. MGDA reaches the low-loss region on all five runs, PCGrad on four of five. But they stop wherever they first touch the Pareto set, with final average losses anywhere from about -10 to -15.1, decided by the start, not by the task.
  3. CAGrad (knob at 0.5) keeps descending. Two of five runs land exactly on the star (-15.09); the other three are still on their way down, at -12.7 to -15.0, when the 20,000 steps run out. Scored by worst task, it finishes best from the default start (-13.98, against PCGrad's -10.50 and MGDA's -9.71); from the far northwest and northeast corners it is still descending at the buzzer, so its worst-task number there is mid-descent, not final.

When to use CAGrad, and what it costs

  1. Reach for it when you want PCGrad-style conflict handling and a real objective to stand behind, especially when runs from different starting points need to behave the same way.
  2. Costs: K backward passes plus one tiny solve per step. With many tasks, CAGrad-Fast works on a random handful of tasks per step and the guarantee survives: about 2x and 5x faster than PCGrad on the Meta-World benchmarks (a suite of simulated robot-arm tasks, run here with 10 and with 50 tasks at once), with similar results.
  3. Keep in mind: the proof assumes smooth losses and small steps. Real networks are messier, so treat c as a knob you tune even though it has a theoretical meaning.
  4. The hidden knob: how many tasks per step. With many tasks, the K backward passes are the real cost. Treat the CAGrad-Fast subset size like a batch size: bigger is steadier and costlier, smaller is noisier and faster.
  5. How I set the knob: I start at 0.2 to 0.5. If the worst task is still being ignored (its loss flatlines while the others fall), I raise the knob, which buys that task a louder voice. If the average loss improves too slowly, I lower the knob, since I am straying too far from the objective.
  6. The sweet spot is wide: on the toy (panels 5 and 6 of Figure 3 above), the knob at 0.5 and 0.8 descends from every start, while 0.2 still stalls from the two hardest starts at 20,000 steps.

Every method in this series reshapes the update: reweight the losses, fix the angles, or leash the step. The series ends here, with the full toolbox on the table: reweight the losses when the tasks are loud, reshape the update when they fight, and protect the worst-off task when you need a guarantee. Diagnose with gradient norms and cosines, then pick your medicine.
