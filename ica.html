Independent Component Analysis (ICA)

Unmixing signals recorded on several sensors.

Principal component analysis (PCA) and its cousin independent component analysis (ICA) both write data as a few directions times amounts; PCA asks for the directions of most spread, ICA for directions whose amounts are statistically independent, and non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) for parts that are never negative.

ICA fits once, in batch, on a whole recording; after that, unmixing a new sample is subtracting the training mean, then one matrix multiply.

2 Independent component analysis

2.1 Two microphones, two voices

Two people talk in one room, and two phones or meeting-room mics record them. Each mic hears its own blend of the two voices.

Each arrow’s weight says how much of a voice reaches a mic, and these four weights make the mixing matrix \(A\). The weights come from Hyvärinen and Oja (2000), the ICA tutorial this page follows.

The figures below also use a toy in which each voice is simply on or off around its own average, with every on and off pair equally common, so knowing one voice says nothing about the other.

Each mic adds up the voices, each weighted by its arrow in Figure 2.

2.2 Whitening with PCA

ICA first whitens the mic readings, as taught in PCA variants. Whitening removes their correlation and sets every spread to 1, so only a rotation is left to find.

Turn the cloud to its PCA axes, then shrink or stretch each axis until its spread is 1. The result is round, so no direction stands out by spread anymore.
\(\displaystyle z = V^{\mathsf{T}}x\)
Measure each sample along the PCA directions, the axes along which the mic readings spread most and least.
\(\displaystyle \tilde{z} = \Lambda^{-1/2}z\)
Divide each coordinate by the square root of its spread, so \(\tilde{z}_{j}\) is \(z_{j}\) divided by \(\sqrt{\lambda_{j}}\), and every direction ends with spread 1.
\(x\) the two mic readings at one instant, the thing being made round.\(V\) the PCA directions, the eigenvectors of the covariance \(C\) of the mic readings, one PCA direction per column; \(V^{\mathsf{T}}\), the transpose, is \(V\) with rows and columns swapped, and \(V^{\mathsf{T}} x\) measures \(x\) along those directions.\(\Lambda\) the eigenvalues of \(C\) on a diagonal, the spread along each PCA direction, and \(\lambda_{j}\) the spread along direction \(j\).\(\tilde{z}\) the whitened sample: every direction has variance 1 and no two coordinates are correlated. The card says PCA, then equalize the spreads.

Round is not separated: a whitened coordinate is still not a voice, because whitening removes correlation, not all of the mixing. Section 2.3 says what is left to undo.

2.3 Undoing the mix

Builds on the matrix inverse (Vectors and Matrices, section 8)

Independent means knowing one source tells you nothing about the other. The whitened samples are uncorrelated, yet one whitened coordinate still says something about the other, because each carries part of both voices. Uncorrelated only rules out a straight-line relation; independent rules out every relation.

Each mic hears a fixed blend of the voices, and inverting the blend gives the voices back. After whitening, the unknown blend only turns or flips the round cloud, so with two mics the whole problem is one angle.
\(\displaystyle s = Wx, \quad W = A^{-1}\)
\(A^{-1}\) takes every mic reading back to the voices that made it, which undoes the Section 2.1 card.
\(\displaystyle \tilde{z} = Ms, \quad MM^{\mathsf{T}} = I\)
\(M\) is the blend that whitening leaves: it takes the voices to the whitened mic readings of the Section 2.2 card.
\(\displaystyle W = M^{\mathsf{T}}\Lambda^{-1/2}V^{\mathsf{T}}\)
Whitening is the Section 2.2 card, and \(M^{\mathsf{T}}\) turns back; multiplied out this is \(A^{-1}\), so only \(M\) is unknown.
\(s\) the sources, the two voices at one instant.\(A\) the mixing matrix, the arrow weights of Figure 2.\(x\) what the mics record.\(W\) the unmixing matrix, the inverse of \(A\), which turns mic readings back into voices.\(M\) the blend left after whitening; \(I\) is the identity, the matrix that changes nothing, so \(M M^{\mathsf{T}} = I\) says \(M\) only turns or flips. As the tutorial puts it, “whitening solves half of the problem of ICA” (section 5.2).

Whitening made the cloud round. ICA fixes each source to variance 1, so the sources are round too; what is left can only turn or flip the cloud.

Every rotation keeps the cloud round; Section 2.4 says which one is right.

2.4 Non-Gaussianity

Gaussian is another name for the bell curve, and non-Gaussian means shaped unlike a bell.

Adding independent signals usually makes a more bell-shaped signal, the central limit theorem, so a blend looks more like a bell than either source. Turn the whitened cloud until the output looks least like a bell, and that output is one source. It works only if at most one source is a bell (the tutorial, section 3.3).

For a signal \(y\) scaled to spread 1, kurtosis is the average of \(y^{4}\) minus 3. The FastICA steps of Section 2.5 use it, and the purple ring of Figure 7 is the size of the kurtosis in each direction.

Both signals are 2 s synthetic stand-ins made in NumPy and embedded in the page, syllables with pauses between them and plucked notes, so they are illustrative. Recorded speech is spiky in the same way: its samples fit a Laplace curve, the spiky shape, better than a bell (Aroudi et al. 2015).

A bell has kurtosis 0. Spiky signals such as speech have positive kurtosis, flat ones such as the on/off toy negative, and a blend sits closer to 0 than its sources.

Each direction in the whitened plane is an angle \(\theta\) from the first PCA axis, and reading the cloud along it gives one output signal.

The on/off voices are flat, so here least bell-like means most negative; the spiky voices of Figure 7 peak instead.

Kurtosis is “very sensitive to outliers” (the tutorial, section 4.2.1), so FastICA's default uses log cosh, the logarithm of the hyperbolic cosine, a U-shaped curve; it is a smooth stand-in for \(y^{4}\) that grows slowly for large values, so one loud outlier cannot decide the answer.

Read the whitened cloud along a direction, score how far that output is from a bell, and keep the direction with the highest score; that output is one source.
\(\displaystyle y = w^{\mathsf{T}}\tilde{z}, \quad \lVert w \rVert = 1\)
Read the whitened cloud along the direction \(w\); each sample gives one output value.
\(\displaystyle \operatorname{kurt}(y) = E[y^{4}] - 3(E[y^{2}])^{2}\)
How far the output is from a bell: 0 for a bell, positive when spiky, negative when flat.
\(\displaystyle G(u) = \log \cosh u\)
Close to half of \(u^{2}\) for small \(u\), and it grows like \(\lvert u \rvert\) for large \(u\), so a loud outlier adds about its size, not its fourth power.
\(\displaystyle J(y) \propto \bigl[E[G(y)] - E[G(\nu)]\bigr]^{2}\)
The first average is the output’s; the second is the same average for a bell, a fixed number; the square counts flat and spiky outputs alike. \(J\) is largest where the output is least like a bell, at a source.
\(w\) a direction of length 1; \(y\) is the output along it; \(\theta\), in Figures 5 and 7, is the angle of \(w\) from the first PCA axis.\(E[\cdot]\) the average over samples, the expected value.\(\operatorname{kurt}(y)\) the bell test; the second term is 3 when \(y\) has spread 1.\(\nu\) a bell with the same spread as \(y\), so \(E[G(\nu)]\) is a fixed number; \(G\) scores each value; \(J\) the robust non-Gaussianity score; \(\propto\) means proportional to, equal up to a constant factor.\(\cosh\) the hyperbolic cosine, the average of \(e^{u}\) and \(e^{-u}\), a U-shaped curve.

Scanning every angle works with two mics; Section 2.5 finds the best angle in a few steps with any number of mics.

2.5 FastICA step by step

Builds on the gradient descent update (Gradient Descent, section 1.1)

FastICA climbs \(J\) of Section 2.4 using \(g\), the slope (derivative) of \(G\). The slope of log cosh is tanh, the hyperbolic tangent, an S-curve from −1 to 1, and the slope of a quarter of \(y^{4}\) is \(y^{3}\), the kurtosis form that Figure 6 steps through. As \(w\) turns, the average of \(G(y)\) changes at the rate given by the average of \(\tilde{z}\, g(y)\), the first term of the update; at the peak that average points straight along \(w\). FastICA moves \(w\) to that average, minus the part a bell would give, and reaches a source in a few steps.

Only the line matters, because \(w\) and \(-w\) give the same source up to sign (the tutorial, section 6.1).

Figure 6. Each step turns \(w\) toward a dashed source line; bars show \(y\).

Each step averages the cloud weighted by how strongly each point lies along \(w\), removes what a bell would give, and rescales. For a bell-shaped cloud that average points straight back along \(w\), as \(3w\) for the cube, so it carries no news; subtracting it leaves only the turn toward a source. Repeat until \(w\) stops turning. It is an approximate Newton step, one that uses the slope of \(g\) to set its own size, so unlike gradient descent it needs no learning rate.
\(\displaystyle w^{+} = E[\tilde{z} g(y) - g'(y) w]\)
Here \(y\) is the output of the first line of the Section 2.4 card, and \(E\) averages everything inside the brackets. The first term weights each whitened sample by \(g\) of its value along \(w\) and averages; the second term is the part a bell would give; their difference is the new direction.
\(\displaystyle w = \frac{w^{+}}{\lVert w^{+} \rVert}\)
Divide \(w^{+}\) by its length \(\lVert w^{+} \rVert\) so \(w\) has length 1 again. Repeat until \(\lvert w_{\text{old}}^{\mathsf{T}} w \rvert\) is about 1, when the line stops turning.
\(y\) the output along \(w\), as in Section 2.4.\(g\) the slope of \(G\), \(u^{3}\) in the kurtosis form and tanh in the log cosh form; repeating the step climbs \(J\) of Section 2.4 to its peak; the tanh form is scikit-learn's default.\(g'\) its slope: \(3u^{2}\) for the cube, and the average of \(3y^{2}\) is 3 on whitened data, so the second term is \(3w\).\(w_{\text{old}}\) the line before the step; the stop test compares it with the new line. More sources repeat this once per source, keeping each new \(w\) at right angles to the ones found.

2.6 What the model assumes, and what ICA cannot recover

Builds on the impulse response and convolution (Filters, section 2) and the frequency response (Filters, section 4)

Basic ICA needs at least as many mics as sources (the tutorial, section 2.1). Extra mics are fine, since whitening drops the extra dimensions (the tutorial, sections 4.5 and 5.2); with fewer mics than sources the outputs stay blends.

ICA cannot recover the order, sign or scale of the sources (the tutorial, section 2.2). As in Section 2.3, each output is fixed to variance 1, and the order and sign can change between runs.

The model also assumes an instant blend with no time delays (the tutorial, section 1). A real room breaks this: a voice reaches each mic first by the direct path, then as quieter echoes off the walls a little later, so each mic hears a blurred sum of the voice over recent time, the voice convolved with the room’s impulse response. One number per arrow of Figure 2 cannot hold that, so \(x = A s\) breaks, and since the echoes boost some frequencies and cut others, the blend differs at each frequency.

The standard fix is frequency-domain ICA (Smaragdis 1998): take the STFT of each mic and run ICA separately in each frequency bin, where the blend is again one matrix, of complex numbers. Each bin returns its voices in its own order, so the last step puts every bin in the same order, the permutation problem. For one recording in a room, NMF can model the reverberation itself. Anushiravani (2016, section 5.11) uses convolutive NMF, whose parts each span several time frames, on spectrograms to split a reverberant recording into the dry sound, the voice with no room echo, and a reverberation kernel, the room’s echo pattern over time.

3 ICA in scikit-learn

Take any two mono WAV recordings of your own at one sample rate, for example a voice and a song, and mix them by the \(A\) of Section 2.1; X has one row per sample and one column per mic, the shape scikit-learn expects.

import numpy as np
import scipy.io.wavfile as wf
import sklearn.decomposition as sd

_, s1 = wf.read("voice.wav")
_, s2 = wf.read("song.wav")
n = min(len(s1), len(s2))
S = np.c_[s1[:n], s2[:n]]
A = np.array([[2, 3], [2, 1]])
X = S @ A.T

ica = sd.FastICA(2)
S_hat = ica.fit_transform(X)
A_hat = ica.mixing_
  1. 1Reads the two sources \(s_{1}\) and \(s_{2}\), a voice and a song, cut to the same length.
  2. 2Mixes them as in the Section 2.1 card, written per row as \(S A^{\mathsf{T}}\).
  3. 3FastICA with 2 outputs; the defaults whiten to unit variance (Section 2.2) and use log cosh (Section 2.4). n_components is the one positional parameter.
  4. 4Fits and returns the sources (Section 2.4 and Section 2.5); A_hat estimates \(A\) up to column order, sign and scale (Section 2.6). With no seed the order and sign can change from run to run.
