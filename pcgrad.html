PCGrad (Projecting Conflicting Gradients)

When tasks pull against each other, cut only the part that interferes. Leave everything else alone.

The weighting methods, GradNorm and Uncertainty Weighting, balance how loud each task's loss is, by giving each loss its own weight. A task's gradient is the direction, computed from its loss (the error score that task is trying to lower), in which that loss rises fastest, so training steps the other way. PCGrad works on these directions instead: it operates on conflicting gradients, projecting away the part that fights another task and leaving the rest of each gradient untouched. Projecting here means finding the part of one gradient that lies along another task's gradient; PCGrad removes that part when it points against the other task.

Conflicting gradients are the culprit

Joint training can come out worse than independent training. Some multitask reinforcement-learning methods quietly gave up: they trained each task separately and then merged the results into one model, which throws away the efficiency the whole thing was for.

The culprit is conflicting gradients: gradients from different tasks that point away from each other, so stepping against their sum can be destructive. The damage turns severe when three conditions hold together, the tragic triad: conflicting gradients, one task much louder than the other, and high curvature, the loss surface bending so fast the slope changes inside a single step. Each of the three is dissected in “When the cut actually pays”.

Picture a deep valley with one task's pull much stronger than the other's: the combined step follows the loud task downhill, the high curvature makes the optimizer, the rule that turns each gradient into a change to the network, misjudge both moves, and the quiet task pays for it.

PCGrad's answer is to stop tuning around the gradients and operate on them directly. It works across multitask supervised learning (learning from examples that come with the right answers) and multitask RL.

The deal: drop the part of your pull that fights me

GradNorm fixes loudness by learning how loud each task should be, driven by how fast each task is training, to keep progress balanced. But loudness was never the only failure. Sometimes the directions themselves fight: two task gradients point partly against each other, and adding them lets the tasks cancel each other out before the step even happens. PCGrad does not try to fix gradient sizes; it goes straight after the direction problem.

I picture it as two people steering one cart. One pulls east and a little north, the other pulls west and a little north, so part of each person's effort is wasted fighting the other. Then they make a deal: “I will drop the part of my pull that works against you, if you drop the part of yours that works against me.” What is left still moves the cart forward for both of them. And nobody gave up any part of their pull that was actually helping.

That deal is the whole method, introduced by Yu and colleagues in 2020. PCGrad stands for projecting conflicting gradients, nicknamed gradient surgery. Its rule fits in one sentence. If two task gradients fight, cut off the part of each one that works against the other, and only then add them up.

A negative cosine marks the conflict, and the fix cuts that part

A fight is measured by the cosine of the angle between two task gradients, a number for how aligned the two arrows are that ignores their lengths: 1 when they point the same way, 0 at a right angle, and −1 when they point in opposite directions. When that cosine is below zero, the pair is conflicting, and PCGrad cuts the interfering part: the part of one gradient that points along (or against) the other.

When the pair conflicts, that part points against the other gradient, so it is exactly the piece that fights. What is left sits at a right angle to the other gradient and is kept. Then the same cut is done on the other gradient. Pairs that are not fighting are never touched, so cooperation costs nothing.

The surgery fits in two lines. Take task i's gradient and subtract the part that fights task j, and only when they fight.
giPC = gi − ⟨gi, gj⟩‖gj‖² gj
The corrected gradient giPC is task i's gradient minus its fighting part. That part is the dot product ⟨gi, gj⟩, divided by task j's squared length, times gj. The angle brackets mark the dot product of two vectors (arrows), which equals their cosine times both lengths, so it has the cosine's sign. The double bars in ‖gj‖² mark gj's length, squared.
when ⟨gi, gj⟩ < 0
Cut only when they fight. If the dot product is zero or positive, the gradients do not fight, and nothing needs cutting.

Do the same cut on both gradients and each cut arrow lands at a right angle to the other task's original gradient, so the two no longer point against each other. On Introduction to Multitask Learning, the panel “(c) Projection, one arrow's shadow on another” draws this cut with arrows you can drag.

Why this exact cut

The surgery is three choices, and each one has a reason.

  1. Why cut exactly the interfering part? For a small enough step, the interfering part is the only part of one task's gradient that the other task can feel: the leftover sits at a right angle to the other gradient, so it cannot move the other task's loss at all. Cutting less would leave some of the fight in place; cutting more would throw away parts of the gradient that were helping its own task for free. The interfering part is the smallest possible edit that makes the two gradients harmless to each other.
  2. Why cut both gradients? One-sided surgery only moves the problem: the uncut gradient still fights the cut task. With two tasks, cutting both sides leaves nothing in the sum that fights either task. With three or more, a later cut can bring some of the fight back.
  3. Why a random order? With more than two tasks, each gradient is cut against the others one by one, and a later cut can undo an earlier one. So the answer depends on the order, and shuffling it every step spreads the advantage evenly over the tasks.

One PCGrad training step

The full procedure, per training step:

  1. Compute each task's gradient on the shared parameters, the weights every task uses (one backward pass per task, the pass that works back from a task's loss to produce its gradient).
  2. For each task i, and for every other task j in random order, if the two gradients fight (a negative dot product, measured against task j's original, uncut gradient), cut the interfering part, which means subtracting the part of this gradient that points along the other task's line.
  3. Combine the edited gradients (sum or average) and hand the result to any optimizer, such as SGD (stochastic gradient descent) or Adam (an optimizer that scales each parameter's step by its recent gradients).

In the code, the @ sign is the dot product. The conflict test is the “when” line of the surgery equation, and the line under it makes the cut, the equation's first line.

import numpy as np
def pcgrad_combine(grads):
    # grads: list of per-task gradient vectors on the shared parameters
    g = [x.copy() for x in grads]
    for i in range(len(g)):
        for j in np.random.permutation(len(g)):   # random order, fairness
            if i != j and g[i] @ grads[j] < 0:     # conflict, measured against task j's original gradient
                g[i] -= (g[i] @ grads[j]) / (grads[j] @ grads[j]) * grads[j]  # remove the interfering part
    return sum(g)

When the cut actually pays

Conflict alone is not fatal. It only turns destructive when three things come together:

  1. Conflicting gradients, where the tasks pull against each other, so their cosine is negative.
  2. Dominating gradients, where one task is much louder than the other.
  3. High curvature along the combined direction, so the hopeful “everyone improves a little” picture breaks within a single step.

Yu and colleagues prove that one PCGrad step leaves the multitask loss no higher than one plain gradient descent step from the same point, under three conditions. First, the two gradients must point against each other strongly enough, and the more their lengths differ, the milder the fight that qualifies; this one condition covers the triad's first two parts. Second, the landscape must be curved enough, the triad's third part. Third, the step must be large enough, a condition outside the triad.

For two tasks with convex losses (bowl shaped, with no local traps where a run can get stuck) and a small enough step, PCGrad is proven to reach either the best point or a standoff: a point where the two gradients point in exactly opposite directions, so each cut removes the whole gradient and the run stops.

Yu and colleagues test the gains on Meta-World, a suite of simulated robot manipulation tasks (a ten-task and a fifty-task set). One shared robot policy, the rule the robot uses to pick its actions, learns all the tasks at once. It is trained with multitask SAC (soft actor-critic), a reinforcement-learning algorithm with two parts: an actor that picks the actions and a critic that scores them. The score is the average success rate across the tasks, compared with the same training run without the surgery. PCGrad improves that success rate by over 30% absolute, meaning 30 percentage points, not 30% of the starting rate.

Loss scale and the optimizer bend PCGrad's direction

PCGrad edits the task gradients at the Balance stage, and clipping and the optimizer then act on the edited sum. Two things outside PCGrad still bend its direction:

  • Loss scale. A loss multiplied by 10 gives a gradient 10 times longer, so the edited sum leans toward that task.
  • Momentum, a running average of past gradients (Adam keeps one too, its first moment). It mixes each step with earlier ones, so a part PCGrad cut on this step can come back.

Gradient Balancing Inside the Training Loop shows both effects in “The loss sets the scale every balancer sees” and “Momentum can bring back a direction PCGrad removed”.

How PCGrad compares with the other balancing methods

The two weighting methods, the volume knobs that learn each task's loss weight (GradNorm and Uncertainty Weighting), and the four gradient methods, PCGrad, GradVac, CAGrad, and MGDA, which stops at a Pareto-stationary point (where no small step lowers every task's loss at once), side by side:

MethodThe question it answers
GradNormHow loud should each task be, to keep progress balanced? (learned, driven by training dynamics)
Uncertainty WeightingHow loud should each task be, given how noisy it inherently is? (learned, driven by the data)
PCGrad (this page)How should the directions be edited so tasks stop fighting?
GradVacHow should each pair of tasks be nudged back to the agreement level where it learns best?
CAGradHow do we protect the worst-off task and stay near the average, with a guarantee?
MGDAWhat direction helps every task at once, and where does the run stop?

Each cut leaves the edited gradient at a right angle to the gradient it fought, a cosine of zero. But is zero the right target for every pair of tasks? That is the GradVac page's question.
