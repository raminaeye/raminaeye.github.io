Policy Gradients: REINFORCE, Actor-Critic and PPO

Learn the move itself, judge it with a critic, and keep each update small.

These methods learn the policy, the rule that picks the move, directly: a network turns the state into move probabilities, or into the action itself, and training shifts the policy toward moves that led to more reward. REINFORCE learns once per episode; actor-critic adds a critic that judges every step; PPO, proximal policy optimization, reuses each batch for several small updates. All three are on-policy: they learn from moves the current policy just made. Moves that are real numbers, such as a robot’s joint torques, are on Continuous-Action Actor-Critic: TD3 and SAC.

1 Policy-based methods

Builds on backpropagation (Backpropagation and Gradient Checking, section 4) and logs turning products into sums (Maximum Likelihood and EM, section 4)

A policy-based agent skips the scores. Its network turns the state into a probability for each move, and the agent samples from them. Sampling matters when any fixed move can be beaten, as in rock-paper-scissors, where the best play is each move a third of the time.

Take a language model writing a reply to the prompt of Aligning Language Models with RL, “Explain the moon landing to a 6 year old”. It picks the first token of its reply from three candidates; replies starting with “People” score best on average (toy scores, Figure 1). The reward here is a reward model’s score of the finished reply, and \(\gamma\), the number that shrinks later rewards, is 1 inside one reply; a score averaged over replies is \(Q\), one reply’s score is \(G\).

Figure 1 shows the push behind the policy gradient: three first tokens, each drawn sometimes, each draw pushing its own chance up and the others down. Each row is one drawn token, the bars above giving its chance, and the bold row at the bottom is the average of the three rows weighted by those chances, up for People and down for The and Explain. Take a draw of People: its row pushes People up and the others down, and over many draws the bold row is the direction that raises J.

Equation 1. A language model writing a reply picks each token by chance, so the way to raise its score is to push the tokens it samples. A rock-paper-scissors player who must mix moves to stay unbeaten faces the same need.
1\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#a5571a}{\pi (a\mid s)} = \frac{e^{z_{a}}}{\sum_{b}\,e^{z_{b}}}\)
Intuition. Scores from the network are not chances. This line turns each token's score into its share of one whole, so the model can draw from them.
The mathematics. This line says \( \htmlStyle{color:#a5571a}{\pi(a\mid s)} \) equals \( e^{z_a} \) over the sum over \( b \) of \( e^{z_b} \). \( \htmlStyle{color:#a5571a}{\pi(a\mid s)} \) is the chance the policy gives token \( a \) after the prompt \( s \), the bar reading given. \( e^{z_a} \) takes the token's score \( z_a \), a logit, and makes it positive; the sum over \( b \) adds that positive score over every candidate token \( b \), here People, The and Explain; dividing by the sum makes the three chances add to 1, so they are shares of one whole. The token with the highest score gets the largest chance, but every token keeps some chance, so the model can still sample a less likely one.
Example. Take the three first tokens: People scores highest, so its chance is largest, yet The and Explain keep some chance and can still be drawn.
2\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{J} = \sum_{a}\,\htmlStyle{color:#a5571a}{\pi (a\mid s)}\,\cdot \,\htmlStyle{color:#c0392b}{Q(s,\,a)}\)
Intuition. One number must say how good the policy is at this prompt, so training has something to raise.
The mathematics. This line says \( \htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{J} \) equals the sum over \( a \) of \( \htmlStyle{color:#a5571a}{\pi(a\mid s)} \) times \( \htmlStyle{color:#c0392b}{Q(s,a)} \). \( \htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{J} \) is one number for the state \( s \), the objective the policy tries to raise. The sum over \( a \) adds over every candidate token \( a \); each term multiplies \( \htmlStyle{color:#a5571a}{\pi(a\mid s)} \), the chance the policy gives that token, by \( \htmlStyle{color:#c0392b}{Q(s,a)} \), the return expected if it writes that token here and plays on; the dot is ordinary multiplication. Each term is one token's score times its chance, so adding them is adding each possible outcome times its chance: the sum is the chance-weighted average, the average score in Figure 1, which is the value \( V \) of \( s \) of RL Basics.
Example. Take the three tokens: People's best score counts most, Explain's lowest least, and the weighted average is the bold row of Figure 1.
3\(\displaystyle \nabla \,\log \,\htmlStyle{color:#a5571a}{\pi (a\mid s)} = \frac{\nabla \htmlStyle{color:#a5571a}{\pi (a\mid s)}}{\htmlStyle{color:#a5571a}{\pi (a\mid s)}}\)
Intuition. Training can only push the token actually drawn. This line turns that push into a form one drawn token can carry.
The mathematics. This line says \( \nabla \log \htmlStyle{color:#a5571a}{\pi(a\mid s)} \) equals \( \nabla \htmlStyle{color:#a5571a}{\pi(a\mid s)} \) over \( \htmlStyle{color:#a5571a}{\pi(a\mid s)} \). \( \nabla \log \htmlStyle{color:#a5571a}{\pi(a\mid s)} \) is how the log of the token's chance changes as the network's weights change, log the natural log. The fraction divides \( \nabla \htmlStyle{color:#a5571a}{\pi(a\mid s)} \), the change in the chance itself, by \( \htmlStyle{color:#a5571a}{\pi(a\mid s)} \), the chance; the division is the whole trick, because the log turns a push on the chance into that push divided by the chance. A token sampled often has a large \( \pi \), so each sample pushes it a little; a rare token has a small \( \pi \), so each sample pushes it a lot; the two cancel, so the average push stays fair. Each row of Figure 1 is this push for one drawn token.
Example. Take Explain, rarely drawn: its chance is small, so the one time it is drawn its push is large, matching the many small pushes on People.
4\(\displaystyle \nabla \htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{J} =\)
Intuition. The goal is not J itself but the direction that raises it. This line names that direction and leaves the rest to the next line.
The mathematics. This line says \( \nabla \htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{J} \) equals the sum on the next line. \( \nabla \htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{J} \) is how the policy's quality \( \htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{J} \) changes as the network's weights change. The equals sign promises the right side, on the next line, is that change written as an average over the tokens the policy samples. It is the gradient of line 2 with \( \htmlStyle{color:#c0392b}{Q} \) held fixed: only the chances move, the scores they weight stay put.
Example. Take the prompt: the direction that raises J is the average push over the three tokens, weighted by their chances.
5\(\displaystyle \sum_{a}\,\htmlStyle{color:#a5571a}{\pi (a\mid s)}\,\cdot \,\nabla \,\log \,\htmlStyle{color:#a5571a}{\pi (a\mid s)} \cdot \,\htmlStyle{color:#c0392b}{Q(s,\,a)}\)
Intuition. One drawn token must stand in for the whole average. This line shows why its single push is a fair sample of the direction.
The mathematics. This line says \( \nabla \htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{J} \) equals the sum over \( a \) of \( \htmlStyle{color:#a5571a}{\pi(a\mid s)} \) times \( \nabla \log \htmlStyle{color:#a5571a}{\pi(a\mid s)} \) times \( \htmlStyle{color:#c0392b}{Q(s,a)} \). The sum over \( a \) adds over every candidate token; each term multiplies three things: \( \htmlStyle{color:#a5571a}{\pi(a\mid s)} \), the token's chance; \( \nabla \log \htmlStyle{color:#a5571a}{\pi(a\mid s)} \), the log push of line 3; and \( \htmlStyle{color:#c0392b}{Q(s,a)} \), the token's score. Multiplying line 3 by \( \pi \) gives back \( \nabla \pi \). Each term is weighted by \( \pi \), so this line is an average over sampled tokens, and one sampled token's \( \nabla \log \pi \) times \( \htmlStyle{color:#c0392b}{Q} \) is a fair one-sample estimate. So the whole equation says this: scores become chances by softmax, J is their chance-weighted average, and the gradient is the average push over sampled tokens, the direction that raises J.
Example. Take many draws: People is drawn most often, and the average of those pushes over many draws is the direction that raises J, the bold row of Figure 1.

REINFORCE, the simplest policy-based method, learns once per episode. After one reply, REINFORCE pushes the token it sampled by the reply’s score (Figure 2).

Figure 2 shows one REINFORCE update: the token chances before and after a reply that starts with The and scores well. The bars on the left are the chances before the update, most on People and least on Explain, and the bars on the right are the chances after, with The risen toward People and Explain fallen a little. Take the sampled token The: the update pushes it up by the reply's score, so its bar grows while Explain's shrinks.

Equation 2. REINFORCE learns once per episode with no critic to judge single steps, so it needs one loss that turns a finished reply into a push on every token in it. A coach who scores a player only on the final result faces the same need.
1\(\displaystyle \text{loss} = -\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\sum_{t}}\,\log \,\htmlStyle{color:#a5571a}{\pi (a_{t}\mid s_{t})}\,\cdot \,\htmlStyle{color:#117864}{G_{t}}\)
Intuition. A finished reply must push every token in it at once. This line turns the whole reply into one number to minimize.
The mathematics. This line says the REINFORCE loss equals minus the sum over \( t \) of \( \log \htmlStyle{color:#a5571a}{\pi(a_t\mid s_t)} \) times \( \htmlStyle{color:#117864}{G_t} \). The sum runs over the tokens. \( \htmlStyle{color:#a5571a}{\pi(a_t\mid s_t)} \) is the chance the policy gave the token written at step \( t \), and its log is the log step of line 3 of the policy-gradient card. The log turns the reply's chance, a product, into this sum. \( \htmlStyle{color:#117864}{G_t} \) is the return from step \( t \) on, the reply's score in Figure 2. The minus turns raise into a loss, pushing each token up by its return. So the whole equation says this: weight each token's log chance by the return after it, take the minus, and the total pushes the better tokens up most.
Example. Take a reply that starts with People and scores best: its tokens are pushed up the most, since the reply's score weights every one of them.
Take a reply that starts with The and scores well, as in Figure 2. Every token in that reply is pushed up by the reply's score, so The rises toward People even though replies starting with People score better on average, and Explain falls a little. That rise is the noise the baseline removes next.

One REINFORCE episode on gymnasium’s CartPole:

import gymnasium as gym
import torch
from torch.distributions import Categorical

gamma = DISCOUNT
env = gym.make("CartPole-v1")
policy = torch.nn.Sequential(
    torch.nn.Linear(INPUT_DIM, HIDDEN), torch.nn.ReLU(),
    torch.nn.Linear(HIDDEN, N_MOVES))
optimizer = torch.optim.Adam(policy.parameters(), lr=LEARNING_RATE)

s, info = env.reset()
log_probs, rewards, done = [], [], False
while not done:
    dist = Categorical(logits=policy(torch.as_tensor(s)))
    a = dist.sample()
    s, r, terminated, truncated, info = env.step(a.item())
    log_probs.append(dist.log_prob(a))
    rewards.append(r)
    done = terminated or truncated
G, returns = 0.0, []
for r in reversed(rewards):
    G = r + gamma * G
    returns.insert(0, G)
G_t = torch.tensor(returns)
loss = -(torch.stack(log_probs) * G_t).sum()
optimizer.zero_grad()
loss.backward()
optimizer.step()
  1. 1Sets \(\gamma\), CartPole and a small policy network with its optimizer.
  2. 2Plays one full episode, keeping each move’s log-probability and reward.
  3. 3Computes the return \(G_{t}\) backward from the last reward.
  4. 4The loss from the REINFORCE card, then one gradient step.

Its weakness is noise: \(G_{t}\) includes the luck of every later reward, so updates vary a lot between episodes (the RL textbook, chapter 13). Subtract what the state usually returns, the average \(J\), and the push keeps its direction with far smaller swings (Figure 3).

Figure 3 shows why the baseline matters: the push on People's score from each sampled token, without and with the baseline. The top line spreads the pushes wide, from G alone, with The's push far from People's, while the bottom line gathers them tight around the same average, because the baseline takes the usual return off every push. Take the reply that starts with The: its push sits far below People's without the baseline, and near it with the baseline, so separate runs end closer together.

Equation 3. One reply is a noisy judge of a token, so each token needs judging against what its state usually returns. A coach who scores a player against the team's usual result faces the same need.
1\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{A_{t}} = \htmlStyle{color:#117864}{G_{t}} -\,\htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{b(s_{t})}\)
Intuition. One reply is a noisy judge of a token. This line scores each token against what its state usually returns.
The mathematics. This line says \( \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{A_t} \) equals \( \htmlStyle{color:#117864}{G_t} \) minus \( \htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{b(s_t)} \). \( \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{A_t} \) is the advantage of the token written at step \( t \), how much better it did than expected. \( \htmlStyle{color:#117864}{G_t} \) is the return from step \( t \) on, the reply's score; minus \( \htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{b(s_t)} \) takes off the baseline, any guess that depends on the state but not on the move, usually a learned \( V \), here the average \( \htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{J} \). A token that beats the guess has a positive advantage, one that falls short has a negative one: best for People, lower for The, lowest for Explain, a move's edge over the average as in the dueling card of Dueling DQN.
Example. Take a reply starting with People, the best on average: its return beats the usual return, so its advantage is positive.
2\(\displaystyle \text{loss} = -\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\sum_{t}}\,\log \,\htmlStyle{color:#a5571a}{\pi (a_{t}\mid s_{t})}\,\cdot \,\htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{A_{t}}\)
Intuition. The advantage must reach every token in the reply. This line swaps it in for the return in the REINFORCE loss.
The mathematics. This line says loss equals minus the sum over \( t \) of \( \log \htmlStyle{color:#a5571a}{\pi(a_t\mid s_t)} \) times \( \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{A_t} \). Loss is the baseline loss, the number training minimizes. The sum over \( t \) adds over the steps; each term multiplies \( \log \htmlStyle{color:#a5571a}{\pi(a_t\mid s_t)} \), the log chance the policy gave the token at step \( t \), by \( \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{A_t} \), the advantage from the line above, how much that token beat the guess. The minus turns raise into a loss, as in the REINFORCE card. The change is only the weight: \( \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{A_t} \) in place of \( \htmlStyle{color:#117864}{G_t} \), so a token that beat the guess is pushed up and a token that fell short is pushed down, instead of every token pushed up by a positive score.
Example. Take a reply starting with Explain that falls short of the usual return: its advantage is negative, so Explain is pushed down.

A baseline shared by every token changes nothing on average: it pushes People, The and Explain by the same amount, and the chances always add to one, so those pushes cancel to zero. The average push stays the same as REINFORCE without a baseline, with far less noise.

Each reply’s score \(G\) is its token’s \(Q\) plus some noise. With no baseline every score here is above 0, so whichever token is drawn gets pushed up. A token drawn a few times early gains chance, gets drawn more and gains more, so over many replies a run (one training from the starting chances) can lock onto a worse token. Subtracting \(b\) keeps the average push but sets how far separate runs drift apart: they agree most when \(b\) sits near \(J\), the state’s average score before any update, while a \(b\) far above \(J\) lets them drift almost as far as with no baseline, with almost every push down instead of up (Figure 5).

Intuition. Figure 4 shows the payoff of the b = J button: with the baseline set to J, each token is judged against the usual return.
The mathematics. Each dot is one token's advantage, its score minus J: People's sits above zero, The's just below, Explain's further below, so the dots spread around zero instead of clustering on one side.
Example. Take the reply starting with The: its advantage sits just below zero, so that token is pushed down a little, and separate runs end close together.

A policy that puts almost all its chance on one token stops trying the others (Figure 6).

Figure 6 shows three ways to spread chance over three tokens, and the entropy of each, measured in nats, the unit of entropy when the log is natural: uneven, even, and certain. The left bars share chance unevenly and carry middling entropy, the middle bars give a third each and carry the most, and the right bar gives everything to one token and carries no entropy. Take the even spread: each token holds a third of the chance, so the policy stays most undecided and its entropy is the highest of the three.

Equation 4. A policy that puts almost all its chance on one token stops trying the others, and a reply that locks in too early never finds a better one. A player who only ever tries one opening faces the same trap.
1\(\displaystyle H(\htmlStyle{color:#a5571a}{\pi (\cdot \mid s)}) = -\sum_{a}\,\htmlStyle{color:#a5571a}{\pi (a\mid s)}\,\cdot \,\log \,\htmlStyle{color:#a5571a}{\pi (a\mid s)}\)
Intuition. A policy that locks onto one token stops learning. This line measures how spread out its chances still are.
The mathematics. This line says \( H(\htmlStyle{color:#a5571a}{\pi(\cdot\mid s)}) \) equals minus the sum over \( a \) of \( \htmlStyle{color:#a5571a}{\pi(a\mid s)} \) times \( \log \htmlStyle{color:#a5571a}{\pi(a\mid s)} \). \( H(\htmlStyle{color:#a5571a}{\pi(\cdot\mid s)}) \) is the entropy of the whole policy from state \( s \), the dot standing for all the chances. The sum over \( a \) adds over the candidate tokens; each term multiplies \( \htmlStyle{color:#a5571a}{\pi(a\mid s)} \), the token's chance, by \( \log \htmlStyle{color:#a5571a}{\pi(a\mid s)} \), the natural log of that chance, which is below 0 for any chance under 1 and lower for rarer tokens; the minus sign in front makes \( H \) positive. When every token is equally likely the terms are all the same and \( H \) is largest; when one token is certain its log is 0 and the rest have no chance, so \( H \) is zero.
Example. Take the three tokens sharing chance equally: each term is the same and the entropy is at its largest.
2\(\displaystyle \text{loss}_{\text{total}} = \text{loss} -\,\beta \,\cdot \,\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\sum_{t}}\,H(\htmlStyle{color:#a5571a}{\pi (\cdot \mid s_{t})})\)
Intuition. The loss must pay the policy to stay undecided. This line takes a small entropy bonus off the total.
The mathematics. This line says \( \text{loss}_{\text{total}} \) equals loss minus \( \beta \) times \( \htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\sum_{t}} \) \( H(\htmlStyle{color:#a5571a}{\pi(\cdot\mid s_t)}) \). \( \text{loss}_{\text{total}} \) is the number training minimizes. Loss is the baseline loss from the card above; then minus \( \beta \) times the sum over \( t \) of \( H(\htmlStyle{color:#a5571a}{\pi(\cdot\mid s_t)}) \) takes off the entropy bonus, \( \beta \) a small weight. Subtracting the entropy rewards keeping options open: \( H \) is most when three tokens share chance equally and none when one token is certain (Figure 6), so the policy pays for locking onto one token too early. So the whole equation says this: take the baseline loss, subtract a small weight times the spread of the chances, and the total keeps the policy learning while keeping its options open.
Example. Take a reply where People gets almost all the chance and The and Explain almost none: its entropy is near zero, so the bonus is small and the loss stays high until the policy spreads some chance back.

2 Actor-critic methods

The full page is Actor-Critic: A2C and A3C.

Waiting for the end of an episode to judge every move is slow and noisy. An actor-critic agent judges each move one step later, by whether things went better or worse than it expected.

In actor-critic, the actor is the REINFORCE policy. The critic \(V(s)\) is a learned baseline that estimates the return from a state. Scoring both the state before and the state after a move lets it judge that move one step later, not at the episode’s end (the RL textbook, chapter 13). After the token The, the critic’s guess for the rest of the reply drops; that drop, \(\delta\), judges the token now (Figure 7).

Figure 7 shows one step through the actor and the critic: the actor picks The, and the world returns no reward and a next state the critic values lower. The critic's guess falls after The, so delta is negative, and the purple arrows carry that delta up to the actor and around in a loop to the critic itself, training both from the one step. Take the token The: the guess falls, delta is negative, and the actor's chance of The is pushed down while the critic's guess for the earlier state is pulled down.

Equation 5. Waiting for a whole reply to judge one token is slow and noisy, so the critic's guess replaces the wait. A coach who judges a move from the position it reaches, instead of the final result, faces the same need.
1\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{A(s,\,a)} = \htmlStyle{color:#c0392b}{Q(s,\,a)} - \htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{V(s)}\)
Intuition. Waiting for a whole reply is slow. This line judges one move against what the critic expected from its state.
The mathematics. This line says \( \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{A(s,a)} \) equals \( \htmlStyle{color:#c0392b}{Q(s,a)} \) minus \( \htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{V(s)} \). \( \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{A(s,a)} \) is the advantage of move \( a \) in state \( s \), how much better it is than the critic expects. \( \htmlStyle{color:#c0392b}{Q(s,a)} \) is the average return of that move, the score averaged over replies; minus \( \htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{V(s)} \) takes off the critic's guess for the state, the baseline card's \( \htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{b} \) with \( \htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{b} \) equal to \( \htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{V} \) and the average \( \htmlStyle{color:#c0392b}{Q} \) in place of one reply's \( \htmlStyle{color:#117864}{G} \). A move that beats the guess has a positive advantage, one that falls short has a negative one: worse than expected for The, whose reply scores less than the critic guessed.
Example. Take the token The: its reply scores less than the critic guessed, so its advantage is negative.
2\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{A} \approx \delta = \htmlStyle{color:#7d6608}{r} +\,\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\gamma }\,\cdot \,\htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{V(s')} -\,\htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{V(s)}\)
Intuition. The critic's guess can judge a move one step later. This line turns that one-step surprise into the advantage estimate.
The mathematics. This line says \( \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{A} \) is estimated by \( \delta \), and \( \delta \) equals \( \htmlStyle{color:#7d6608}{r} \) plus \( \htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\gamma} \) times \( \htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{V(s')} \) minus \( \htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{V(s)} \). \( \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{A} \) is the advantage, and almost equals reads is estimated by, so \( \delta \) is the estimate, not the advantage itself. \( \delta \) is the TD error of Q-Learning with \( \htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{V} \) in place of \( \htmlStyle{color:#c0392b}{Q} \): \( \htmlStyle{color:#7d6608}{r} \) is the one real reward, plus \( \htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\gamma} \) times \( \htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{V(s')} \), the next state's discounted guess, minus \( \htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{V(s)} \), this state's guess. In Figure 7, \( \htmlStyle{color:#7d6608}{r} \) is nothing, \( \htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\gamma} \) is 1, and \( \delta \) is negative because the guess fell after The. It estimates \( \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{A} \) from one step, only as good as the critic: it misses the true advantage for The. REINFORCE with a baseline waited for the episode's end with \( \htmlStyle{color:#117864}{G} \) minus \( \htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{V(s)} \); \( \delta \) is less noisy.
Example. Take the step after The: the guess for the rest of the reply falls, so the surprise is negative and the estimate says The did worse than expected.
3\(\displaystyle \text{actor loss} = -\log \,\htmlStyle{color:#a5571a}{\pi (a\mid s)}\,\cdot \,\htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{A}\)
Intuition. The actor learns from the surprise alone. This line pushes the move by how much it beat the guess.
The mathematics. This line says actor loss equals minus \( \log \htmlStyle{color:#a5571a}{\pi(a\mid s)} \) times \( \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{A} \). Actor loss is the one number the actor minimizes for this step. Minus \( \log \htmlStyle{color:#a5571a}{\pi(a\mid s)} \) takes the log of the chance the policy gave the move, and times \( \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{A} \) multiplies it by the advantage, here \( \delta \) from the line above. The minus sign turns raise into a loss to minimize, as in the REINFORCE card: minimizing the loss pushes the move's chance up when \( \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{A} \) is positive and down when \( \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{A} \) is negative, so the move gets more likely only if it beat the guess.
Example. Take the token The with its negative surprise: the actor loss pushes The down, so it grows less likely next time.
4\(\displaystyle \text{critic loss} = (\htmlStyle{color:#7d6608}{r}\,+\,\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\gamma }\,\cdot \,\htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{V(s')}\,-\,\htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{V(s)})^{2}\)
Intuition. The critic learns from the same surprise. This line squares it so shrinking the square teaches the critic to guess better.
The mathematics. This line says critic loss equals \( \htmlStyle{color:#7d6608}{r} \) plus \( \htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\gamma} \) times \( \htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{V(s')} \) minus \( \htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{V(s)} \), all squared. Critic loss is the number the critic minimizes. The brackets hold \( \delta \) from the line above: \( \htmlStyle{color:#7d6608}{r} \), the one real reward, plus \( \htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\gamma} \) times \( \htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{V(s')} \), the next state's discounted guess, minus \( \htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{V(s)} \), this state's guess; the power of 2 outside makes it a squared gap, so a miss either way counts. Making it smaller pulls \( \htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{V(s)} \) toward \( \htmlStyle{color:#7d6608}{r} \) plus the guess at \( s' \), held fixed as the target (no_grad in the code). Together, this is REINFORCE with a learned baseline. So the whole equation says this: judge each move against the critic's guess, push the move by how much it beat the guess, and square the one-step surprise so shrinking it teaches the critic to guess better.
Example. Take the guess for the state before The: the critic loss pulls it down toward the new lower guess, so the next guess starts closer.
Take the token The, after which the critic's guess for the rest of the reply drops. The surprise is negative, so the actor loss pushes The down and the critic loss pulls the guess for the earlier state down toward the new lower guess.

One update on the last \(B\) steps the current policy played, where no_grad holds \(V(s')\) fixed.

import torch
from torch.distributions import Categorical

gamma = DISCOUNT

def actor_critic_update(actor, critic, optimizer, batch):
    s, a, r, s_next, done = batch   # tensors, B steps each
    v_s = critic(s).squeeze(-1)
    with torch.no_grad():
        v_next = critic(s_next).squeeze(-1)
    delta = r + gamma * v_next * (1 - done) - v_s
    A = delta.detach()
    log_pi = Categorical(logits=actor(s)).log_prob(a)
    actor_loss = -(log_pi * A).mean()
    critic_loss = delta.pow(2).mean()
    optimizer.zero_grad()
    (actor_loss + critic_loss).backward()
    optimizer.step()
  1. 1Critic values and \(\delta\), eq card in Section 2, line 2.
  2. 2Actor loss with \(A\) = \(\delta\), line 3, the up arrow in Figure 7.
  3. 3Critic loss, line 4, the loop in Figure 7 that trains the critic.
  4. 4One gradient step on both losses.

A3C, asynchronous advantage actor-critic, runs many workers on copies of the world, all updating one shared network, and uses an \(n\)-step return (Figure 8) with the entropy bonus. A2C, advantage actor-critic, is the synchronous version: all copies step, then make one joint update.

Figure 8 shows the n-step return: n real rewards, then the critic's guess V for the rest. The boxes add the first rewards one by one, each shrunk by gamma, and the last box is the critic's guess at the far state, so the bracket below holds the whole return. Take the return with three real rewards: it adds the first three rewards, each shrunk by gamma, and then the critic's guess for the rest.

3 PPO clipping

The full page is Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO).

A batch of steps only describes the policy that played it, so learning from it several times is safe only while the policy stays close to that one. PPO reuses one batch of replies for several updates, but stops paying once a token’s chance has moved too far from where the batch was collected (Figure 9).

Figure 9 shows the clipped objective in two panels: the top for a move with a positive advantage, the bottom for a negative one. In each panel the dashed line is the unclipped objective and the solid line follows it inside the band from 0.8 to 1.2; past the edge on the helpful side the solid line goes flat, while on the harmful side it keeps moving. Take People: past the ratio 1.2 its line goes flat and the change counts at the edge, while The, scored badly and made more likely, keeps counting in full.

PPO is actor-critic that reuses each on-policy batch for several updates but stops rewarding any change that moves the policy far from the one that collected it. Without that stop, every extra pass pushes the same moves further using advantages measured under the old policy, the new policy drifts far from it, and performance can collapse:

Equation 6. A batch of steps only describes the policy that played it, so learning from it several times is safe only while the policy stays close to that one. A trainer who caps how far one session may move an athlete faces the same need.
1\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{\text{ratio}} = \frac{\htmlStyle{color:#a5571a}{\pi _{\text{new}}(a\mid s)}}{\htmlStyle{color:#5f6b6d}{\pi _{\text{old}}(a\mid s)}}\)
Intuition. A batch only describes the policy that played it. This line measures how far the new policy has moved from that one.
The mathematics. This line says \( \htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{\text{ratio}} \) equals \( \htmlStyle{color:#a5571a}{\pi_{\text{new}}(a\mid s)} \) over \( \htmlStyle{color:#5f6b6d}{\pi_{\text{old}}(a\mid s)} \). \( \htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{\text{ratio}} \) is one number per move and state. The fraction divides \( \htmlStyle{color:#a5571a}{\pi_{\text{new}}(a\mid s)} \), the chance the policy being trained gives the move, by \( \htmlStyle{color:#5f6b6d}{\pi_{\text{old}}(a\mid s)} \), the chance the policy that collected the batch gave it, the bar reading given in both. Above 1 the new policy makes the move more likely, below 1 less likely, and at 1 it makes it exactly as likely, unchanged. Its gradient at ratio 1 equals the log push of line 3 of the policy-gradient card, so the first update on each batch is the actor-critic step.
Example. Take the token People: the new policy gives it more chance than the old one did, so its ratio sits above 1.
2\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#117864}{\text{clipped}} = \operatorname{clip}(\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{\text{ratio}},\,1\,-\,\varepsilon ,\,1\,+\,\varepsilon )\)
Intuition. The move must stop counting past the band edge. This line clips the ratio to the band before it is used.
The mathematics. This line says \( \htmlStyle{color:#117864}{\text{clipped}} \) equals clip of \( \htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{\text{ratio}} \) between 1 minus \( \varepsilon \) and 1 plus \( \varepsilon \). \( \htmlStyle{color:#117864}{\text{clipped}} \) is the ratio after clipping, one number per move. Clip takes three things: the ratio from the line above, 1 minus \( \varepsilon \), the bottom of the band, and 1 plus \( \varepsilon \), the top; \( \varepsilon \) here is the band's half-width, not the exploration chance. A ratio inside the band comes out unchanged; a ratio above the top comes out as the top, and one below the bottom as the bottom, so Figure 9's band edges are 1 minus \( \varepsilon \) and 1 plus \( \varepsilon \).
Example. Take a ratio inside the band: it comes out unchanged, while one past the top comes out as the top.
3\(\displaystyle \text{objective} = \min (\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{\text{ratio}}\,\cdot \,\htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{A},\,\htmlStyle{color:#117864}{\text{clipped}}\,\cdot \,\htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{A})\)
Intuition. The update must take the safer of the two terms. This line picks the smaller one at every step.
The mathematics. This line says objective equals the min of \( \htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{\text{ratio}} \) times \( \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{A} \) and \( \htmlStyle{color:#117864}{\text{clipped}} \) times \( \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{A} \). Objective is the number training makes larger. Min takes the smaller of two terms: \( \htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{\text{ratio}} \) times \( \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{A} \), the plain term, and \( \htmlStyle{color:#117864}{\text{clipped}} \) times \( \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{A} \), the clipped term, each its line's ratio times the same advantage. Past the band, moving the ratio further earns nothing, while a change that hurts still counts in full: People past the edge with a positive advantage counts at the edge, while The pushed the wrong way counts in full. Together, this is the actor-critic step held inside a trust band, reused by the RLHF and GRPO tutorials (group relative policy optimization). So the whole equation says this: take the smaller of the plain and clipped terms, and raising that number is the PPO update.
Example. Take a move the new policy favors far more, with a positive advantage: its ratio sits past the top of the band, so the clipped term counts it at the edge and pushing the ratio further earns nothing.

One PPO update over a rollout, the steps just played, EPOCHS passes over shuffled minibatches, small slices of the rollout used for one update each:

import torch
from torch.distributions import Categorical

EPS_CLIP, EPOCHS, MINIBATCH = CLIP_WIDTH, N_EPOCHS, MINIBATCH_SIZE   # CleanRL ppo.py

def ppo_update(actor, optimizer, s, a, old_log_pi, A):
    old_log_pi, A = old_log_pi.detach(), A.detach()
    for epoch in range(EPOCHS):
        for mb in torch.randperm(len(s)).split(MINIBATCH):
            dist = Categorical(logits=actor(s[mb]))
            ratio = torch.exp(dist.log_prob(a[mb]) - old_log_pi[mb])
            clipped = ratio.clamp(1 - EPS_CLIP, 1 + EPS_CLIP)
            objective = torch.min(ratio * A[mb], clipped * A[mb])
            loss = -objective.mean()
            optimizer.zero_grad()
            loss.backward()
            optimizer.step()
  1. 1Freezes old log-probabilities and advantages; loops over epochs and minibatches.
  2. 2Ratio, eq card in Section 3, line 1: exp of a difference of logs is the ratio of the chances.
  3. 3Clip, line 2, the band in Figure 9.
  4. 4Objective, line 3, turned into a loss.
  5. 5One gradient step per minibatch.

PPO gets \(A\) from GAE, a weighted blend of the one-step \(\delta\) over the next steps. PPO is also the RL step of RLHF, reinforcement learning from human feedback, in Aligning Language Models with RL.
