Training GANs: Mode Collapse, WGAN and the Move to Diffusion

Why training generative adversarial networks (GANs) breaks, and the fixes, including the Wasserstein GAN (WGAN).

Before you start Minimax Optimal discriminator Autograd Instance normalization

The generative adversarial network (GAN) game has no single loss that goes down. When the discriminator wins too easily, the generator’s signal vanishes or turns noisy; when the generator finds one image that fools it, it makes only that kind. The fixes change what the judge measures (the Wasserstein distance, scored by a critic, a judge that scores instead of classifying) and keep the critic’s slope in bounds (clipping, then a gradient penalty).

As in Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), x is one image, G the generator, D the discriminator, z its noise from pz, and pdata and pg the real and generated images; the Jensen-Shannon (JS) divergence compares them.

2 Mode collapse

Builds on quality and diversity (Generative Models: What They Learn and How They Differ, section 3)

A mode is one kind of image the data has, such as all 3s. The generator can fool the discriminator with one kind of sample, so the gradient stops rewarding the others; the discriminator then learns to reject that kind, and the generator hops to another (Figure 1, panel 1).

3 Why a perfect discriminator stops teaching

Builds on the average over many draws, E[X] (Probability and Bayes Rule, section 2), the JS divergence and its value at no overlap (GANs, section 4) and the non-saturating generator loss (GANs, section 5)

The WGAN paper (Arjovsky et al. 2017) puts real data on a vertical line at 0 and generated data on a parallel line a distance θ away, with θ being the generator’s only setting. JS stays at log 2, about 0.693, for every nonzero θ, whether θ is 0.1 or 10, so its slope with respect to θ is 0. The Wasserstein distance W, defined in the equation of this section, is |θ|, which slopes toward 0.

Neither G loss escapes the flat JS, because the minimax slope vanishes (Figure 1, panel 2) and the non-saturating slope turns unstable and noisy (Arjovsky and Bottou 2017). Real and generated 64x64 images each lie on a thin, low-dimensional surface inside pixel space, so the two surfaces barely overlap.

W is the least total work, mass times distance, needed to turn one pile into the other. “Earth mover’s distance” is the field’s other name for it.
W(p, q) = infγ ∈ Π(p, q)
E(x, y)∼γ[‖x − y‖]
x and y are images from piles p and q. A transport plan γ says how much mass moves from each x to each y. Π(p, q) is the set of plans whose totals match the two piles. ‖x − y‖ is the distance each bit moves, E under γ averages it over the plan, and inf takes the cheapest plan.

The GAN measured JS (GANs, section 4); W replaces it with this distance, whose slope Figure 2 shows.

4 WGANs use a critic, not a classifier

Builds on the minimax inner max (GANs, section 3)

Images live in [−1, 1]. Real background pixels are −1.0 (black), and the generator paints them −0.4 (grey).

The critic gives real images high scores and generated ones low scores, but its slope may never exceed 1, so the score gap measures distance.
W = max1-Lipschitz f
Ex∼pdata[f(x)]
− Ez∼pz[f(G(z))]
1-Lipschitz means the slope is never steeper than 1 anywhere, so the gap cannot be inflated. f outputs any real number, not a probability (no log, no sigmoid), so it is a critic rather than a classifier. The first average is over real images and the second over generated ones, so the max picks the scorer that separates them most.
The two forms of W are equal (the Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality, as the WGAN paper shows): the least moving cost equals the largest score gap a slope-limited scorer can find, and a slope limit K instead of 1 only scales it by K. The max is the inner max of the minimax objective (GANs, section 3).
G wants the critic to score its images high.
LG = −Ez∼pz[f(G(z))]
Only the second term of the critic’s form of W depends on G, so the first drops out of G’s loss. The minus sign turns “score high” into a loss to minimize. f has no log, so the loss never flattens the way the GAN’s minimax loss does.

Same goal as the generator loss of GANs, section 5, with no log and no sigmoid.

4.1 Keeping the critic’s slope under 1

WGAN clamps each critic weight to [−c, c] after every step, with c = 0.01. Bounded weights bound the critic’s slope. A large c lets the weights take a long time to reach their limit, so the critic trains slowly; a small c makes its gradients vanish.

5 Gradient penalty keeps the critic smooth

Builds on the gradient and its size (Gradient Descent, section 1) and backprop through a gradient (Backpropagation and Gradient Checking, section 5)

Clipped critics learn overly simple functions, and depending on c their gradients vanish or explode (Gulrajani et al. 2017). Their fix keeps the same pixels as Figure 4 and charges the critic for its slope instead. The slope is checked at a mix point x, a random blend of a real and a generated pixel with mix weight u, and the penalty weight λ sets how much a wrong slope costs.

One iteration of WGAN with the gradient penalty (WGAN-GP) on a toy dataset, a ring of 8 point clusters, each a mode.

Step 1 of 6

Figure 7. The penalty checks the critic’s slope between real and fake.

A real and a generated batch.
Push real scores up and generated scores down, and pay whenever the slope between them drifts from 1.
x = u·x
+ (1 − u)·G(z)
u ∼ uniform(0, 1)
x is a random point on the line between a real and a generated image, one per pair, and u is the mix weight. The paper proves the best critic has slope 1 on lines between optimally paired points; random pairs are the practical stand-in, so the penalty checks there.
Lcritic = E[f(G(z))]
− E[f(x)]
+ λ·E[(‖∇x f(x)‖ − 1)2]
Lcritic is the critic’s loss. ∇x f(x) is the slope with respect to the input image, not the critic’s weights. ‖·‖ is the slope’s size for each image, then averaged over the batch. The square makes the penalty two-sided, so a slope of 0.5 is charged like 1.5, unlike a hard “at most 1”. λ weights the penalty.
The first two terms are the score gap of the critic’s form of W (Section 4), sign flipped, so minimizing this loss maximizes it. The third term replaces the hard clipping of Section 4.1 with a soft version of the same slope limit. The paper writes ε for u. Spectral normalization (Miyato et al. 2018) is the other way, dividing each layer’s weights by their largest stretch, as on Normalization Layers, section 4.

A plain GAN and WGAN-GP each train on that ring for 3,000 steps from the same start.

Judge

Figure 8. G can fool D with one or two kinds and drop the rest; here it does.

On images the same collapse is measured by counting modes: Metz et al. (2017) stack three handwritten digits into one image, giving 1,000 possible triples, and count how many a GAN produces.

6 A WGAN-GP step in PyTorch

Builds on Adam (Adaptive Optimizers: AdaGrad, RMSProp and Adam, section 4) and root mean square propagation (RMSProp) (section 3)

WGAN and WGAN-GP run ncritic = 5 critic steps per generator step (the setting of both papers). State is G, the critic and their two optimizers. The penalty’s create_graph=True adds roughly one more backward pass per critic step.

One WGAN-GP iteration on a random stand-in batch.

import torch, torch.nn as nn
G = nn.Sequential(  # noise to a 28x28 image: 7 to 14 to 28
    nn.Linear(100, 128 * 7 * 7), nn.Unflatten(1, (128, 7, 7)),
    nn.ConvTranspose2d(128, 64, 4, 2, 1), nn.BatchNorm2d(64), nn.ReLU(),
    nn.ConvTranspose2d(64, 1, 4, 2, 1), nn.Tanh())
f = nn.Sequential(  # the critic: a raw score, layer norm, no sigmoid
    nn.Conv2d(1, 64, 4, 2, 1), nn.LeakyReLU(0.2),
    nn.Conv2d(64, 128, 4, 2, 1), nn.GroupNorm(1, 128), nn.LeakyReLU(0.2),
    nn.Flatten(), nn.Linear(128 * 7 * 7, 1))
opt_f, opt_G = [torch.optim.Adam(m.parameters(), lr=1e-4, betas=(0.0, 0.9)) for m in (f, G)]
real, lam = torch.rand(128, 1, 28, 28) * 2 - 1, 10
for _ in range(5):  # n_critic critic steps
    fake, u = G(torch.randn(128, 100)).detach(), torch.rand(128, 1, 1, 1)
    x_hat = (u * real + (1 - u) * fake).requires_grad_(True)
    grad, = torch.autograd.grad(f(x_hat).sum(), x_hat, create_graph=True)
    penalty = ((grad.flatten(1).norm(dim=1) - 1) ** 2).mean()
    loss_f = f(fake).mean() - f(real).mean() + lam * penalty
    opt_f.zero_grad(); loss_f.backward(); opt_f.step()
loss_G = -f(G(torch.randn(128, 100))).mean()
opt_G.zero_grad(); loss_G.backward(); opt_G.step()
  1. 1Two small networks for 28x28 digits. The critic f returns a raw score with no sigmoid, and GroupNorm(1, 128) is layer normalization over each image. Adam for each, with β1 = 0 and β2 = 0.9.
  2. 2The mix point x (Section 5), one weight u per image; the fakes are cut off from G’s graph.
  3. 3The slope with respect to x, kept in the graph so the penalty can be backpropagated.
  4. 4The penalty, one norm per image, the third term of the critic loss.
  5. 5The whole critic loss, and the critic step.
  6. 6One G step on the generator loss LG (Section 4).

  • The critic’s W estimate tracks sample quality, so log it (the WGAN paper shows this).

7 StyleGAN controls what changes

Builds on instance normalization (Normalization Layers, section 2.3)

StyleGAN is GAN quality at its peak on narrow domains such as face photos (Karras et al. 2019). A mapping network turns z into a style vector w, and w sets each layer’s scale and shift through adaptive instance normalization (AdaIN), the move Guided Diffusion, section 5 reuses. Take one channel of a StyleGAN face generator.

Wipe the channel’s own mean and spread, then stamp on the ones the style asks for.
AdaIN(h, w) =
scale(w)·h − μ(h)σ(h)
+ shift(w)
h is one channel’s values and w the style vector. μ(h) and σ(h) are the channel’s mean and standard deviation over its pixels (instance normalization, Normalization Layers, section 2.3), so the first part erases the channel’s own style. scale(w) and shift(w) are computed from w, so the style decides the new spread and centre.

The WGAN and WGAN-GP losses change how G is graded; AdaIN changes G’s layers. The StyleGAN paper writes AdaIN(x, y). The diffusion transformer of Guided Diffusion, section 5 uses the same move to inject the step and the class. Use StyleGAN when one network call per image matters.

8 What GANs leave unsolved, and what they kept

Builds on mode collapse and vanishing gradients (sections 2 and 3 of this page) and likelihoods (Generative Models, section 1)

  1. GANs give no likelihood to check, because pg can only be sampled (GANs, section 4).
  2. Diffusion trains one network with one stable regression loss. On ImageNet, a benchmark of 1.2 million photos in 1,000 classes, at 256x256 and class-conditional, Dhariwal and Nichol (2021) report higher recall for diffusion than for BigGAN-deep, a large class-conditional GAN, where recall is the share of real images the generator can produce something close to. With classifier guidance (a separate classifier steering samples, Guided Diffusion, section 2), its Fréchet Inception Distance (FID), the distance between the feature statistics of real and generated images inside Inception, a trained image classifier (lower is better), passes the best GAN. In the same table unguided diffusion (FID 10.94) does not beat BigGAN-deep (6.95).
  3. GANs need one network call per image; diffusion needs hundreds, which Faster Diffusion Sampling tackles.
  4. Adversarial losses live on. Stable Diffusion’s autoencoder is trained with an adversarial loss (Rombach et al. 2022; Guided Diffusion, section 4), and adversarial distillation trains few-step diffusion samplers (Sauer et al. 2023).

What if we generate by removing noise a little at a time, with one plain regression loss? Diffusion Models: Adding Noise on a Schedule builds that, and Generative Models, section 1 places it among the families.
