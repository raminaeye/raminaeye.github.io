Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)

Write a group of answers to one question and let them set their own bar, so no second scoring network, the critic, is needed.

This page builds on RL Basics and Aligning Language Models with RL. The terms below are the ones this page keeps using.

  • An agent is the learner that picks the moves. A state is the agent’s situation right now; a move, or action, is one choice the agent can make in a state; a reward is the number the world hands back after each move, good or bad. (RL Basics)
  • A token is a word or a piece of a word; a language model writes its reply one token at a time. (Aligning Language Models with RL)
  • Here the agent is the language model. Here the state is the prompt plus the tokens written so far, and the move is the next token. Here the reward is one number for the whole reply, given when the reply ends.
  • RL, short for reinforcement learning, is learning which moves to make from rewards rather than from labelled answers. (RL Basics)
  • A policy is the agent’s rule for choosing a move in each state; it may always pick the same move or pick by chance. The policy is the language model being trained to write replies. (RL Basics, Aligning Language Models with RL)

1The critic is as big as the model

The usual way to train a language model with reinforcement learning needs a second network, a critic, of about the same size as the model, which adds a whole second model to train and a large memory and compute cost.

The critic has to guess, at every point while the answer is written, how the whole answer will be rewarded, though the reward arrives only once at the end, so its guesses are hard to make accurate.

Its guess is the score the answer can be expected to get, on average over the ways it could turn out, and each answer is compared with it so the update knows which tokens to push up and which down.

The running example is a competition math question with one known final answer. The model writes its reasoning and then its final answer, and a program checks whether that answer matches the known one.

GRPO, group relative policy optimization, keeps the usual reinforcement learning update and replaces the critic with a group of answers to the same question.

GRPO was introduced in the DeepSeekMath paper.

The answer in Figure 1 is marked right only when its final answer matches the known one.

2Let the other answers set the bar

Each answer is compared with the other answers written for the same question, and that comparison decides its push.

For a math question, the model writes an answer and gets one reward for it.

The final answer sits in the last few tokens of the answer in Figure 1.

A program that checks the final answer can give the reward.

While it trains, the model writes several answers to each math question.

GRPO is an RL method built for this setting, where one reward arrives at the end of each answer.

The DeepSeekMath paper, where GRPO comes from, used a learned reward model instead, a network trained to score math answers from solutions that were checked automatically. (Reward Models from Human Preferences)

The policy gradient pushes each move the agent played by a score, written \(\Psi_t\), of how well it turned out, up when the score is positive and down when it is negative. (Policy Gradients: REINFORCE, Actor-Critic and PPO)

Here the score \(\Psi_t\) of each token is built later on this page from the group of answers.

With a right answer rewarded one and a wrong one zero, the raw reward would never push a wrong answer down and would push every right answer by the same amount.

So every update needs a bar to compare each answer’s reward with.

The return is all the reward still to come, with later rewards counted a little less. (RL Basics)

Here the reward arrives when the reply ends and later rewards are not counted any less, so the return from any point in a reply is the reward the finished reply gets.

An expected value is the average over everything that can happen by chance, weighted by how likely each outcome is. (Probability and Bayes Rule, RL Basics)

That bar is the score an answer can be expected to get.

A critic is a second network that guesses each state’s value, the return expected from it. (Actor-Critic: A2C and A3C)

PPO, proximal policy optimization, is the usual RL method for language models.

GRPO drops the critic and gets that bar another way.

A baseline is the usual result from a state, subtracted from the return so the push is less noisy. (REINFORCE and the Baseline)

Here K is the number of answers written for one question, the same role K plays on Reward Models for the replies to one prompt.

GRPO gets its baseline without a critic, by writing K answers to the same question and using their average reward as the expected score.

3Equations for the group comparison and the update

3.1Advantage from the group

A move’s advantage is how much better it is expected to do than the usual result from that state. (Dueling DQN)

Here the usual result is the group’s average.

In GRPO, an answer’s advantage is how much better it scored than the average of its group, the answers written for the same question, divided by how far apart the group’s scores are.

An answer that beat its group’s average is pushed up, and one below it is pushed down.

Equation 1. The group advantage. Each answer is scored against its own group, not against a critic’s guess.

So line 1 says how far answer i beat its group, measured against how far apart the group’s scores are.

3.2How far each token has moved

Here a batch is a set of prompts and the replies the policy just wrote for them, trained on together.

Each GRPO update uses the answers the policy has just written.

PPO is the policy-gradient method that makes small, limited updates so one batch cannot wreck the policy. (Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO))

The old policy is the policy as it was when it wrote these replies.

The ratio \(\rho_{i,t}\) compares the new policy’s chance of the move taken with the old policy’s chance; at one, nothing has changed. (Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO))

A ratio above one means the token has become more likely since the answers were written.

Equation 2. The token ratio. How far each token’s chance has moved since the answers were written.
\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt sym-rho}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-rho)}{\rho_{i,t}}}(\theta)=\dfrac{\htmlClass{eqt sym-pi}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-pi)}{\pi_\theta}}\big(\htmlClass{eqt sym-y}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-y)}{y_{i,t}}}\mid\htmlClass{eqt sym-x}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-x)}{x}},\,\htmlClass{eqt sym-y}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-y)}{y_{i,<t}}}\big)}{\htmlClass{eqt sym-pi}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-pi)}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}}}\big(\htmlClass{eqt sym-y}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-y)}{y_{i,t}}}\mid\htmlClass{eqt sym-x}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-x)}{x}},\,\htmlClass{eqt sym-y}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-y)}{y_{i,<t}}}\big)}\)

3.3Clip, average and pay for drift

Clipping holds the ratio between two clip edges set by \(\epsilon_{\text{clip}}\), so an update stops rewarding a helpful change once it passes an edge. (Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO))

Here each move is a token, so in one update each token’s probability can move only so far from where it was.

GRPO clips every token’s ratio this way.

SFT, supervised fine-tuning, trains the model on replies people wrote with the ordinary next-token loss; RL usually starts from that model. (Aligning Language Models with RL)

DeepSeekMath’s starting policy, DeepSeekMath-Instruct, is such an SFT model.

The reference model \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\) is a frozen copy of the SFT model. (Aligning Language Models with RL)

KL measures how different two probability distributions are, zero when they match; the KL penalty charges the policy for drifting away from the reference model. (Aligning Language Models with RL)

GRPO adds this penalty straight to its loss, not to each token’s reward (PPO and GRPO side by side).

The sign \(\sim\) means drawn at random from. (RL Basics)

\(1\pm\epsilon_{\mathrm{clip}}\) means the range from one minus \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{clip}}\) to one plus it.

\(\beta\) is the strength of the KL penalty.

So one GRPO update raises answers that beat their group and lowers the rest, gives a token no extra push once its ratio passes the clip edge in the direction of its push, and pays for drifting from the reference.

Equation 3. The GRPO objective. PPO’s update with group advantages, averaged over each answer’s tokens and then over the group, minus a KL penalty. This is maximized.
\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt sym-J}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-J)}{\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{GRPO}}}}(\theta)=\htmlClass{eqt sym-E}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-E)}{\mathbb{E}}}_{\htmlClass{eqt sym-x}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-x)}{x}},\{\htmlClass{eqt sym-y}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-y)}{y_i}}\}\sim\htmlClass{eqt sym-pi}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-pi)}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}}}}\Bigg[\)
Example: one batch of math questions, K answers each. Intuition: the update is PPO’s, with the critic’s guesses replaced by group advantages. With one update per batch the ratio equals one in value, so lines 3 and 4 agree and the clip is idle. Yet the ratio is the exponential of the gap between the new and old log-probabilities, \(\rho = \exp(\log \pi_{\theta} - \log \pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}})\), so a small change in the log-probability changes the ratio by the ratio times that change. At ratio one that factor is one, so the two nudges are the same size, and the gradient of line 3 is the advantage times the gradient of the token’s log-probability, the log of its chance (DeepSeekMath App A.1.6, Eq 20): the push is still there. The braces \(\{y_i\}\) hold the K answers the old policy wrote for one question; \(\mathbb{E}\) is the expected value reminded above, so line 1 averages over questions and the answers written for them.
\(\displaystyle \quad\dfrac{1}{\htmlClass{eqt sym-K}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-K)}{K}}}\sum_{i=1}^{\htmlClass{eqt sym-K}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-K)}{K}}}\dfrac{1}{|\htmlClass{eqt sym-y}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-y)}{y_i}}|}\sum_{t=1}^{|\htmlClass{eqt sym-y}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-y)}{y_i}}|}\)

\(\displaystyle \quad\Big\{\min\Big(\htmlClass{eqt sym-rho}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-rho)}{\rho_{i,t}}}\htmlClass{eqt sym-A}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-A)}{\hat A_{i,t}}},\)
min takes the smaller of the two, so maximizing \(\rho\) times \(\hat A\) raises the probability of tokens in answers with positive advantage and lowers it for negative ones; this is the policy-gradient idea (Policy Gradients: REINFORCE, Actor-Critic and PPO). The braces opened here close on line 5, holding the clipped term and the KL term together inside both sums.
\(\displaystyle \quad\operatorname{clip}\big(\htmlClass{eqt sym-rho}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-rho)}{\rho_{i,t}}},\,1\pm\htmlClass{eqt sym-eps}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-eps)}{\epsilon_{\mathrm{clip}}}}\big)\,\htmlClass{eqt sym-A}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-A)}{\hat A_{i,t}}}\Big)\)
Clip keeps the ratio inside the range \(1\pm\epsilon_{\mathrm{clip}}\), and with the min a token that already moved further earns no extra push, so a batch reused for several updates cannot move the model far.

The braces opened on line 3 close here. The KL penalty: \(\mathrm{KL}_t\) measures, at token t, how far the policy has moved from the reference model, using a never-negative per-token estimate of the KL that is zero when the two agree on that token (KL Divergence); \(\beta\) is its strength; subtracting it pulls the policy back toward the reference, so it cannot drift into odd text while chasing reward. Here the KL is in the objective directly, not folded into the reward as in RLHF, so it does not change \(\hat A\).

3.5Scoring the whole answer or each step

Outcome supervision gives one reward per answer, shared by all its tokens, as in the group advantage card. Process supervision has a process reward model, a reward model that scores each reasoning step, one piece of the working that leads to the final answer, and credits each token with the step scores from its step to the end, each normalized across the group the same way as the group advantage card. This credit is like a return that counts every later step fully.

DeepSeekMath’s runs use outcome rewards unless stated.

4.1What runs and how often

Each iteration runs the policy (trained) and reference (frozen) language models, plus the reward model or the checker. Each question gets K answers per iteration.

Iterative GRPO works in rounds: after each round the reference is reset to the current policy, and the reward model keeps training on a mix of new answers and some saved older ones.

5Group advantages and the GRPO loss

Shapes: per-token log-probabilities (num_answers, length) with a mask for padding; group_advantages returns one row of K advantages per question, and adv holds one value per answer in the same order as the answers. The function returns the negative of \(\mathcal{J}\), so minimizing it maximizes \(\mathcal{J}\). logp_old and logp_ref come from frozen models and are computed without gradients; only logp carries the policy’s gradient. With one update per batch, ratio is one in value, but the ratio is the exponential of the gap between the new and old log-probabilities, so a small change in the log-probability changes the ratio by the ratio times that change, and at ratio one that factor is one, so the two nudges are the same size and ratio still carries that gradient, as the GRPO objective card explains. The code line ratio = torch.exp(logp - logp_old) is the same fact in code.

import torch
def group_advantages(rewards, eps=1e-6):          # rewards: (num_questions, K)
    mean = rewards.mean(dim=1, keepdim=True)
    std = rewards.std(dim=1, keepdim=True, unbiased=False)
    return (rewards - mean) / (std + eps)         # [1] the group advantage card; all-equal group gives zeros
def grpo_loss(logp, logp_old, logp_ref, adv, mask, eps_clip=0.2, beta=0.04):
    logp_old, logp_ref = logp_old.detach(), logp_ref.detach()  # no gradient
    ratio = torch.exp(logp - logp_old)            # [2] the token ratio card, per token
    a = adv.unsqueeze(-1)                         # every token shares its answer's advantage
    surr = torch.min(ratio * a, ratio.clamp(1 - eps_clip, 1 + eps_clip) * a)  # [3] lines 3 and 4 of the GRPO objective card
    log_u = logp_ref - logp
    kl = torch.exp(log_u) - log_u - 1             # [4] per-token KL estimate, never negative
    per_tok = (surr - beta * kl) * mask           # line 5 of the GRPO objective card
    per_ans = per_tok.sum(-1) / mask.sum(-1)      # [5] 1/|y_i|, then 1/K below
    return -per_ans.mean()                        # negative of J, minimized
  1. 1The group advantage card; an all-equal group gives zeros.
  2. 2The token ratio card, per token.
  3. 3Lines 3 and 4 of the GRPO objective card.
  4. 4Per-token KL estimate, never negative.
  5. 51/|y_i|, then 1/K below.

7GRPO next to other ways of training on rewarded answers

Rejection sampling fine-tuning (RFT) writes answers, keeps the right ones and fine-tunes on them.

Online RFT does the same with answers from the current model as it trains.

GRPO sizes each answer’s push by its advantage.

DPO, direct preference optimization, trains on fixed preference pairs with one loss and no reward model.

In DeepSeekMath’s comparison, the DPO pairs were built by a program checking which answer was right.

GRPO’s reward can come from a program or from a reward model.

GRPO beats online RFT because it pushes wrong answers down and sizes each answer’s push by its reward relative to the group.

8Running GRPO in practice

TRL, Hugging Face’s open library for training language models with these methods, has a GRPOTrainer. Its defaults have changed between releases, so set the KL weight (beta), the normalization (scale_rewards: True divides by the group spread, False keeps the raw reward gaps, “batch” divides by the spread over the batch) and the loss type (loss_type: “grpo” averages per answer, “dapo” averages over tokens, “dr_grpo” divides by a constant, removing the length bias) explicitly and check the GRPOConfig docs for the version you install.

The learning rate \(\eta\) sets how big a step each update takes. (RL Basics)

DeepSeekMath makes one update per sampled batch, so the clip never acts. More updates per batch would reuse each batch and let the clip act, at a risk of drift.

The paper trained on math questions with step-by-step solutions, from GSM8K and MATH only. Its reward came from a learned reward model, not a rule checker, and that reward model’s training data was built automatically, following Math-Shepherd, which labels each step by checking whether continuations from it reach the right answer, with no human labels.

GRPO beats online RFT. Process supervision beats outcome supervision in their runs. Iterative RL helps most in its first round.

DeepSeek-R1 later ran GRPO with rule rewards instead of a learned reward model.

9When GRPO is the right tool

On-policy means learning from answers the same policy just wrote. (SARSA)

Policy gradients are on-policy, because each push counts a move as often as the current policy plays it, so play by an older or different policy would push by the wrong amounts. (Actor-Critic: A2C and A3C)

GRPO is on-policy; it writes fresh answers every iteration.

  • Rewards that rank answers for the same question, from checkers or from a reward model.
  • Long answers where a per-token critic is hard.
  • Memory-limited RL, where a second model of the same size does not fit.

Use DPO when you only have fixed pairs and no way to score new answers.

10How group advantages go wrong

DAPO is a later GRPO variant from ByteDance.

Dr. GRPO is a corrected GRPO from Liu and colleagues.

In GRPO each answer’s push depends on how far apart the group’s scores are and on its length.

Entropy \(\mathcal{H}\) is how undecided the policy is; it is high when the moves are about equally likely and zero when one move is certain. (REINFORCE and the Baseline)

Entropy collapse means the model’s choices become nearly certain.

Then the answers in a group look alike and the model stops trying different answers.

Reward hacking is when the policy finds replies that score high without being good. (RL Basics)

A checker that compares final answers is much harder to fool than a learned reward model.

\(\mathrm{Pass}@n\) is the chance that at least one of n sampled answers is right.

\(\mathrm{Maj}@n\) is the accuracy of the most common answer among n samples.

DeepSeekMath found that RL raised \(\mathrm{Maj}@n\) but left \(\mathrm{Pass}@n\) about flat.

11From math rewards to long reasoning

GRPO with rule rewards grows long reasoning. Long reasoning is a long chain of thought written before the final answer. Longer chains reach right answers more often, so the update keeps the longer successful chains.

What happens if the reward is just a program that checks the answer, and you run GRPO at scale?

DeepSeek-R1 answers that question, with rewards a program checks and GRPO at scale.
