Faster Diffusion Sampling: DDIM and Flow Matching

Fewer, bigger steps from noise, with denoising diffusion implicit models (DDIM) and flow matching.

Before you start Clean-image estimate Reverse (denoising) step Forward process, closed form Gradient

The original sampler of denoising diffusion probabilistic models (DDPM; Ho et al. 2020) takes all 1,000 small random steps, so one image costs 1,000 network calls, the call-count row of the map on Generative Models. Reading the network’s noise guess as an arrow toward likely images, the score, explains why the same trained network can take far fewer, larger steps that follow that arrow without adding fresh noise. DDIM (Song, Meng and Ermon 2021) does this with no retraining, and the diffusion probabilistic model solver (DPM-Solver) takes even fewer. Flow matching trains a model on straight paths, so a few large steps stay on course; Stable Diffusion 3 (SD3) uses it.

Sampling runs once per generated image, after training. Its cost is the number of network calls. The only state carried between steps is the current noisy image \(x_{t}\).

1 The original sampler takes 1,000 small random steps

Builds on the reverse step (Training a Diffusion Model: Predicting the Noise, section 3) and the Gaussian (Common Probability Distributions, section 5)

Sampling starts from \(x_{T}\) drawn from N(0, \(I\)), where the forward process ends (Diffusion Models, section 3). The symbols are those of the training page: \(x_{t}\) is the noisy image at step \(t\), \(\bar\alpha_{t}\) the clean variance left, and \(\varepsilon _{\theta }\) the network’s noise guess. Follow one pixel at step 500.

Figure 3. Each step is a small denoise plus a little fresh noise.

Start from the current noisy pixel \(x_{500}\).
Step to the network’s best guess of the previous pixel, then add back a little fresh noise.
\(\displaystyle x_{t-1} = \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=mu}{\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\mu _{\theta }(x_{t}, t)}}} + \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=z}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\sigma _{t}\cdot z}}}\)
\(\displaystyle z\ \text{from } N(0, I)\)
\(\mu _{\theta }\) is the network’s step back, built from its noise guess. \(\sigma _{t}\) is the reverse spread, \(\tilde{\beta}_{t}\) or \(\beta _{t}\) under the root, as in the reverse step. \(z\) is fresh noise, a different draw from the training noise \(\varepsilon\). No noise is added on the last step.
It is the network’s step plus noise, repeated 1,000 times.

2 The score points to likely images

Builds on slope as a direction (Gradient Descent, section 1), the log-density (Maximum Likelihood and EM, section 4) and the Gaussian (Common Probability Distributions, section 5)

Score-based models (Song and Ermon 2019) learn the score, the arrow of Figure 4, directly; Song et al. (2021) showed they and DDPM are one family. Figure 4 is the running pixel’s own noisy Gaussian at step 500.

The noise guess, flipped and scaled, is an arrow toward the data.
\(\displaystyle \text{score}(x) = \nabla _{x} \log p(x)\)
\(x\) is any image, and \(\nabla_{x}\) is the slope in every pixel direction at once. log \(p\) is the log-density, so the score points uphill in likelihood.
\(\displaystyle \nabla _{x_{t}} \log q(x_{t} | x_{0})\)
\(\displaystyle = \frac{-\varepsilon }{\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_{t}}}\)
Line 2 is the score of one image’s own noisy Gaussian, the arrow of Figure 4: it points from the noisy pixel back toward the centre. \(\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_{t}}\) is the noise weight of the jump.
\(\displaystyle \nabla _{x_{t}} \log p_{t}(x_{t})\)
\(\displaystyle \approx \frac{-\varepsilon _{\theta }(x_{t}, t)}{\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_{t}}}\)
\(p_{t}\) is the density of all noisy images at step \(t\), which no one can write down. Training on line 2’s target, averaged over clean images, makes the network learn line 3. This is denoising score matching (Vincent 2011), so the noise-prediction loss is also a score-training loss (Dhariwal and Nichol 2021).
The clean-image guess used the same guess to point at \(\hat{x}_{0}\). Classifier guidance (Guided and Latent Diffusion, section 2) adds a classifier’s slope to this score.

3 DDIM takes fewer steps with no new noise

Builds on the forward recipe (Diffusion Models: Adding Noise on a Schedule, section 3) and the clean-image guess (Training a Diffusion Model: Predicting the Noise, section 2)

A 50-step DDIM run jumps about 20 steps at a time. Take one move.

Guess the clean image, then re-noise it to the lower level with the predicted noise and, if \(\eta \gt 0\), a little fresh noise.
\(\displaystyle x_{t'} = \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=clean}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t'}}\cdot \hat{x}_{0}}}}\)
\(\displaystyle + \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=dir}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_{t'} - \sigma _{t}^{2}}\cdot \varepsilon _{\theta }}}}\)
\(\displaystyle + \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=z}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\sigma _{t}\cdot z}}}\)
\(t' \lt t\) is the next kept step. The first term puts back the clean part at the lower level. The second points along the predicted noise, the “direction pointing to \(x_{t}\)” in the paper’s words. \(\sigma_{t} \cdot z\) is fresh noise, the \(z\) of the ancestral step.
\(\displaystyle \sigma _{t}(\eta ) = \eta \cdot \sqrt{\frac{1 - \bar\alpha_{t'}}{1 - \bar\alpha_{t}}}\)
\(\displaystyle \cdot \sqrt{1 - \frac{\bar\alpha_{t}}{\bar\alpha_{t'}}}\)
\(\eta\) scales the fresh noise, and 0 gives a deterministic sampler. The first root is the noise level at \(t'\) over that at \(t\), the second the noise added from \(t'\) to \(t\); at \(\eta = 1\) their product is DDPM’s spread for this big step. DDIM writes \(\alpha _{t}\) for this page’s \(\bar\alpha_{t}\).
It is the jump’s recipe run backwards, with the network’s two guesses in place of \(x_{0}\) and \(\varepsilon\).

The grey dots are a swiss roll, a flat strip curled into a spiral. It is a toy data shape: the real data sits only on that thin curled strip, so a sampler has to land on the strip, not just anywhere in the box.

Each dot is one image; Stable Diffusion does this in its 64×64×4 latent (a compressed copy of the image).

4 Flow matching walks straight from noise to data

Builds on the forward recipe (Diffusion Models: Adding Noise on a Schedule, section 3)

Walk in a straight line from the image to the noise, and train the network to predict the direction of that walk.
\(\displaystyle x_{s} = (1 - s)\cdot x_{0} + s\cdot \varepsilon\)
\(s\) is a continuous time from data (0) to noise (1), not the integer step \(t\), and used only in this section. This follows SD3 (Esser et al. 2024), which writes this time \(t\); Lipman et al. (2023) and Liu et al. (2023) write the mix the other way.
\(\displaystyle v = \varepsilon - x_{0}\)
\(v\) is the direction of the line, the target velocity.
\(\displaystyle L = \|v - v_{\theta }(x_{s}, s)\|^{2}\)
\(v_{\theta }\) is the network’s guess of that direction, trained with plain mean squared error (MSE) like the noise-prediction loss.
Step backwards along the predicted direction; on a straight line, one big step lands where many small ones would.
\(\displaystyle x_{s-\Delta s} = x_{s} - \Delta s\cdot v_{\theta }(x_{s}, s)\)
\(\Delta s\) is the step size in \(s\), so 1 means one step from noise to image. The minus sign walks from noise toward data, against \(v\). This is an Euler step, one straight move along the current direction.
\(\displaystyle \hat{x}_{0} = x_{s} - s\cdot v_{\theta }(x_{s}, s)\)
Line 2 is the whole remaining walk in one step, the flow version of the clean-image guess.
Diffusion’s forward recipe mixes on a curve with \(\sqrt{\bar\alpha}\) and \(\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha}\); the straight path above replaces it, and this step is DDIM’s idea on that line.

Real paths still bend, because crossing lines are averaged, and reflow, from the same Liu et al. paper, retrains on the model’s own pairs to straighten them.

SD3 trains with rectified flow, this straight path, and samples with 50 Euler steps in its evaluations.

5 How many steps do you need?

The DDIM paper sampled one trained model with fewer steps on a benchmark of 60,000 photos of 32x32 pixels in 10 classes (CIFAR-10). Quality is scored by the Fréchet Inception Distance (FID), the distance between feature statistics of real and generated images inside a trained image classifier; lower is better (Diffusion Models, section 7).

With \(\eta = 0\) each sample is a fixed function of its starting noise, so fewer steps follow the same path more coarsely and end on the same digit. DDPM draws fresh noise at every step, so its endpoint changes. Over 16 starting noises, DDIM kept one digit at all 9 step counts for 10 of them, and DDPM for none.

Figure 11. DDIM keeps the digit as the step count changes; DDPM does not.

With DDIM at 20 steps, every noise still gives its 1,000-step digit.

The DDIM paper reports the same consistency on real images: samples from one starting noise keep their high-level features as the step count changes. With \(\eta = 0\) the sampler is solving an ordinary differential equation (ODE), a rule giving the direction of the path at every point. A higher-order solver estimates that direction at two or more points per step, so it follows a curving path with fewer, larger steps.

MethodIdeaCalls
DDIMdeterministic steps, \(\eta = 0\)about 50
DPM-Solver, DPM-Solver++a higher-order solver for the same path, using slopes at several points per step10 to 20 (Lu et al. 2022)
Heun’s methoda second-order step, using slopes at two points35 per image on CIFAR-10 (Karras et al. 2022)

None of these needs retraining.

7 A DDIM sampling loop in PyTorch

Builds on the training loop (Training a Diffusion Model: Predicting the Noise, section 7)

A 50-step DDIM loop, with a tiny network standing in for the trained one.

import torch

T, steps = 1000, 50
alphas_cumprod = torch.cumprod(1 - torch.linspace(1e-4, 0.02, T), dim=0)
model = lambda x, t: torch.zeros_like(x)  # stand-in for the trained network

x = torch.randn(4, 1, 28, 28)
ts = torch.linspace(T - 1, 0, steps).long()
a = torch.cat([alphas_cumprod[ts], torch.ones(1)])  # 1.0 after the last
with torch.no_grad():
    for i, t in enumerate(ts):
        eps = model(x, t)
        x0_hat = (x - (1 - a[i]).sqrt() * eps) / a[i].sqrt()
        x0_hat = x0_hat.clamp(-1, 1)
        x = a[i + 1].sqrt() * x0_hat + (1 - a[i + 1]).sqrt() * eps
images = x
  1. 1The training schedule, a stand-in that always guesses zero noise in place of the trained network, and the start from N(0, \(I\)).
  2. 2The kept steps, from \(T - 1\) down to 0, are the \(t'\) of the DDIM step; their \(\bar\alpha\) values end with 1.0 after the last.
  3. 3The noise guess, then the clean-image guess, clamped.
  4. 4The DDIM update with \(\eta = 0\), using \(\bar\alpha\) of the next kept step.
  5. 5The samples.

The diffusers library’s DDIMScheduler implements this step, with \(\eta = 0\) and the clamp to [−1, 1] as its defaults (Hugging Face 2026). Sample with the exponential moving average (EMA) weights of the training page.

  • Use the training schedule.
  • Clamp \(\hat{x}_{0}\).
  • For fewer steps, DDIM needs only \(\bar\alpha\) at the kept steps; DDPM at fewer steps also needs \(\beta\) and \(\tilde{\beta}\) recomputed from those \(\bar\alpha\) (Nichol and Dhariwal 2021).
