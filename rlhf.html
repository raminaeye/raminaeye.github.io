This page builds on RL Basics and Aligning Language Models with RL. The terms below are the ones this page keeps using.

  • An agent is the learner that picks the moves. A state is the agent’s situation right now; a move, or action, is one choice the agent can make in a state; a reward is the number the world hands back after each move, good or bad. (RL Basics)
  • A token is a word or a piece of a word; a language model writes its reply one token at a time. (Aligning Language Models with RL)
  • Here the agent is the language model. Here the state is the prompt plus the tokens written so far, and the move is the next token. Here the reward is one number for the whole reply, given when the reply ends.
  • RL, short for reinforcement learning, is learning which moves to make from rewards rather than from labelled answers. (RL Basics)
  • A policy is the agent’s rule for choosing a move in each state; it may always pick the same move or pick by chance. The policy is the language model being trained to write replies. (RL Basics, Aligning Language Models with RL)

Why a score is not enough

A network trained to score replies the way people would can score any reply, but a score alone changes nothing: something must train the language model to write replies that score high.

The running example is a prompt from the InstructGPT paper, "Explain the moon landing to a 6 year old". A model writes several replies to it, such as a simple story or a list of dates, and a person says which reply is better. (Aligning Language Models with RL)

The InstructGPT paper is OpenAI’s work that trained its GPT-3 language model to follow instructions, and InstructGPT is the model it produced.

The people OpenAI hired wrote replies and compared them for prompts sent to OpenAI’s service.

A reward model is a language model with a one-number output, trained on people’s comparisons so the reply they preferred scores higher. (Reward Models from Human Preferences)

RLHF, reinforcement learning from human feedback, trains in three stages: training on replies people wrote, a reward model on people’s comparisons, then reinforcement learning against that reward model.

People’s work enters twice: once as written replies and once as comparisons.

Replies people write to show the wanted behaviour are called demonstrations.

The first stage, supervised fine-tuning (SFT), trains on them with the ordinary next-token loss, which teaches the model to predict each next token.

SFT copies demonstrations but cannot learn from "this is better than that".

One score per reply, small steps toward it

The third stage lets the model write replies, scores each one once at the end, and takes small steps toward higher scores.

Nobody tells the model which words to change; it only sees each reply’s score.

The reply about the moon landing in Figure 1 runs to many tokens.

So every token of one reply shares one score, and the method must work out which tokens earned it.

In RLHF the scores come from the reward model trained in the second stage.

The policy starts as the SFT model, so at first it writes replies like the ones people demonstrated.

An episode is one run from the start until it ends. (RL Basics)

Here one episode is one reply.

The third stage repeats this over many prompts.

The reference model \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\) is a frozen copy of the SFT model. (Aligning Language Models with RL)

KL measures how different two probability distributions are, zero when they match; the KL penalty charges the policy for drifting away from the reference model. (Aligning Language Models with RL)

The reward is the reward model’s score at the end minus a KL penalty, and an RL update that takes small, limited steps trains the policy to earn it.

From demonstrations to reinforcement learning

The second and third stages can repeat.

In the third stage a second network, the critic, starts as a copy of the reward model, initialized from it, and guesses the return each partial reply will end with.

The objective and the reward per token

An expected value is the average over everything that can happen by chance, weighted by how likely each outcome is. (Probability and Bayes Rule, RL Basics)

So the goal is the score the policy gets on average, not on one lucky reply.

The prompts the model is trained on are written \(\mathcal{D}\).

The sign \(\sim\) means drawn at random from. (RL Basics)

In \(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\) the dot stands for any reply, so it is the whole range of replies the policy might write to \(x\).

So the objective below scores the replies the policy actually writes, on the prompts it is trained on.

A piece of ordinary pretraining text is written \(x_{\mathrm{pre}}\), and the pretraining set it comes from is \(\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{pre}}\).

Mixing such text back in keeps the model good at ordinary writing.

PPO, proximal policy optimization, is the RL method this page walks through below.

Setting \(\lambda_{\mathrm{ptx}}\) above zero gives what the paper calls PPO-ptx.

InstructGPT’s final models use it, so every RL update also sees a batch of pretraining text.

The alignment tax is the drop in ordinary benchmark scores an aligned model can suffer.

Line 3 is there to shrink that tax: it trades a little reward for keeping the model’s ordinary writing skills.

In RL Basics, the reward that follows the action at step t, written \(a_t\), is \(r_{t+1}\); here the action is the token \(y_t\), and t is its position in the reply.

So the reward after each token is a small KL penalty, and the reward model’s score arrives once, after the last token.

One RL step on a batch of replies

Walking through one step

The policy gradient pushes each move the agent played by a score \(\Psi_t\) of how well it turned out, up when the score is positive and down when it is negative. (Policy Gradient Methods)

Here the score \(\Psi_t\) of each token is built in the steps below from the real rewards and a learned guess of the usual score.

Here a batch is a set of prompts and the replies the policy just wrote for them, trained on together.

So the tokens of one reply can be pushed by different amounts, even though the reply earned one score.

PPO is the policy-gradient method that makes small, limited updates so one batch cannot wreck the policy. (Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO))

One PPO step in RLHF uses four networks: the policy, the reference model, the reward model and the critic.

Which models train and which stay frozen

The policy and the critic are trained; the reference model and the reward model stay frozen.

The policy and the reference model run on every token, the reward model runs once per reply, and the critic runs on every token.

The loop repeats for the episode count in the settings table.

Rewards per token and the PPO-ptx loss

ppo_loss is the clipped PPO loss from Proximal Policy Optimization, computed with advantages from these rewards. Inputs are per-token log-probs, the log of each token’s probability, shape (batch, reply length), replies aligned so the last column is the last token. Column t of r holds \(r_{t+1}\), the reward after token t; libraries call it the reward at token t. The total is a loss, so it is minimized. The per-token rewards are computed without gradients, because they are targets for PPO, not something to backpropagate through.

import torch, torch.nn.functional as F
@torch.no_grad()                                  # rewards are targets, no gradient
def token_rewards(logp_policy, logp_ref, rm_score, beta):     # [1] the per-token reward card
    r = -beta * (logp_policy - logp_ref)          # KL penalty after every token
    last = torch.zeros_like(r)
    last[:, -1] = rm_score                        # reward-model score after the last token
    return r + last                               # new tensor, no in-place edit
def ppo_ptx_loss(ppo_loss, lm_logits, pretrain_ids, lam_ptx): # [2] line 3 of the InstructGPT objective card
    ptx = F.cross_entropy(lm_logits[:, :-1].flatten(0, 1),
                          pretrain_ids[:, 1:].flatten())
    return ppo_loss + lam_ptx * ptx               # PPO-ptx
  1. The per-token reward card: a KL penalty after every token, the reward model’s score after the last one.
  2. Line 3 of the InstructGPT objective card: the PPO loss plus the pretraining loss, weighted.

How the KL penalty holds the policy back

The best policy reweights the reference by the reward, and beta sets how strongly.

With the toggle on and beta in the lower half of the slider, the padded reply wins.

What InstructGPT found

Labelers preferred InstructGPT over GPT-3, and hallucination fell.

When RLHF with PPO is worth its cost

On-policy means learning the values of the same policy that is playing. (SARSA)

RLHF with PPO is on-policy: it learns only from replies the current policy just wrote.

Reach for RLHF with PPO when you have a learned reward and want the model to explore fresh replies.

It is the most flexible and the most expensive.

How RLHF goes wrong

Reward hacking is when the policy finds replies that score high without being good (RL Basics).

GRPO, group relative policy optimization, drops the critic.

In InstructGPT the critic is far smaller than the largest policy, because it starts from the one smaller reward model.

Constitutional AI is a later method in which a model guided by a written list of principles writes the harm labels.

During training, InstructGPT’s labelers were asked to put helpfulness to the user first; truthfulness and harmlessness came first only in the final evaluations.
