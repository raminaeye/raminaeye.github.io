Principal Component Analysis (PCA)

Faces from parts.

What if I told you a face is 10 numbers? Not a metaphor. The notebook loads 400 faces, each one 64 by 64 pixels, which is 4096 numbers per face. Then it rebuilds each face from just 10 numbers, and you can still tell who it is. That is PCA: find the directions where the data actually varies, keep those, throw away the rest.

One wrinkle: the notebook runs on 64 by 64 faces, but the tools on this page run the same faces at 32 by 32 (1024 numbers) so they stay fast in a browser. Tool numbers, like eigenface 1's 25.39% share, come from the smaller grid. Notebook numbers, like the 66 components for 90% energy, come from the full 64 by 64.

Think of it like describing a person to a sketch artist. You do not list all 4096 pixels. You say how much of each part this face has: a bit more of part 1, a bit less of part 2. PCA finds the parts automatically, from the data alone, no labels. The parts look like ghostly faces, and the paper that started it all literally calls them eigenfaces.

Find the directions where the data stretches most

Every face in the dataset is a point in 4096-dimensional space. One coordinate per pixel. Those points are not scattered randomly: faces all have eyes above a nose above a mouth, so the cloud of points is stretched along some directions and flat along others. The background pixels barely change from face to face. The pixels around the eyes and mouth change a lot.

PCA asks one question: along which directions does this cloud vary the most? Those directions are the principal components. The first one points along the biggest stretch of the cloud, the second along the next biggest (at a right angle to the first), and so on. Keep the top k directions and you keep most of the variation while describing each face with k numbers instead of 4096.

What this shows

  • The point: The face cloud is stretched, not round. PCA keeps the long stretches of the cloud and drops the flat ones.
  • What you'll see: Each dot is one of 400 faces, and longer principal components mark the directions of greatest stretch that PCA keeps.

Because each direction is itself a 4096-dimensional vector, you can reshape it into a 64 by 64 image and look at it. What you see is a ghostly face: bright where faces differ a lot along that direction, dark where they differ the other way. Turk and Pentland wrote it plainly: the eigenvector can be displayed "as a sort of ghostly face which we call an eigenface."

Not every pixel earns its keep

A face is 4096 numbers, but the numbers do not all work equally hard. The corner pixels are background, nearly identical in every face. Spending code on them buys nothing: they never change, so there is nothing to say about them.

The pixels around the eyes, nose, and mouth are the opposite. They change from face to face, and those changes are what make one face different from another. That is where the information lives.

PCA spends its budget where the faces actually differ. It finds the directions of biggest variation in the cloud and keeps those, so a 10-number code captures the parts that vary instead of the parts that sit still.

From 4096 pixels to 10 numbers, in six steps

The notebook implements PCA from scratch in numpy. Six steps take you from 4096 pixels to a 10-number code.

Center the data

Subtract the mean face from every face. The mean face is just the average of all 400 faces, pixel by pixel. (The paper shows it as its Figure 1b, the average face.) Centering matters because variance is measured around the mean: without this step, the first component would just point at the average face instead of pointing along the variation.

Each centered face records how far every pixel sits from average: a brighter-than-average pixel goes positive, a darker-than-average one goes negative.

$$ \mu = \frac{1}{n}\sum x $$
$$ \bar{X} = X - \mu $$

Find the average face: add up every face and divide by the count, then subtract the average from every face, so only what makes each face different remains. Here X is the faces, μ is the mean face, X̄ is each face minus the mean, n is the face count, and Σ means add up.

The covariance matrix

The covariance matrix records how pixels vary together. Start with the centered data matrix: one row per face, one column per pixel (400 by 4096 in the notebook). Multiplying it by its transpose gives a square matrix where each entry answers: when one pixel is brighter than average, is the other also brighter than average? Positive means they move together, negative means they move opposite. The diagonal entries are just each pixel's own variance.

$$ C = \frac{1}{n}\bar{X}^{T}\bar{X} $$

Build the covariance: multiply the centered faces by their transpose to see which pixels vary together. Here C is the covariance: what varies together, and T is the transpose: flip rows and columns.

Eigenvectors and eigenvalues

An eigenvector is a direction in pixel space; its eigenvalue is a single number. Multiplying the covariance matrix by an eigenvector just stretches it by its eigenvalue, without rotating it. That only happens for special directions, and those special directions are exactly the directions of pure variation. The eigenvalue tells you how much the data varies along that direction. Big eigenvalue: faces differ a lot along it. Small eigenvalue: nearly constant, safe to drop.

The eigenvectors come out sorted by eigenvalue, biggest first. Reshape the first eigenvector into a 64 by 64 image and you are looking at the first eigenface.

$$ Cv = \lambda v $$

Find the eigenfaces: the special directions where the covariance just stretches, each with its variation amount. Here v is an eigenface: a direction of variation, and λ is how much variation that direction holds.

Project (encode)

Take a centered face and dot it with each eigenface. The dot product measures alignment: the result is large when this face has a lot of that eigenface in it. Those k numbers are the compressed code for the face. This is the encoding half: 4096 numbers in, k numbers out.

$$ z = \bar{X}V_k $$

Encode this face: project it onto the top k eigenfaces to get a short code of mix amounts. Here z is the code: this face's mix of eigenfaces, and Vk is the top k eigenfaces.

Reconstruct (decode)

Start from the mean face, then add back each eigenface scaled by how much of it this face had. With k small you get a ghost: the right person, blurry. With k bigger the details fill in. This sum is exactly what the faceToy below computes live, in your browser, from the notebook's own eigenfaces.

$$ \hat{x} = \mu + zV_k^{T} $$

Rebuild the face: mix the top eigenfaces by the code amounts and add the average face back. Here x̂ is the rebuild.

Variance kept by k components

The share of the dataset's total variation explained by one component is a ratio: the variance along that component, over the total variance across all components. Add up the first k ratios and you get the cumulative energy kept by k components. The notebook asks: how many components to keep 90% of the energy? The true answer is 66. Its loop prints 64, and the gap is bookkeeping, not math. The loop searches cumsum, the running total of variance as components pile up, with the first entry sliced off (cumsum[1:]), so every printed index sits one step behind the full curve; and an index counts from zero, one more step behind the count of components. Two shifts of one: 64 printed, 66 components.

$$ \mathrm{share}_j = \frac{\lambda_j}{\sum_k \lambda_k} $$
$$ \mathrm{kept}(k) = \frac{\sum_{j=1}^{k} \lambda_j}{\sum_k \lambda_k} $$

Written as formulas, the share of one component is the variance along that component, its eigenvalue λ, over the total variance across all components, the sum of every eigenvalue. The tool code computes exactly this ratio from its eigenvalues.

How to play

  • The point: A face is its difference from average, written in the language of variation. Six stages take one face from the mean face to its rebuild.
  • What you'll see: A face is encoded as its difference from the mean face, expressed as weights on eigenfaces, and rebuilt by adding those weighted eigenfaces back to the mean.

Rebuild a face from k parts

One slider, k. The reconstruction sum from the decode step is computed live in your browser from the notebook's own eigenfaces (precomputed offline from the Olivetti set, 32 by 32 working grid). As k grows, the face goes from ghost to person.

k is a tradeoff, not a law: too few and faces blur, too many and the code stops being a compression. The notebook's 10 is a choice; 66 keeps 90% of the energy.

Eigenfaces: PCA recognizing faces

The eigenface idea comes from Turk and Pentland's 1991 paper. Their recognizer trained on 16 face images, one of each of 16 people, and kept just 7 eigenfaces for the classification step: the ones with the largest eigenvalues.

How to read the figure: each cell is one eigenface, a direction of variation reshaped into an image. Brightness is a direction, not a face: a bright patch marks pixels that vary one way along that direction, a dark patch the opposite way. The cells run in decreasing eigenvalue order, so the first eigenface carries the most variation and the seventh the least. Two cells are black because the paper computed 7 eigenfaces and left the last two cells of its 3-by-3 grid empty.

The figure below is the real Figure 2 from the paper, shown with its original caption.

Figure 2 of Turk and Pentland (J. Cognitive Neuroscience 1991). doi:10.1162/jocn.1991.3.1.71

On the 32 by 32 grid, the notebook's eigenface 1 explains 25.39% of the variance and eigenface 7 only 2.49%, a ten-to-one drop. That steep fall is why Turk and Pentland's recognizer kept just the top 7 eigenfaces from its 16 training images.

How to play

  • The point: rank is a quantity, not a label. The bars show the cliff directly: a ten-to-one drop in share from eigenface 1 to eigenface 7.
  • What you'll see: An eigenface's importance is its variance share on the notebook faces, 25.39% for eigenface 1 down to 2.49% for eigenface 7, not its place in the paper's figure.
  • Keep top k: faces past the top k fade, and the readout gives the variance the kept eigenfaces hold.

Same idea, tougher situations

The plain recipe has dials. The notebook's sklearn pass uses whiten=True, which divides each code by the square root of its eigenvalue so every direction has unit variance: without it, the first few eigenfaces dominate everything downstream. When the cloud curves instead of stretching straight, kernel PCA runs the same idea in a warped space where the curve straightens out. When the matrix is huge, randomized SVD (singular value decomposition) finds the top directions without the full eigendecomposition.

Three ways it breaks. PCA chases variance, so a feature on a bigger scale hogs the first components: standardize first. One outlier face can yank a direction toward itself, which is what robust variants guard against.

The geometry trick travels. PCA treats a face as a point in a cloud of faces and finds the cloud's geometry. Recommendation treats each movie as a point in user space, which turns "people who liked this" into a geometry question.
