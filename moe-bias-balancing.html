Auxiliary-Loss-Free Load Balancing

How DeepSeek-V3 keeps experts evenly loaded by nudging a per-expert bias after every step, instead of adding a balance loss that pulls on the model.

1 Balance without a loss

A router left alone sends most tokens to a few experts. The usual fix is a balance loss (Mixture of Experts, the balance loss), but its gradient pulls against the language-model loss. Auxiliary-loss-free balancing (Wang et al. 2024) moves balancing out of the gradient. Each expert gets a bias that is used only to pick experts, and the bias is nudged by hand after each training step.

methodloads balancedpulls on the model’s gradientuses future tokens
strong balance lossyesstronglyno
weak balance lossnoweaklyno
Expert Choiceyesnoyes
loss-free biasyesnono

Wang et al. sum up the alternatives this way (their Table 1). Expert Choice (Zhou et al. 2022) lets experts pick tokens, which balances perfectly but lets a token’s routing depend on later tokens in the batch. DeepSeek-V3 (DeepSeek-AI 2024) keeps one bias per expert in every MoE layer, updates it after every step from that step’s loads, and holds it fixed when the model generates text in the generate loop.

2 Scores and the biased pick

DeepSeek-V3 scores each expert with a sigmoid, then adds the bias only when choosing the top \(k\). The gate weights come from the scores alone. With a single picked expert and gates normalized over the picked set, the gate is one whatever the score, so the router would get no gradient through it; DeepSeek-V3 picks eight.

Sigmoid scores, a bias that only picks, and gates from the original scores.

(1)\(\displaystyle {\htmlClass{eqt sym-s}{\htmlStyle{color:#117864}{s_i}} = \sigma(\htmlClass{eqt sym-z}{\htmlStyle{color:#1f6aa5}{z_i}}) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-\htmlClass{eqt sym-z}{\htmlStyle{color:#1f6aa5}{z_i}}}}}\)
Each expert gets its own score between zero and one. \(z_i\) is the router’s logit for expert \(i\) and \(\sigma\) the sigmoid of Logistic Regression. Unlike a softmax, the scores do not compete: one expert scoring high does not push the others down.
(2)\(\displaystyle {\mathcal{S} = \operatorname{TopK}_{i}\big(\htmlClass{eqt sym-s}{\htmlStyle{color:#117864}{s_i}} + \htmlClass{eqt sym-b}{\htmlStyle{color:#a85a14}{b_i}},\ k\big)}\)
Pick the \(k\) experts with the highest score after adding each expert’s bias \(b_i\). The bias is used only here, to decide who is picked; \(\mathcal{S}\) is the picked set.
(3)\(\displaystyle {\htmlClass{eqt sym-g}{\htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{g_i}} = \frac{\htmlClass{eqt sym-s}{\htmlStyle{color:#117864}{s_i}}}{\sum_{j \in \mathcal{S}} \htmlClass{eqt sym-s}{\htmlStyle{color:#117864}{s_j}}} \quad (i \in \mathcal{S})}\)
A picked expert’s weight \(g_i\) uses its original score, divided by the picked scores’ sum so the weights add to one. The bias never changes how much an output counts.

3 Updating the bias

When a step ends, each expert’s load is counted. An overloaded expert’s bias goes down by \(\gamma\), an underloaded one’s goes up by \(\gamma\). The update uses the loads of the step that just finished, so no token’s routing depends on tokens that come after it (Wang et al. sections 3 and 5.2).

The bias update after each step, and how imbalance is measured.

(4)\(\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}\htmlClass{eqt sym-b}{\htmlStyle{color:#a85a14}{b_i}} &\leftarrow \htmlClass{eqt sym-b}{\htmlStyle{color:#a85a14}{b_i}} \\ &\quad + \gamma\, \operatorname{sign}\!\big(\htmlClass{eqt sym-l}{\htmlStyle{color:#c0392b}{\bar{\ell}}} - \htmlClass{eqt sym-l}{\htmlStyle{color:#c0392b}{\ell_i}}\big)\end{aligned}}\)
After each step, an expert above the average load becomes a little less likely to be picked, and one below it a little more. \(\ell_i\) is the load of expert \(i\) in the step that just finished, \(\bar{\ell}\) the average load, and \(\gamma\) the update speed; sign gives plus one, minus one or zero. Brought back to equations (2) and (3): the bias changes who is picked, never how much they count.
(5)\(\displaystyle {\mathrm{MaxVio} = \frac{\max_i \htmlClass{eqt sym-l}{\htmlStyle{color:#c0392b}{\ell_i}} - \htmlClass{eqt sym-l}{\htmlStyle{color:#c0392b}{\bar{\ell}}}}{\htmlClass{eqt sym-l}{\htmlStyle{color:#c0392b}{\bar{\ell}}}}}\)
How far the busiest expert sits above an even share, as a fraction of that share. Zero is perfect balance. It is the curve in the tool and the metric in the evidence table.

5 The tool: tuning the update speed

Too small a speed balances late, too large a speed keeps the loads swinging around the average, and in between the curve falls and settles.

6 The evidence

Wang et al. compared the bias against a balance loss. Lower perplexity is better; lower MaxVio means better balance. The bias matched the balance loss on perplexity while balancing far better.

Their update-speed study matches the tool: the smallest speed balances slowly early in training, the largest keeps fluctuating late, and the middle one works best (their Figure 4). Adding the error itself instead of its sign balanced slightly better but did not improve the model, and a multiplicative bias did slightly worse (their Tables 3 and 4). DeepSeek-V3 reports the bias method beating a balance loss on most benchmarks at two model scales, and that balancing over the whole batch instead of each sequence lets experts specialize by domain. Batch-wise balancing matched sequence-wise balancing on validation loss.

7 Guarding one sequence

The bias balances the batch as a whole, but one long sequence could still crowd a single expert. DeepSeek-V3 keeps a very small sequence-wise balance loss, with weight \(\alpha\) set to 0.0001, only to avoid extreme imbalance inside one sequence. Its form is the balance loss of Mixture of Experts, counted per sequence.

8 Where it is used and what it costs

The update speed is dropped to zero late in training, and the sequence-wise guard stays small throughout.

The cost is small: one vector of biases per MoE layer, one count of tokens per expert per step, and no gradient.

9 Code

Code 1, bias-balanced routing, then the bias update once per step.

import torch
def route_with_bias(z, b, k):         # z: (tokens, n), b: (n,)
    s = torch.sigmoid(z)
    _, idx = (s + b).topk(k, dim=-1)  # the bias only picks
    g = s.gather(-1, idx)
    g = g / g.sum(-1, keepdim=True)   # gates from s alone
    return g, idx
@torch.no_grad()
def update_bias(b, idx, n, gamma=1e-3):
    load = torch.bincount(idx.flatten(), minlength=n).float()
    b += gamma * torch.sign(load.mean() - load)
    return load
  1. 1Equations (1) and (2): sigmoid scores, with the bias used only in the pick.
  2. 2Equation (3): the gates use s, not s + b.
  3. 3Equation (4), run once per step after the batch. b is a buffer (register_buffer), not a trained weight, so the update sits inside no_grad.

Code 2, what to log for every MoE layer.

def log_router(load, keep, z):
    max_vio = (load.max() - load.mean()) / load.mean()
    dropped = 1 - keep.float().mean()
    share = load / load.sum()
    router_entropy = -(share * share.clamp_min(1e-9).log()).sum()
    z_loss = torch.logsumexp(z, dim=-1).pow(2).mean()
    return dict(max_vio=max_vio.item(), dropped=dropped.item(),
                router_entropy=router_entropy.item(), z_loss=z_loss.item())
  1. 1max_vio is equation (5).
  2. 2dropped is the share of token-to-expert assignments past capacity, from the keep mask of Mixture of Experts, capacity; a layer with no capacity limit, such as DeepSeek-V3’s, logs zero.
  3. 3router_entropy is the entropy of the load shares: \(\ln N\) when the load is even and \(\ln k\) when the router has collapsed onto its favourites (Figure 4).
  4. 4z_loss is the z-loss of Mixture of Experts, the z-loss.
