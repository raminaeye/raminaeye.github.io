ADC Fundamentals

Your brain speaks analog, continuous voltage that can take any value. Your computer only speaks integers. The ADC is the translator between the two, and if you don't understand how that translation works, you will misread every neural signal you record. Here is how I think about it. Fair warning: this is a topic I am still learning, so treat this page as my working notes, not gospel.

Microvolts (millionths of a volt) in, integer codes out: the ADC samples in time and rounds in amplitude.

An ADC turns a wave into numbers

An ADC turns continuous voltage into integer codes. Bit depth decides how fine the stairs are (the LSB, the Least Significant Bit, the voltage width of one stair step), and that sets your quantization noise floor (the level of rounding noise sitting under the signal), measured as the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). Sampling rate plus an anti-aliasing filter decide whether you capture the signal or a distorted ghost of it.

What the ADC measures

The ADC, analog to digital converter, translates the brain's continuous voltage into integer codes.

Every ADC has a reference voltage, Vref, that sets the top and bottom of what it can measure. The ADC is differential (it measures the difference between its two inputs), so a span of 2.4 V reads from -1.2 V to 1.2 V. Anything beyond saturates: the output flatlines at its maximum code.

Quantization rounds the voltage to the nearest code: a ramp goes in, a staircase comes out, and each stair step is one LSB (Least Significant Bit). The resolution, n bits, sets how many steps fit in the range; together with Vref it sets the smallest detectable change.

How many codes n bits give.
codes = 2n
n is the bit count, and 2n is 2 multiplied by itself n times. At 10 bits that is 1024 codes, numbered 0 to 1023 on this page (the bottom of the range is code 0), one per voltage bin.

The LSB: how wide one stair step is

The LSB is the voltage gap between two adjacent codes: the full span divided by the number of codes. At ±1.2 V and 10 bits that is 2.34 mV per code. A wider Vref stretches each step; more bits shrink every step.

Four moves:

  1. Grab the voltage
  2. Snap it to the nearest rung
  3. Write down the bits
  4. Accept the rounding error

The rung size, as one line:

The LSB: how wide one stair step is.
LSB = 2·Vref2n
LSB is one rung step, the smallest voltage step: the full voltage span the ADC can see, 2 times Vref, the reference voltage, split into 2n equal steps, the raised n meaning 2 multiplied by itself n times, n the bit count. It is the bridge between physical voltage and digital code, the one number that ties the analog world to the digital one.

At 10 bits the spike does not even fill a twentieth of one step. The ADC literally cannot see it.

One sample, four steps

Before the details, here is the whole conversion on a single sample: a toy 3-bit ADC over ±1.2 V reading one fixed 0.42 V sample.

The whole ADC in three lines: the rung size, the code the sample lands on, and the rounding error left behind.
LSB = 2·Vref2n
Work out the rung size: split the full voltage range, 2 times Vref, the reference voltage, into 2n equal steps, the raised n meaning 2 multiplied by itself n times. It is the one number that ties the analog world to the digital one.
code = round(Vin − VminLSB)
Find which rung the sample lands on: shift Vin, the sampled voltage, by Vmin, the bottom of the range, divide by rung size, round to the nearest whole step. The code is the integer the ADC hands onward: an ADC turns continuous voltage into integer codes, and the samples are all the ADC ever sees.
e = Vin − Vcode
Measure the rounding error: e is the gap between Vin, the true voltage, and Vcode, the rung voltage it got snapped to. Even a noiseless perfect signal carries that half-step of uncertainty because of digitization.

The same three lines in NumPy, with saturation as a clip at the bottom and top code:

import numpy as np
v_ref, n = 1.2, 10
lsb = 2 * v_ref / 2**n                     # 2.34 mV per code
v_in = np.array([0.42, 1.5])               # sampled volts; 1.5 V is past the rail
code = np.clip(np.round((v_in + v_ref) / lsb), 0, 2**n - 1)   # V_min = -v_ref
v_code = -v_ref + code * lsb               # rung voltage
e = v_in - v_code                          # rounding error, within half an LSB unless saturated

Gain: a magnifying glass that shrinks the room

Neural signals are thousands of times smaller than the ADC's 2.34 mV step, so wired straight in they read as zero. The amplifier multiplies the electrode voltage by a constant gain. At 250x one code covers 9.375 μV at the electrode, but the window that fits between the ±1.2 V rails shrinks by the same factor, a clipping risk.

Gain, read from the electrode side, in three lines:

The amplifier simply multiplies the electrode voltage by a constant factor, and the room shrinks in lockstep.
electrode LSB = ADC LSBgain
The electrode-side LSB is the ADC-side LSB divided by the gain: the bigger the gain, the finer the slice of the original signal each code covers. Gain is the constant factor the amplifier multiplies by.
amplified voltage = gain × electrode voltage
The amplified voltage is the gain times the electrode voltage, and what the ADC sees must stay within the ±1.2 V rails.
electrode window = ±Vrefgain
The electrode window is plus or minus Vref, the reference voltage, divided by the gain: the amplified voltage and the electrode window are the same multiplication read from the two ends. With a smaller LSB you can detect smaller voltages, but the input range that fits without saturating shrinks too, a clipping risk.

In code, at the page's 250x gain:

lsb_adc = 2 * 1.2 / 2**10       # 2.34 mV per code at the ADC
gain = 250
lsb_electrode = lsb_adc / gain  # 9.375 uV per code at the electrode
window = 1.2 / gain             # largest electrode voltage before clipping

More gain magnifies the spike and shrinks the electrode window at the same rate, until the spike clips.

At the ADC input the range stays ±1.2 V whatever the gain. Adding bits splits it into finer steps; only a smaller reference voltage shrinks the range itself.

Rounding error never goes away: it becomes the noise floor

Rounding to the nearest code leaves a quantization error of at most half an LSB either way, even on a perfect noiseless signal. On a slow ramp the error is a sawtooth from -½ LSB to +½ LSB that resets at every step crossing.

On a real signal those sawtooth cycles fall out of phase and blur into noise: zero average, every value in the range about equally likely. That is a uniform random variable, so its variance (how spread out the errors are) is the LSB squared over twelve:

Rounding error never goes away: it becomes the noise floor.
e = Vin − Vcode
e is the rounding error: the gap between the true voltage, Vin, and the rung voltage, Vcode, it got snapped to. Even a noiseless perfect signal carries that half-step of uncertainty because of digitization.
variance = LSB212
The variance is the step squared over twelve for a flat distribution, so the LSB sets the noise floor: quantization error carries no bias, its average is zero, and any particular error value in the range is roughly as likely as any other.
σq = LSB√12
σq is the standard deviation (the typical size of the error, in the same units as the error itself), the LSB over the square root of 12. Shrink the step and the standard deviation shrinks in direct proportion to the step; the square belongs to the variance on the line above, the step squared over twelve.

A quick NumPy check on random voltages at 8 bits:

v_ref, n = 1.2, 8
lsb = 2 * v_ref / 2**n
v = np.random.uniform(-v_ref, v_ref, 100_000)
e = v - (-v_ref + np.round((v + v_ref) / lsb) * lsb)
print(e.min() / lsb, e.max() / lsb)   # about -0.5 and +0.5
print(e.var() / (lsb**2 / 12))        # about 1.0

Below, a slow ramp goes in and the error is tallied in a histogram, a bar chart of how often each error value occurs. The zoom shows only a handful of steps; a real 12-bit window would hold thousands.

Max |error| = 0.5 LSB (always)
σq = LSB/√12

Finer steps, same sawtooth, same flat histogram: that flatness is why quantization error is treated as uniform noise.

Every extra bit buys you 6.02 dB

The signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) compares signal power to noise power, in decibels:

SNR in decibels.
SNR = 10·log10(PsignalPnoise) dB
Psignal and Pnoise are the signal and noise powers. log10, the logarithm, counts ratios in equal steps: every tenfold rise in the power ratio adds 10 dB, so very large and very small ratios fit on one scale.

For a full-scale sine wave (one that swings across the whole range the ADC can measure), that ratio works out to a famous rule of thumb:

Every extra bit buys you 6.02 dB.
SNR = 6.02N + 1.76 dB
The SNR is about six decibels for every bit, on top of a fixed 1.76: 6.02 is the per bit gain, because one bit doubles the codes, which halves the noise voltage, and halving the voltage is 6.02 dB. The 1.76 is a shape factor: the head start the sine wave's shape gives you over the noise's shape. N is the bit count.

Quantize a full-scale sine and measure it:

v_ref, n = 1.2, 10
lsb = 2 * v_ref / 2**n
x = (v_ref - lsb / 2) * np.sin(2 * np.pi * 0.01234 * np.arange(100_000))
xq = -v_ref + np.clip(np.round((x + v_ref) / lsb), 0, 2**n - 1) * lsb
snr = 10 * np.log10(np.mean(x**2) / np.mean((x - xq)**2))
print(snr)   # about 62 dB; the rule gives 6.02 * 10 + 1.76 = 61.96
SNR = 61.96 dB
Noise voltage = halves each bit
6.02 dB for each bit, on a 1.76 dB base

Each bit stacks one more 6.02 dB block; ten of them clear the 60 dB a neural recording needs.

The ADC staircase

If analog input is a ramp, ADC is a staircase. The bit depth sets the stair size: more bits, finer stairs, and a smaller gap between the steps and the signal.

If analog input is a ramp, ADC is a staircase.
code = round(Vin − VminLSB)
code is the bit code the sample lands on, found by shifting Vin, the sampled voltage, by Vmin, the bottom of the range, dividing by rung size, and rounding to the nearest whole step. The bit depth sets the stair size: more bits, finer stairs, and a smaller gap between the steps and the signal.
e = Vin − Vcode
e is the rounding error, the gap between Vin, the true voltage, and Vcode, the rung voltage it got snapped to.

These are the code and e lines as taught in One sample, four steps.

More bits, finer stairs, closer to the real signal. The error stays within half a stair until the signal climbs past the top stair, as it does at 1 and 2 bits.

Sample too slowly and high frequencies fold back as impostors

The LSB sets the amplitude axis; the sampling rate fs, samples per second, sets the time axis. The highest frequency in the signal must stay below the Nyquist frequency, half the sampling rate, or it folds back into lower frequencies: aliasing.

Why it folds: sampling keeps one value every sample period T, one over fs, like multiplying the signal by a train of delta spikes (infinitely narrow pulses). In the frequency domain, the signal described as a mix of frequencies (its spectrum), that copies the spectrum to every multiple of fs:

Sampling repeats the spectrum.
Xs(f) = 1T Σk X(f − k·fs)
X(f) is the signal's spectrum and Xs(f) is the spectrum after sampling. The Σ adds one term per whole number k, positive or negative, and each term is a copy of X shifted to k times fs. If the copies overlap, the frequencies mix.

With actual sines: at 1 kHz (one sample every 1 ms) Nyquist is 500 Hz, and anything above it mirrors back around 500 Hz.

A 600 Hz sine lands at 400 Hz: two different analog frequencies, identical samples. The samples tell the truth and lie at the same time. In phase terms, the 600 Hz sine advances more than 180° between samples, so it looks like a slower reverse rotation at 400 Hz.

Sample too slowly and high frequencies fold back as impostors.
fNyquist = fs2
The Nyquist frequency is half the sampling rate: at any sample rate fs, the ceiling is half of it. If you sample lower than that, the high frequency part of the signal folds back into lower frequencies and you get aliasing.
alias frequency = |fsig − fs|, for fsig between half of fs and fs
The alias frequency is the fold |fsig − fs|: the bars mean absolute value, the size of the difference with its sign dropped, and fsig is the signal frequency. A signal above the Nyquist ceiling reappears below it, at its distance from the sampling rate. The simple form covers frequencies between half the sample rate and the sample rate, the usual case.

Check it on the samples:

fs, f_sig = 1000, 600
k = np.arange(20)                                  # sample index
a = np.cos(2 * np.pi * f_sig * k / fs)             # 600 Hz, sampled
b = np.cos(2 * np.pi * abs(f_sig - fs) * k / fs)   # 400 Hz alias
print(np.allclose(a, b))                           # True: same dots

Rebuilding a smooth wave from the dots alone is what a perfect DAC (a digital-to-analog converter, the ADC run in reverse) does: it puts one sinc pulse, sin(x)/x, on every dot, sizes it to that dot's value and adds them up, which gives the one smooth wave through every dot. Past Nyquist, that rebuilt wave is the alias. A low-pass filter in front of the ADC removes the high frequency before it can be sampled.

Higher sampling rate means more data, higher power and better temporal precision. The quantization noise is spread over the full Nyquist band, so increasing the sampling rate spreads the noise thinner per Hz, slightly improving SNR per bandwidth.

Kill the high frequencies before they can fold

If you can't raise the sampling rate, put an analog low-pass anti-aliasing filter in front of the ADC. It removes everything above the Nyquist frequency, so nothing is left to fold back.

analog signal low-pass filter ADC digital samples

Kill the high frequencies before the sampler and they can never fold.

The cutoff sits a little below Nyquist. Because filters are not perfect, we usually oversample slightly (sample a bit faster than the signal needs) and downsample digitally later (keep only some of the samples to bring the rate back down).

Real filters roll off gradually. The cutoff is where output power falls to half: -3 dB, about 0.7x the amplitude. Past it the response falls at the roll-off rate, in dB per decade (a decade is 10x frequency), and each filter order adds -20 dB per decade. Higher order is steeper but costs complexity, power and phase distortion (frequencies delayed by different amounts, so their timing no longer lines up).

Back to the aliasing example. With a 500 Hz cutoff the filter is only -3 dB down at the cutoff, and at order 4 a strong 600 Hz component still leaks through at about 0.44x amplitude (about -7 dB) and aliases to 400 Hz. You want about -40 dB on that 600 Hz interferer. For a Butterworth low-pass filter (as flat as possible where the signal passes, then falling past the cutoff), the response is:

Kill the high frequencies before they can fold: the Butterworth response says how much of each frequency gets through.
|H(f)| = 1√(1 + (ffc)2n)
|H(f)| is the filter response, the share of the signal that gets through: the bars mean magnitude, the size of the response, and the root sign means square root: the root covers one plus the frequency ratio, f over fc, raised to the 2n power. f is the frequency being judged, fc is the cutoff frequency, and the fraction f over fc says how far past the cutoff the frequency sits. The order n sets the steepness in the 2n power: higher order means a steeper collapse past the cutoff, a first order filter attenuating at -20 dB per decade and each additional order adding another -20 dB/decade.

The same response in decibels, at the tool's defaults (cutoff 400 Hz, order 4) and at higher orders:

def butter_db(f, fc, n):             # 20*log10(|H(f)|)
    return -10 * np.log10(1 + (f / fc)**(2 * n))
butter_db(150, 400, 4)    #   0.0 dB: wanted 150 Hz passes
butter_db(600, 500, 4)    #  -7.2 dB: 600 Hz leaks at a 500 Hz cutoff
butter_db(600, 400, 4)    # -14.3 dB: same interferer at the 400 Hz default
butter_db(600, 400, 8)    # -28.2 dB at order 8
butter_db(600, 400, 12)   # -42.3 dB: -40 dB takes an order near 12

So even a 400 Hz cutoff needs about order 12 to put the 600 Hz interferer at -40 dB. That is why you pay for higher order when Nyquist sits close to your signal.

Higher order has a side effect: phase shift. Each frequency is delayed by a different amount, so a waveform with many frequencies changes shape even when its spectrum looks fine.

Common IIR filters (infinite impulse response: the output feeds back into itself, so every blip keeps echoing) like Butterworth have non-linear phase, and more of it at higher order. So two lower-order filters cascaded, one after the other, are sometimes wiser than one high-order filter.

We typically care about signals up to 250 Hz. High gamma, the fastest band of brain rhythms, is around 70-200 Hz. At 1 kHz sampling a high-order filter captures it, but its group delay (how much it delays each frequency) ripples can extend well into 200 Hz and slightly skew the timing of high gamma activity (HGA).

The 600 Hz interferer folds to a 400 Hz ghost. The filter decides how loud that ghost is.

Three converters, three tradeoffs: speed, precision, power

Flash compares the voltage against every rung at once, with one comparator (a circuit that reports which of two voltages is larger) per rung. It is the fastest, but the comparators multiply with the bits, so it stays at low bit depths and drinks power.

SAR (successive-approximation register) finds one bit per higher-or-lower question: three questions for eight rungs. It is the middle ground most neural headstages use (the small front-end electronics at the electrodes): fast enough, fine enough, sips power.

Sigma-delta samples far faster than it needs to and averages the noise away: slow, but extremely fine. A feedback loop keeps a running sum (sigma) of the difference (delta) between its coarse output and the input, which pushes the rounding noise up to high frequencies where the averaging removes it. It is the oversampling idea from Sample too slowly and high frequencies fold back as impostors taken to its limit.

SAR's questions as a loop, on the 3-bit sample from One sample, four steps:

def sar(v_in, v_ref=1.2, n=3):
    lsb = 2 * v_ref / 2**n
    lo, hi, code = -v_ref - lsb / 2, v_ref - lsb / 2, 0   # half-LSB shift: rounds to the nearest rung
    for _ in range(n):                 # one question per bit
        mid = (lo + hi) / 2
        bit = int(v_in >= mid)         # higher or lower?
        code = 2 * code + bit
        lo, hi = (mid, hi) if bit else (lo, mid)
    return code
sar(0.42)                              # 5, binary 101

Okay, so that is ADC in a nutshell, at least how I understand it so far. Bit depth sets how many codes you get, and together with V_ref that gives you the LSB, the size of each stair step. Gain is what shrinks the electrode LSB down to microvolts so tiny neural signals don't get lost between steps. Quantization error is stuck at half a step no matter how clean your signal is, and each extra bit buys you 6.02 dB of SNR.

Then the time axis: sample faster than twice your highest frequency or the high stuff folds back as aliases, and if you can't sample faster, put an anti-aliasing low-pass filter in front and accept the phase cost. I'm still learning this, so take all of it as working notes.
