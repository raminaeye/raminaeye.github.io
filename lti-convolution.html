LTI Systems and Convolution

What a filter or a room does to a sound.

A filter, a microphone and a room change a sound in the same way: each output sample is a weighted sum of current and past input samples. For a linear, time-invariant (LTI) system, its reply to one click, the impulse response h, tells what it does to any input.

1 What a system is

A system takes a signal in and gives one out: an equalizer (EQ) in a music app, the microphone in a phone, the room a voice travels through. Write the input as \(x[n]\) and the output as \(y[n]\), where \(n\) counts samples, and write \(H\{x\}\) for the output the system gives for input \(x\). The definitions follow chapter 2 of Anushiravani (2016), which models a recording’s EQ and room this way. The same model later describes a room’s echoes and an adaptive filter, one that keeps learning while it runs.

2 Linear systems scale and add

A second microphone, one sample farther from the talker, hears each sample one step late at half the amplitude. Figure 2 sends three inputs through it.

So speech and noise that reach one microphone come out as two parts that add, the starting point of spectral subtraction.

A linear system gives the same output whether inputs are mixed before or after it.
\(\displaystyle H\{a x_{1} + b x_{2}\} = {a H\{x_{1}\} + b H\{x_{2}\}}\)
\(x_{1}\) and \(x_{2}\) are two inputs and \(a\), \(b\) any two numbers. Scaling and adding the inputs first gives the same output as scaling and adding the two outputs. Row 2 of Figure 2 scales one input; row 3 adds two inputs. Section 3 asks the same of a delay.

3 The same reply at any time

Now the first input arrives one sample later (Figure 3).

In a time-invariant system, a delay in is the same delay out.
\(\displaystyle \begin{aligned} \text{if } H\{x[n]\} &= y[n] \\ \text{then } H\{x[n - r]\} &= y[n - r] \end{aligned}\)
\(r\) is a delay in samples, and \(x[n - r]\) is the input started \(r\) samples late. The system acts the same at every moment, so its reply only shifts; Figure 3 shows a delay of one sample. A system that follows this rule and the one in section 2 is LTI.

Two familiar systems break the rules. A microphone preamplifier (preamp) driven past its range clips, so twice the input is no longer twice the output: not linear. A phone moved during a call changes the path to its microphone, so the reply depends on when a sound arrives: not time-invariant. An adaptive filter, which keeps re-learning, handles that case.

4 The reply to one click

A unit impulse \(\delta[n]\) is one click: 1 at \(n = 0\) and 0 everywhere else. A system’s output for it is its impulse response \(h[n]\). A hand clap is close to a click, so a clap recorded in a room is close to the room’s impulse response. This page uses a stand-in room: the direct sound, then two quieter echoes, each one sample after the last (real reflections are milliseconds apart). Any signal is a row of clicks, each scaled by one sample value (Figure 4).

Any signal is a sum of shifted, scaled clicks.
\(\displaystyle x[n] = {\sum_{k} x[k]\, \delta[n - k]}\)
\(\delta[n - k]\) is a click at sample \(k\), and \(x[k]\) scales it to that sample’s value. The sum over every \(k\) rebuilds the signal, as the three clicks of Figure 4 do. Sections 2 and 3 say what an LTI system does with each click; section 5 puts the two together.

5 Convolution stacks copies of h

Each click in \(x\) comes out as a copy of \(h\), scaled by the click’s height (linear) and starting at the click’s time (time-invariant). The output is the copies added, column by column.

Convolution adds shifted, scaled copies of \(h\).
\(\displaystyle y[n] = {\sum_{k} x[k]\, h[n - k] = (x \ast h)[n]}\)
Term \(k\) is copy \(k\): \(h\) started at sample \(k\) and scaled by \(x[k]\). \(h[n - k]\) reads \(h\) backwards as \(k\) grows; that is the flip in convolution. The star \(\ast\) names the whole sum. Each output sample collects one term from every copy that overlaps it, and the copy that started latest brings the earliest tap of \(h\).
\(\displaystyle \text{length of } y = N + M - 1\)
\(N\) is the number of input samples and \(M\) the number of values in \(h\), called taps. The last copy starts at sample \(N - 1\) and runs \(M\) samples, so the output is \(M - 1\) samples longer than the input. Section 6 chains two such systems.

A convolution layer in a convolutional neural network (CNN) computes the same sums without the flip, so strictly it is a cross-correlation, the same sliding sum with \(h\) read forwards. A learned kernel, the layer’s own \(h\), absorbs the flip; a fixed filter or a measured room does not.

6 Order does not matter

Speech front ends, the first steps that turn audio into features for a model, often apply pre-emphasis, \(h_{2} = [1, -0.97]\), a filter that lifts high frequencies (the 0.97 default on the audio features page). Put it after a single echo \(h_{1}\) and send \(x\) through both (Figure 6).

Three rules for combining systems.
\(\displaystyle (x \ast h_{1}) \ast h_{2} = {x \ast (h_{1} \ast h_{2})}\)
Two systems in a chain act as one (the associative rule), with impulse response \(h_{1} \ast h_{2}\).
\(\displaystyle h_{1} \ast h_{2} = h_{2} \ast h_{1}\)
Swapping the two stages gives the same chain (the commutative rule), rows 1 and 2 of Figure 6.
\(\displaystyle x \ast (h_{a} + h_{b}) = {x \ast h_{a} + x \ast h_{b}}\)
Two paths side by side, the direct sound \(h_{a}\) and a wall echo \(h_{b}\), act as one system \(h_{a} + h_{b}\) (the distributive rule). Section 10 shows where these rules stop: at a stage that clips.

7 One multiply per frequency

An LTI system changes each sine wave only in size and in timing, the starting point of finite and infinite impulse response (FIR and IIR) filters. So in the frequency domain, convolution becomes one multiply per frequency bin, one frequency slot of the spectrum: the spectrum of \(y\) is the spectrum of \(x\) times the spectrum of \(h\), as the Fourier page shows. A copy of a voice at half height 100 ms later, multiplies the spectrum by a comb, evenly spaced peaks and dips; the longer the delay, the closer together they sit.

8 Cost and where it runs

The direct sum costs \(N \times M\) multiplies, so it grows with the product of the two lengths: a room response a second long makes it billions of multiplies for each second of audio. The route through the fast Fourier transform (FFT), the quick way to compute a spectrum, uses the rule of section 7, that convolution in time becomes multiplication of spectra. It pads both signals with zeros to at least \(N + M - 1\) samples, so no copy wraps around, rounded up to a power of two \(L\), the length FFTs run fastest on. It then runs three FFTs, one for \(x\), one for \(h\) and one inverse to get \(y\) back, each about \(L \log_{2} L\) operations. That grows only a little faster than the length itself, so on long signals the FFT route is far cheaper (Figure 7).

numpy.convolve computes the direct sum. scipy.signal.fftconvolve takes the FFT route, which its documentation calls much faster for arrays longer than about 500 samples. A short filter runs as a direct sum on every sample as audio streams in; a room response thousands of taps long goes through FFTs a block at a time.

9 Convolution in code

The stand-in room from section 4, with \(x\) and \(h\) three samples each, through both functions from section 8.

import numpy as np
import scipy.signal as sps

x = np.array([1.0, 2.0, 3.0])
h = np.array([1.0, 0.5, 0.25])

y = np.convolve(x, h)

y_fft = sps.fftconvolve(x, h)
assert np.allclose(y, y_fft)

ys = np.convolve(x, h, "same")
  1. 1\(x\) is three samples of a word; \(h\) is the stand-in room, the direct sound and two echoes.
  2. 2The full output, \(N + M - 1\) values: the stacked copies of section 5.
  3. 3The FFT route gives the same values, up to rounding.
  4. 4Mode "same" keeps the middle \(N\) values, so the output lines up with the input.

10 When the output looks wrong

Convolution becomes multiplication in frequency; the spectrum itself is built on Fourier Transform and the FFT.
