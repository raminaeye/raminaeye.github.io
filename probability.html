1 A model’s output is a list of chances

A language model does not output a word. It reads and writes tokens, words or pieces of words, and for the next token it outputs a probability for every choice. Here each token is a whole word, in the toy example from Softmax, Temperature and Decoding.

2 A random variable is a value chance picks

Which word comes next is a random variable, a value chance picks. So is whether dropout zeroes a unit, and so is the rounding error of an ADC, the analog-to-digital converter, the chip that turns a microphone voltage into numbers. A list of values gets a chance per value, the PMF (probability mass function).

An ADC outputs only evenly spaced levels; the gap between neighbouring levels is one step Δ. It rounds each voltage to the nearest level, so its error is equally likely anywhere in a width Δ centred on 0. A value on a line like this gets a PDF (probability density function) instead. Its flat shape and the bell curve are taught in Common Probability Distributions; here only their areas and running totals matter.

Chances add to 1; for a value on a line, the area under the density is 1.
Σx P(x) = 1
Capital X names the random variable and lowercase x one value it can take. P(x) is the chance that X takes the value x; Σx adds it over every value. The ten words add to 1, so when one gets likelier the others lose the same amount.
∫ p(x) dx = 1
∫ … dx measures the area under the density p(x), a height, not a chance. The rounding error is flat at height one over Δ across a width Δ, so its area is 1. Only ranges get chances, so a narrow density can rise above 1. Figure 2 draws the word chances (top) and the flat density (middle).
The running total of chance from the left, the CDF (cumulative distribution function).
F(x) = P(X ≤ x)
x is any value of X, used as a cut point. For a list, F adds the PMF up to x; for a line, it is the area under p up to x. It climbs from 0 to 1 and never goes down. Top-p, a decoding rule, keeps the likeliest words until their running total reaches a set share p, so it cuts the list where F crosses p. The rounding error’s running total reaches one half at its centre, 0. The standard bell curve is the hump-shaped density centred on 0 that noise often follows, taught in Common Probability Distributions, section 5. Its running total is written Φ (capital phi), the bottom panel of Figure 2.

An activation function sits after each layer of a network. ReLU, the simplest, passes positive inputs and zeroes negative ones. GELU, used inside models such as BERT and GPT-2, is a smooth ReLU, taught with the other activations in Multilayer Perceptrons.

GELU weights each input by the bell’s running total at that input.
GELU(x) = x Φ(x)
Here x is one input value, a number fed to the activation. Φ(x) is the bottom curve of Figure 2: near 0 for very negative x and near 1 for large x. So GELU passes large inputs almost unchanged, nearly zeroes very negative ones and bends smoothly in between, where ReLU has a sharp corner at 0.

In dropout, each unit is dropped with chance p, and a kept unit is divided by 1 − p, one minus the drop chance (inverted dropout). Take one unit whose output, its activation, is a.

The expected value, also called the mean, is the long-run average: each value times its chance, all added.
E[X] = Σx x P(x)
Each value x is weighted by its chance and the weighted values are added. For the dropout unit, the kept value a divided by 1 − p, times its chance 1 − p, gives back a, the balance point of Figure 3, so nothing needs rescaling at test time. In training, an expected loss, the error score training pushes down, is estimated by averaging the loss over a batch of examples (cross-entropy, policy gradients).

Spread is the variance, the average squared distance from the mean; its square root is the standard deviation σ.

The variance measures how far a value tends to land from its mean.
Var(X) = E[(X − E[X])²]
X − E[X] is how far one value lands from the mean; squaring counts both sides; E averages. The standard deviation σ is its square root. For the rounding error it is Δ² over 12, what the ADC page calls the noise power; Bias and Variance splits a model’s error this way. Figure 4 draws the squared distance and its average for the rounding error.

3 Joint, marginal and conditional

A speech or silence detector reads one audio frame, a slice of sound a few tens of milliseconds long, and asks whether it is loud. Write s for speech or silence and o for what is observed, loud or quiet. A speech frame is often loud, though speech has pauses, and a silent frame is loud only now and then, from noise bursts.

One table of joint chances gives how often two things happen together, each alone, and one given the other.
P(o) = Σs P(s, o)
P(s, o), the joint, is one cell of the table in Figure 5. Adding a column over s gives the marginal, the chance of loud whatever s is. When s is never seen, this sum is how a model scores what it does see: the hidden s is speech or silence in a hidden Markov model, the cluster that made a point in a Gaussian mixture model (GMM), and the alignment that says which audio frame spells which letter in Connectionist Temporal Classification (CTC), a speech-recognition loss. Expectation maximization (EM) fits models with hidden values like these.
P(o | s) = P(s, o)P(s)
The bar reads given: the conditional keeps one row and rescales it to add to 1. Loud given speech is the speech row rescaled, marker 1 in Figure 5. Swapping the roles gives P(s | o), speech given loud, the loud column rescaled, marker 2. So P(loud | speech) and P(speech | loud) are different numbers: they divide the same cell by different totals.
P(s, o) = P(o | s) P(s)
The product rule says chance first picks speech or silence, s, then loud or quiet given it, o. The share of frames that are speech, times the share of those that are loud, gives the speech-and-loud cell.

A language model scores a whole reply the same way, one token at a time (decoding). Models often keep the natural log of a chance, written ln: the power you raise the number e to in order to get that chance.

The chain rule of probability builds a joint one variable at a time, each given everything before it.
P(w1, …, wT) = P(w1) P(w2 | w1) ⋯ P(wT | w1, …, wT−1)
wt is token t of T. The dots … stand for the tokens in between and ⋯ for the factors in between. Each factor is the product rule again, and the history behind the bar grows by one token per factor. Many chances below 1 multiplied together shrink fast, so a long reply’s chance is tiny even when every token is likely. The log of the product is a sum of per-token logs, so models add log chances instead of multiplying tiny numbers. Figure 6 draws the fall.

Models cut the history in one of three ways.

history keptfactorwhere
all of itP(wt | w1 … wt−1)a language model (decoding)
one step backP(wt | wt−1)a bigram model, which looks one word back; the hidden states of an HMM
noneP(wt)independent data points, which do not affect each other (section 5), so the joint is a product and its log a sum (likelihood, naive Bayes)

Backpropagation has a chain rule too, the calculus one for derivatives: same name, different rule.

4 Bayes rule flips a conditional

An always-on keyword spotter listens for a wake word, often with a small gated recurrent network (gated RNN). Each firing without the word is a false alarm, and on-device spotters are judged by false alarms per hour. When the spotter fires, is the word there? Write A for the cause, the word, and B for the evidence, a firing.

Bayes rule turns the chance of the evidence given a cause into the chance of the cause given the evidence.
P(A | B) = P(B | A) P(A)P(B)
P(B | A), the likelihood, is how often the spotter fires when the word is there. P(A), the prior, is how common the word is before any firing. P(A | B), the posterior, is the product rule written both ways and solved for the cause. A rare word keeps the numerator small, so the posterior stays small even for a good spotter; Figure 7 counts it out in clips.
P(B) = P(B | A) P(A) + P(B | not A) P(not A)
The denominator adds every way to see a firing: real firings with the word, then false alarms without it, in gray as in Figure 7. When almost every clip is empty, the false alarms can far outnumber the real firings.

To pick only the most likely cause, compare the numerators, since the denominator is the same for every cause. Naive Bayes picks the topic of a forum post, its class, this way, by an argmax, the choice with the largest score, the rule greedy decoding uses for words.

Counting clips instead of chances, the frequency formats of Gigerenzer and Hoffrage (1995), makes the flip visible.

Figure 7. 1,000 clips; the bar splits the firings.

A rare cause needs strong evidence. Put the speech or silence detector of section 3 in a quiet room where speech is rare. The odds of speech are its chance divided by the chance of silence, and each loud frame multiplies them by the same factor: the chance a speech frame is loud divided by the chance a silent frame is. Each frame is scored on its own here; an HMM lets speech or silence carry over from step to step. Dividing the odds by 1 plus the odds gives the chance back.

The snippet computes the keyword spotter’s posterior. prior is the chance of the word, fire_if_word and fire_if_none are the two firing rates, and fire is the denominator.

def posterior(prior, fire_if_word, fire_if_none):
    fire = fire_if_word*prior + fire_if_none*(1-prior)
    return fire_if_word*prior/fire

  2. 2Every way to fire, the Bayes card’s second line.
  3. 3The posterior, the Bayes card’s first line.

5 Independence and correlation

Builds on the dot product and cosine (Vectors and Matrices, section 3)

Dropout draws each unit’s keep mask, a 1 that keeps the unit or a 0 that zeroes it, on its own (Srivastava et al. 2014). Knowing one mask says nothing about the other, so the two masks are independent.

Two words in a post are not independent. The 20 Newsgroups dataset collects posts from Usenet topic forums, such as comp.windows.x, about the windowing system of Unix computers, and rec.sport.hockey. “Port” and “motif”, a window toolkit, both show up in comp.windows.x posts. In Figure 9, X and Y are a pair’s two yes-or-no variables, unit 1 and unit 2 kept, or port and motif in a post, and x and y are their yes values.

Independence turns a whole table into two short lists, because the joint becomes a product.
P(x, y) = P(x) P(y)
Knowing X no longer changes the chances of Y, so every row of the table has the same shape. The two masks pass, the matching rows of Figure 9. Port and motif across all posts fail: they show up together more often than the product says, so they are dependent.
P(x, y | c) = P(x | c) P(y | c)
c is the class. When the product holds inside each class, X and Y are conditionally independent given c, as port and motif are within comp.windows.x. Naive Bayes assumes this for all words; CTC treats each frame’s prediction as independent of the others given the audio.

Correlation asks a weaker question, whether two values rise together along a straight line. Take one second of a 440 Hz tone, 440 cycles per second, sampled at 16 kHz, 16,000 numbers per second.

paircorrelationindependent
a sample and the next oneclose to 1no; a signal against its own shifted copy is its autocorrelation
the tone and microphone noiseabout 0yes
the tone and its squareexactly 0no, the tone fixes its square

Sums also need the covariance: how much two values rise and fall together along a line.

Covariance measures how two values move together; correlation is covariance without units; covariance decides how variances add.
Cov(X, Y) = E[(X − E[X])(Y − E[Y])]
Each bracket is a distance from its own mean; the product is positive when both land on the same side. Var(X) is Cov(X, X).
ρ = Cov(X, Y)σX σY
Dividing by both standard deviations gives the correlation ρ (rho), from −1 to 1. It is the cosine between the samples of X and the samples of Y, each list with its mean subtracted. Independent implies ρ = 0; ρ = 0 does not imply independent (Goodfellow et al. 2016, sec. 3.8). Figure 10 shows all three cases.
Var(X + Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y) + 2 Cov(X, Y)
When X and Y are uncorrelated, the covariance term is 0, so the variances simply add. Attention scores a token’s query, what it looks for, against another token’s key, what it offers, by a dot product: a sum of dk products of independent entries. When each product has variance 1, the score’s variance is dk. That is why attention divides its scores by √dk, the square root of the key length, which brings the variance back to 1 (Vaswani et al. 2017).

Three methods on the site act on correlation.

methodwhat it does
principal component analysis (PCA) whiteningrotates and rescales data so every direction has variance 1 and no correlation; it misses the tone-and-square link
independent component analysis (ICA)finds sources that are fully independent, not only uncorrelated
mel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCC)a discrete cosine transform (DCT) mostly removes the correlation between neighbouring mel bands, the overlapping frequency bands of a spectrum

The snippet checks the three correlations on one second of samples.

import numpy as np
n = np.arange(16000)
tone = np.sin(2*np.pi*440*n/16000)
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
noise = rng.standard_normal(16000)
def rho(a, b):
    return np.corrcoef(a, b)[0, 1]
r1 = rho(tone[:-1], tone[1:])  # close to 1
r2 = rho(tone, noise)          # about 0
r3 = rho(tone, tone**2)        # exactly 0
  1. 1One second of the tone at 16 kHz and a second of noise.
  2. 2The correlation ρ, the covariance card’s second line, read from np.corrcoef’s 2 × 2 table.
  3. 3The three rows of the correlation table.

6 Common traps

  • P(fires | word) is not P(word | fires), so never flip the conditional. In Figure 7 the spotter fires on almost every clip with the word, yet only a small share of its firings hold the word.
  • Zero correlation does not mean independent: the tone and its square, Figure 10.
  • A density above 1 is not a bug; only areas must stay at or below 1.
  • A row or slice is a conditional only after you rescale it by its total.
  • One zero count in a small table wipes out a whole product; smoothing, adding a small count to every cell, fixes it (Naive Bayes).

7 Naive Bayes and the common shapes

Put a post’s words in the evidence slot, its topic in the cause slot and add conditional independence, and you have Naive Bayes; the Maximum Likelihood and EM page fits the chances. The few shapes those chances take are in Common Probability Distributions.

Bayes rule piecein naive Bayes
prior P(A)the share of posts in each topic
likelihood P(B | A)each word’s chance given the topic, multiplied over the post’s words, by conditional independence
posterior P(A | B)each topic’s chance after reading the words; the argmax is the predicted topic
