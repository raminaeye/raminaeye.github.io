1 Why one big step can wreck a good paddle

One batch is reused for several passes.

Picture a paddle that already hits back most balls, then \(1\) update changes its chances so much that it starts missing them. A batch is the group of steps \(1\) update learns from, and playing \(1\) batch takes a long stretch.

Reuse each batch for a few passes, and cap how far each move’s chance may move. \(1\) pass is \(1\) run over the batch. Without the cap, \(1\) update can move the chances too far.

When it works, the paddle keeps learning from the same batch pass after pass, and not even \(1\) update can wreck it.

The running example is Atari Pong, a game on the Atari video game system. Each player moves a paddle up and down to hit the ball back past the other. The state is a stack of the last few frames, so the ball’s direction shows. The moves are up, stay and down. The paddle moves once every few frames of the game; \(1\) such move is \(1\) step. Each step shifts the paddle only a little, so it has to start moving before the ball arrives. The reward is \(+1\) for a point won, \(-1\) for a point lost and \(0\) on every other step. \(1\) episode is \(1\) full game. A point ends only when a player misses the ball, so most steps of a game pass with nothing won or lost. \(J\) is the average points per game, the quantity the policy gradient moves the weights to raise; it is the average over games of points won \(-\) points lost.

The agent is the learner that picks the moves. A state is the agent’s situation right now; a move, or action, is \(1\) choice the agent can make in a state; a reward is the number the world hands back after each move, good or bad. The agent sees a state, makes a move and gets a reward, over and over. An episode is \(1\) run from the start until it ends, and a policy is the agent’s rule for choosing a move in each state; it may always pick the same move or pick by chance.

Figure 1 shows Atari Pong, the running example for the whole page: \(2\) paddles, \(3\) moves, and a point for every ball the other side misses. Take the dashed line down the middle: it is the net, splitting the court in two.

A policy network is a network that looks at the state and outputs the chance of each move.

The policy gradient pushes each move the agent played by a score \(\Psi_t\) of how well it turned out, up when the score is positive and down when it is negative.

A smaller step for every weight does not fix this, because a smaller step cannot tell how far each move’s chance has moved, so it must stay small enough for the move that moved furthest and wastes most of the batch.

Figure 2 shows \(1\) too-big update. Below the bars, the first \(7\) rally marks are green hits and the last \(3\) are red misses, so the paddle misses the last \(3\) rallies in a row. Take the up bar: it shrinks from the longest to a sliver in \(1\) update.

The return is all the reward still to come, with later rewards counted a little less. A state’s value \(V(s)\) is the return the agent expects from state \(s\). In Pong, a state with the ball heading past the other paddle has a high value, and a state with the ball slipping past this paddle has a low value.

On-policy means learning the values of the same policy that is playing. Policy gradients are on-policy, because each push counts a move as often as the current policy plays it, so play by an older or different policy would push by the wrong amounts. The batch in hand was played by the policy as it was before the update.

\(\theta_{\text{old}}\) is the frozen copy of the weights that played the batch; \(\theta\) is the copy being updated. \(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s)\) means the whole set of move chances conditioned on state \(s\), up, stay and down together, rather than the chance of \(1\) move.

1.1 An earlier hard limit and its cost

Trust Region Policy Optimization (TRPO), an earlier method, keeps each update inside a trust region, a limit on how far the new chances may move from the old ones in \(1\) update.

How far the chances move is measured by the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence, how different \(2\) sets of chances are.

TRPO’s limit needs an expensive extra calculation at every update, and it is awkward with networks that share their layers between the policy and other outputs, or that use dropout, switching off units at random while training.

PPO uses only ordinary gradient steps, the same kind that train any network.

Equation 1. TRPO kept each update inside a hard limit, but that limit cost an extra calculation at every update, so PPO needs a cheaper way to keep the paddle near the chances that played the batch. A coach who caps how much a tennis serve may change in one week faces the same trade, how much to change at once without wrecking what works.
1\(\displaystyle \begin{aligned}&\hat{\mathbb{E}}_t\Big[\mathrm{KL}\big(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#1f4e9c}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}}(\cdot\mid s_t)\,\Vert\,\\&\quad\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#1f4e9c}{\pi_\theta}}(\cdot\mid s_t)\big)\Big]\le\Delta\end{aligned}\)

Figure 3 shows why the limit caps how far \(1\) update can move the chances: the KL sits at \(0\) where the new chance of up equals the old one and climbs as the new chance moves away in either direction, so capping the KL caps how far the chances can move in \(1\) update.

Intuition. \(1\) update must not move the chances far, or a good paddle can be ruined at once. The limit holds the average KL over the batch, and PPO swaps it for a cheaper cap on each move’s chance.
The mathematics. \( \hat{\mathbb{E}}_t \) is the average over the batch, built by adding the values up and dividing by the batch size. Inside it sits KL, the gap between \(2\) chance sets. KL adds up, over the \(3\) moves, the old chance times the log of old over new. \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#1f4e9c}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}}(\cdot\mid s_t) \) is the old chances conditioned on state \( s_t \), from the frozen weights; \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#1f4e9c}{\pi_\theta}}(\cdot\mid s_t) \) is the new chances from the updated weights. The dot is all \(3\) moves at once; the double bar reads as between the old and new sets of chances. \( \Delta \) is the allowed size of the change. So the whole equation says this: average how far the new chances moved, and keep it at or below Delta.
Example. Take a state where the ball is far away and both chance sets assign the same chance: the KL on that state is \(0\), so it adds nothing to the average.

Inside the limit, TRPO raises a stand-in objective, the surrogate, that rewards making good moves more likely, weighted by how good they were. That surrogate is the CPI objective, conservative policy iteration, the earlier method behind this push.

The KL-penalty version of PPO swaps the limit for a penalty weight. The weight subtracts a cost that grows with how far the chances moved, instead of a hard limit.

2 Capping how far each move’s chance can move

The ratio measures the drift

Picture the paddle’s chance of up drifting further from the chance it was played with, pass after pass, until the paddle barely plays up at all.

The cap holds the ratio: once it has moved far enough, the push stops counting.

When it works, \(1\) batch can be reused for a few passes, and the paddle stays near the chances that played it.

2.1 Score each move against the usual result

Each move in the batch needs a score of how well it turned out. A move’s advantage is how much better it is expected to do than the usual result from that state, the baseline. \(\hat A_t\) is an estimate of the advantage of the move at step \(t\), built from real rewards and a guess of the usual result, the baseline. PPO uses the advantage in place of the score \(\Psi_t\).

That usual result has to be guessed.

A critic guesses each state’s value, the return expected from it, through a second network, or a second head on the layers the policy shares. In PPO the critic’s guess of a state’s value is the usual result each move is compared with.

The network being trained is the actor, the policy network that picks the action; the critic’s guesses are used to score how good it was. Actor-Critic’s actor push raises the chances of the moves the critic scored above usual and lowers the chances of the rest.

Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) builds the advantage estimate \(\hat A_t\) from the real rewards and the critic’s guesses, blending short and long stretches of real reward into \(1\) estimate. \(\lambda\) sets how quickly the long stretches fade. Raising \(\lambda\) leans on longer stretches of real reward, so the estimate trusts the critic less but jumps around more; lowering \(\lambda\) leans on shorter stretches, so the estimate is steadier but trusts the critic's guesses more. So in Pong a point won many steps later still raises the scores of the moves that led to it, less the further back they are.

2.2 Compare the chance, cap it, repeat the round

Each update works on a minibatch, a small slice of the batch.

The ratio \(\rho_t\) compares the new chance of the move taken with the old chance, and the clip sets \(2\) edges with \(\epsilon_{\text{clip}}\): past an edge the push stops counting, so an update stops rewarding a helpful change once it passes an edge.

\(\epsilon_{\text{clip}}\) is unrelated to epsilon-greedy exploration on the value-based pages.

Figure 4 shows \(1\) PPO round. The arrow back to the first box says the round repeats, so each batch feeds the next. Take box 3: the cap sits under the passes, because every pass is capped.

3 The clipped push, the critic and the entropy bonus work in one step

Take the smaller of two pushes

Picture a move that scored well, its chance climbing a little on every pass, until it is far more likely than when the batch was played. The push should stop there, not keep climbing.

At every step take the smaller of the \(2\) pushes: the plain push, score times ratio, and the push held at the edge.

When it works, the push stops at the edge for a good move, while a bad move that has grown more likely keeps its full penalty.

Equation 2. PPO replaces the hard limit with a cap on each move's chance, so \(1\) batch can be reused for a few passes without the paddle swinging far past what the batch supports. Like reusing one practice session for a few drills: the session is reused, but no move is pushed too far.

2\(\displaystyle L^{\text{CPI}}(\theta)=\hat{\mathbb{E}}_t\big[\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#6c3483}{\rho_t}}\,\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#196f3d}{\hat{A}_t}}\big]\)
3\(\displaystyle \begin{aligned}\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#6c3483}{\bar{\rho}_t}}&=\operatorname{clip}\big(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#6c3483}{\rho_t}},\\&\quad 1-\epsilon_{\text{clip}},\;1+\epsilon_{\text{clip}}\big)\end{aligned}\)

  2. Marker 2 is the clip edge, where the rising line goes flat.
  3. Marker 3 marks the flat line, where there is no more push.
  4. Marker 4 marks the falling line: the solid line is flat left of the clip edge and falls right of it, while the dashed line falls throughout.
Intuition. Each move needs a number saying how far its chance has moved since the batch was played.
The mathematics. \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#6c3483}{\rho_t}}(\theta) \) is the ratio for \(1\) step. The numerator, \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#1f4e9c}{\pi_\theta}}(a_t\mid s_t) \), is the chance the policy now gives the move taken, \( a_t \), conditioned on this state \( s_t \). The denominator, \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#1f4e9c}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}}(a_t\mid s_t) \), is the chance the frozen old policy gave it. The divide compares them: at \(1\) nothing has changed, above \(1\) the move is more likely now, below \(1\) the move is less likely now. \( \theta \) is the weights being updated; \( \theta_{\text{old}} \) is the frozen weights that played the batch. So this line says: divide the new chance of the move taken by its old chance. It compares the same \(2\) policies as the trust-region limit, old and new, but on the \(1\) move taken.
Example. Take the first pass for a move that scored well: its new chance is barely above its old chance, so the ratio sits near \(1\).
Intuition. The push should reward making good moves more likely, weighted by how good they were.
The mathematics. \( L^{\text{CPI}}(\theta) \) is the surrogate objective, the number training makes larger. \( \hat{\mathbb{E}}_t \) is the batch average, built by adding the values up and dividing by the batch size. Inside it, \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#6c3483}{\rho_t}} \) times \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#196f3d}{\hat{A}_t}} \) pairs each move's score with how much more often the new policy would play it. \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#196f3d}{\hat{A}_t}} \) is the move's score, built by GAE from real rewards and the critic's guesses; the hat means estimate. The product rewards making good moves more likely: a positive score with the ratio above \(1\) raises the objective. With no cap the push grows without limit as the ratio grows, so it needs a cap. So this line says: average the score times the ratio over the batch.
Example. Take a move that scored well on an early pass: its score times a ratio near \(1\) gives a small push, and the push grows as the ratio grows.
Intuition. The ratio must stop counting once it has moved far enough: the cap is a band around \(1\).
The mathematics. \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#6c3483}{\bar{\rho}_t}} \) is the clipped ratio, and the bar marks the clipped version. Clip holds \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#6c3483}{\rho_t}} \) between \(1\) minus \( \epsilon_{\text{clip}} \) and \(1\) plus \( \epsilon_{\text{clip}} \); \( \epsilon_{\text{clip}} \) starts at \(0.1\) on Atari, so the band starts at \(0.9\) to \(1.1\) and narrows to nothing over training, and is \(0.2\) on MuJoCo. MuJoCo is a physics simulator for robot bodies. Those are the clip edges. Inside the band it equals the ratio; past an edge it sticks. The real ratio can sit past an edge, where the push simply stops counting. So this line says: the clipped ratio sticks at the edge.
Example. Take a ratio of \(1.3\) on a later pass: the clipped ratio stays at \(1.1\), so the extra \(0.2\) counts for nothing.
Intuition. Of the plain push and the capped push, only the smaller push counts, so the push stops at the edge.
The mathematics. \( L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta) \) is the clipped objective. Inside it, min takes the smaller of the plain term, \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#6c3483}{\rho_t}} \) times \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#196f3d}{\hat{A}_t}} \), and the clipped term, \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#6c3483}{\bar{\rho}_t}} \) times \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#196f3d}{\hat{A}_t}} \). For a good move, \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#196f3d}{\hat{A}_t}} \) positive, past the right edge the clipped term is smaller, so it stops changing and gives no push. For a bad move, \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#196f3d}{\hat{A}_t}} \) negative, multiplying both terms by a negative score swaps their order, so the plain term is the smaller one and counts: pushing it down stops at the left edge, while making it more likely keeps its full penalty. So this line takes the smaller term. The clipped objective is the push part; the critic fit and the entropy bonus are the other \(2\) parts.
Example. Take a move that scored well after several passes: once the ratio passes the right edge, the push stops.

Entropy \(\mathcal{H}\) is how undecided the policy is; it is high when the moves are about equally likely and \(0\) when \(1\) move is certain.

The entropy bonus is a small extra term that rewards the policy for staying undecided between moves, so it keeps trying the others for longer.

Equation 3. The clipped push alone is not a whole training step. A whole training step covers three jobs at once, the push, the critic and staying undecided, and none may swamp the others.
1\(\displaystyle \begin{aligned}&L^{\text{PPO}}(\theta,\phi)=\hat{\mathbb{E}}_t\big[L^{\text{CLIP}}_t(\theta)\\&\quad -\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{c_V}}\big(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{V_\phi(s_t)}}-\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#196f3d}{\hat{R}_t}}\big)^2\\&\quad +\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6d80}{c_H}}\,\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6d80}{\mathcal{H}}}\big(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#1f4e9c}{\pi_\theta}}(\cdot\mid s_t)\big)\big]\end{aligned}\)

Figure 6 shows the full objective against the ratio for \(3\) entropy weights, with the critic gap held fixed: the critic term is the same on every curve, so the curves differ only in how much the entropy bonus lifts them. The squared gap pulls the critic’s guess toward its target, and the gap shrinks as the guess moves closer. Past the clip edge the push stops, and the curves sag slightly as the policy grows more decided.

Intuition. \(1\) step must do \(3\) jobs at once: push the moves, fit the critic, and stay undecided.
The mathematics. \( L^{\text{PPO}}(\theta,\phi) \) is the full objective for the policy weights \( \theta \) and the value weights \( \phi \). \( \hat{\mathbb{E}}_t \) is the batch average: it averages the bracket over the batch. Inside it, \( L^{\text{CLIP}}_t(\theta) \) is the clipped objective for step \( t \), the smaller of the \(2\) terms. The minus subtracts the critic term: \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{c_V}} \) times the squared gap between \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{V_\phi(s_t)}} \), the critic's guess given state \( s_t \), and \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#196f3d}{\hat{R}_t}} \), its target return, the hat marking an estimate; the square keeps the gap positive. \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{c_V}} \) weighs the critic's fit. The plus adds the entropy bonus: \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6d80}{c_H}} \) times \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6d80}{\mathcal{H}}} \) of \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#1f4e9c}{\pi_\theta}}(\cdot\mid s_t) \), how undecided the policy is; \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6d80}{c_H}} \) weighs staying undecided.
Example. Take a state where the ball is slipping past the paddle: the critic's guess sits too high, so the squared gap pulls it down, the clipped term pushes the good moves up, and the entropy bonus keeps a sliver of chance on the moves the paddle rarely tries, so \(1\) update covers the push, the critic and staying undecided together.

5 PPO’s settings and runs with and without the clip

5.1 The settings differ by test suite

The discount \(\gamma\) sets how much a reward \(1\) step later still counts.

Bias here means the push leans the same wrong way on average, instead of just wobbling from \(1\) update to the next.

Counting later points a little less makes the push less noisy, because far-off points depend on many later chance moves and vary the most; the price is a small bias. Counting them more means less bias, more noise.

Spread is how much results vary from \(1\) training run to the next.

The learning rate \(\eta\) sets how big a step each update takes.

  • Passes over each batch: \(3\) on Atari, \(10\) on MuJoCo robots. More passes reuse the data more but drift further from the old policy; fewer are safer and waste more data.
  • Clip range \(\epsilon_{\text{clip}}\): \(0.1\) on Atari, annealed, lowered step by step, to \(0\), and \(0.2\) on MuJoCo robots. A larger range allows bigger changes per batch, faster and riskier; a smaller range is safer and slower.
  • Critic weight \(c_V\) and entropy weight \(c_H\): \(1\) and \(0.01\) on Atari. On MuJoCo robots \(c_V\) is not used, because the policy and value networks share no weights, so the critic is trained on its own, with its own loss; \(c_H\) is none, because no entropy bonus is used. As the full objective shows: raising \(c_V\) makes the critic learn faster, raising \(c_H\) keeps the paddle undecided longer.

The settings come from the PPO paper: the Atari values are its Table 5 and the MuJoCo values are its Table 3, with the clip range from section 6.2.

5.2 Three habits that keep training steady

  • Normalize the advantages in each minibatch: shift and scale them so each minibatch averages \(0\) with a similar spread, so \(1\) batch's pushes do not dwarf another's. The Atari script of CleanRL, a library of short single-file training scripts, normalizes in every minibatch.
  • Anneal the learning rate and the clip range on Atari, so large steps early give way to small steps late.
  • Watch how many moves hit a clip edge in each update, and the KL between the old and new policy: a rising clip count or KL says the policy is drifting too far. Its Atari script tracks both on every update.

\(1\) Atari rollout, \(1\) round of collecting a batch, takes \(12\) gradient steps: \(3\) passes of \(4\) minibatches each, over a batch of \(8\) workers times \(128\) steps, cut into minibatches of \(256\).

\(1\) Atari batch is \(1024\) steps of play, and the network holds about \(1.7\) million weights.

5.3 The clip wins, and PPO learns fastest

The PPO paper also tried an adaptive penalty weight with a KL target of \(0.01\), the value its best adaptive run used: halved only when the measured KL sits below the target divided by \(1.5\), doubled only when it sits above the target times \(1.5\), unchanged inside the band. A KL below target means the penalty is holding the policy back too much, so halve it; above target means it is too weak, so double it. The starting weight mattered little, since the rule corrects it fast.

On \(7\) MuJoCo robot tasks, scored so a random policy scores \(0\) and the best scores \(1\), and averaged over the last \(100\) episodes across \(21\) runs, the clipped version scored \(0.82\). It beat \(0.74\) for the best adaptive KL penalty, \(0.72\) for the best fixed penalty, and \(-0.39\) with neither a clip nor a penalty.

Replay keeps a memory of past steps, and training draws random batches from it.

The paper ran PPO beside \(2\) other actor-critic methods, advantage actor-critic (A2C) and actor-critic with experience replay (ACER), an actor-critic that reuses old play by reweighting it with a ratio of new to old chances, much like PPO’s ratio, so play by an older policy no longer pushes by the wrong amounts. A2C learns from fresh play by several workers at once, and fits when fresh batches are cheap.

On \(49\) Atari games PPO won \(30\) games to ACER’s \(18\) and A2C’s \(1\) when scores were averaged over all of training, so it learned faster; on the last \(100\) episodes alone ACER won \(28\) to PPO’s \(19\), so ACER finished ahead and PPO won on speed of learning, not the final score.

Figure 8 shows the clip beating the penalty on MuJoCo, PPO winning most Atari games, and ACER winning the last \(100\), with \(1\) tie on the last \(100\).

6 PPO training in PyTorch

This is a toy PPO training loop for Pong in PyTorch, to read rather than run; each block’s comment names the equation line it computes. Its loop follows the structure of the Atari PPO script in CleanRL; the values are the Atari ones from the settings list, and the network uses that script’s layer sizes. The loss lines follow the paper's full objective, with no \(0.5\) factor on the value loss and no value clipping, the cap on how far the critic's guess moves per update, unlike the CleanRL script. Code names map to the equations, the settings list and note 1. N_ENVS, N_STEPS and N_EPOCHS are workers, steps and passes; CLIP_EPS is \(\epsilon_{\text{clip}}\); LEARNING_RATE is \(\eta\); DISCOUNT is \(\gamma\); LAMBDA is \(\lambda\); GRAD_CAP is the gradient-norm cap. Pong has \(6\) buttons; the code keeps the \(3\) that move the paddle. The code uses gymnasium, the library that runs the games, and ale_py, the Atari game set it loads. The network outputs \(3\) logits, the raw outputs \(z\) of the softmax line on the Policy Gradient Methods page; the softmax turns raw outputs into move chances.

import gymnasium as gym, ale_py
import torch
from torch import nn
from torch.distributions import Categorical

N_ENVS, N_STEPS, N_EPOCHS = 8, 128, 3   # Atari values from the settings list
CLIP_EPS, C_V, C_H = 0.1, 1, 0.01
LEARNING_RATE, DISCOUNT, LAMBDA, GRAD_CAP = 2.5e-4, 0.99, 0.95, 0.5

gym.register_envs(ale_py)
MOVES = [2, 0, 3]   # Atari action numbers: 2 moves the paddle up, 0 stays, 3 moves it down
n_envs, n_steps = N_ENVS, N_STEPS   # workers, each plays a batch of steps
# network shapes: 4 stacked frames, 32 then 64 then 64 channels, 512 hidden
FRAMES, C1, C2, C3, HIDDEN, N_MOVES = 4, 32, 64, 64, 512, 3
FLAT = C3 * 7 * 7   # 64 channels on a 7 by 7 map: 3136
MINIBATCH_SIZE = 256   # the batch of 1024 steps cut into 4 minibatches
EPS_GUARD = 1e-8   # a tiny guard so a flat minibatch never divides by zero

def make_pong():
    env = gym.make("ALE/Pong-v5", frameskip=1)   # the emulator itself skips no frames
    env = gym.wrappers.AtariPreprocessing(env, frame_skip=4)   # each move repeats for 4 frames
    return gym.wrappers.FrameStackObservation(env, 4)
envs = gym.vector.SyncVectorEnv([make_pong] * n_envs,
    autoreset_mode=gym.vector.AutoresetMode.SAME_STEP)

class ActorCritic(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__()
        self.body = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Conv2d(FRAMES, C1, 8, stride=4), nn.ReLU(),
            nn.Conv2d(C1, C2, 4, stride=2), nn.ReLU(),
            nn.Conv2d(C2, C3, 3, stride=1), nn.ReLU(),
            nn.Flatten(), nn.Linear(FLAT, HIDDEN), nn.ReLU())
        self.policy_head = nn.Linear(HIDDEN, N_MOVES)   # actor: up, stay, down
        self.value_head = nn.Linear(HIDDEN, 1)    # critic: V_phi(s)
    def forward(self, x):
        h = self.body(x)
        return self.policy_head(h), self.value_head(h).squeeze(-1)   # squeeze: one guess per state

settings = {"clip_eps": CLIP_EPS, "n_epochs": N_EPOCHS, "minibatch_size": MINIBATCH_SIZE,
            "c_v": C_V, "c_h": C_H, "lr": LEARNING_RATE, "max_grad_norm": GRAD_CAP}
clip_eps, n_epochs, minibatch_size = settings["clip_eps"], settings["n_epochs"], settings["minibatch_size"]
c_v, c_h, max_grad_norm = settings["c_v"], settings["c_h"], settings["max_grad_norm"]

agent = ActorCritic()
opt = torch.optim.Adam(agent.parameters(), lr=settings["lr"])

obs, _ = envs.reset()
while True:
    obs_b, act_b, logp_b, rew_b, done_b, val_b = [], [], [], [], [], []
    for _ in range(n_steps):                          # each worker plays a batch of steps
        x = torch.as_tensor(obs, dtype=torch.float32) / 255
        logits, v = agent(x)
        dist = Categorical(logits=logits)   # turns the raw outputs into the move chances
        a = dist.sample()
        obs, r, term, trunc, _ = envs.step([MOVES[i] for i in a.tolist()])
        obs_b.append(x); act_b.append(a); logp_b.append(dist.log_prob(a)); val_b.append(v)
        rew_b.append(torch.as_tensor(r, dtype=torch.float32))   # r_{t+1}: the reward after move t
        done_b.append(torch.as_tensor(term | trunc, dtype=torch.float32))   # game ended at move t
    with torch.no_grad():
        _, v_last = agent(torch.as_tensor(obs, dtype=torch.float32) / 255)   # next state's guess
    values = torch.stack(val_b).detach()
    # GAE, built backward from the rewards, the critic's guesses and the done flags
    advantages = torch.zeros_like(values)
    gae = 0.0
    for t in reversed(range(n_steps)):
        next_v = v_last if t == n_steps - 1 else values[t + 1]
        delta = rew_b[t] + DISCOUNT * next_v * (1 - done_b[t]) - values[t]
        gae = delta + DISCOUNT * LAMBDA * (1 - done_b[t]) * gae
        advantages[t] = gae
    returns = advantages + values.detach()
    old_logp = torch.stack(logp_b).detach()
    actions = torch.stack(act_b)
    obs_f = torch.cat(obs_b)
    # flatten the batch, so each minibatch index picks one step
    advantages = advantages.reshape(-1)
    returns = returns.reshape(-1)
    old_logp = old_logp.reshape(-1)
    actions = actions.reshape(-1)
    for epoch in range(n_epochs):
        for mb in torch.randperm(len(obs_f)).split(minibatch_size):
            adv_mb = (advantages[mb] - advantages[mb].mean()) / (advantages[mb].std() + EPS_GUARD)
            new_logits, new_value = agent(obs_f[mb])
            dist_mb = Categorical(logits=new_logits)
            new_logp = dist_mb.log_prob(actions[mb])
            ratio = (new_logp - old_logp[mb]).exp()   # the ratio line
            clipped = ratio.clamp(1 - clip_eps, 1 + clip_eps)   # the clipped ratio line
            pg_loss = -torch.min(ratio * adv_mb, clipped * adv_mb).mean()   # the clipped objective
            v_loss = (new_value - returns[mb]).pow(2).mean()
            entropy = dist_mb.entropy().mean()
            loss = pg_loss + c_v * v_loss - c_h * entropy   # the full objective equation
            opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward()
            nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(agent.parameters(), max_grad_norm)   # not the ratio clip
            opt.step()
  1. 1The other settings, the same on both test suites except where noted: steps per batch \(128\) on Atari and \(2048\) on MuJoCo robots; minibatches of \(256\) on Atari and \(64\) on MuJoCo robots; \(8\) workers on Atari, not listed for MuJoCo robots; learning rate \(2.5\times10^{-4}\), annealed, lowered step by step, to \(0\) on Atari and \(3\times10^{-4}\) on MuJoCo robots; \(\gamma\) and \(\lambda\) \(0.99\) and \(0.95\); gradient-norm clip \(0.5\), the default in CleanRL. A larger minibatch steadies each update; a smaller one is noisier but updates more often. A longer horizon, more steps in each batch, waits longer between updates but learns from more play; a shorter one updates sooner on less play. More workers fill a batch faster; fewer are slower to fill it. The gradient-norm clip is a separate cap on the size of each gradient step, unrelated to the ratio clip. The paper never lists it. The loop leaves out \(3\) details: Adam's eps of \(10^{-5}\) and the annealing of the learning rate, both CleanRL details, and the annealing of the clip range, which the paper uses: the paper shrinks the clip from \(0.1\) to \(0\) over training, while the loop holds it fixed at \(0.1\). Adam is the optimizer the loop uses, and eps is the tiny number that keeps its division steady.

  3. 3GAE, the advantage estimate, built backward from the rewards, the critic’s guesses and the done flags.   6. 6The clipped ratio and the clipped objective lines of the clip equation: the ratio held between the \(2\) edges, and the smaller of the plain and clipped terms, averaged over the minibatch. The minus in front flips the signs, since training minimizes the loss.
  7. 7The full objective equation: the critic’s loss, the squared gap between its guess and the return it is trained to predict, plus the entropy bonus.

7 Step size and how far each pass pushes

Step size meets the clip edge.

Picture the dot racing along the line toward the clip edge, pass after pass, while the hollow dot runs on past it. The step size is a scaled-up stand-in for the learning rate, how big a step each update takes.

Each pass moves the chance by the step size times the push, and the clip stops the push at the edge. The tool runs at the MuJoCo settings, clip \(0.2\) and \(10\) passes, and varies the step size the walkthrough held fixed. The MuJoCo numbers show the ratio climbing over \(10\) passes, and the paddle move is the Pong move up.

Figure 9 shows where the clip stops the push. The \(2\) dashed lines are the lower and upper edges: the right edge stops the push for a good move, the left edge stops pushing a bad move down. When the ratio plot piece shows, an open circle on the dashed line marks where the hollow dot ends after \(10\) passes at the current step size, so dragging the slider moves that circle. Over the passes, larger steps reach the clip edge in fewer passes. Take the largest step size: \(1\) pass leaves the ratio past the edge, so this tool's ratio dot sits past it, but the push stops at the edge.

Figure 10 shows the clip tool with the toggle set to a move that scored badly: the solid line is flat to the left of the clip edge and keeps falling to the right, while the dashed line falls throughout. The \(2\) dashed lines are the lower and upper edges; the left edge stops pushing this move down.

8 When PPO fits

PPO is a robust default for games, robots and language-model fine-tuning, teaching a language model with ranked replies, when fresh batches are cheap enough to collect; the settings still differ by domain, so \(1\) set does not work across many tasks.

Prefer deep Q-network (DQN) family methods when steps are costly and replay is needed. PPO also fits robot control, where the moves are amounts from a range, such as joint angles or steering.

Figure 11 shows the fit choice: fresh batches point to PPO, while costly steps with replay point to DQN.

9 What breaks PPO

Each symptom names its fix.

Picture a paddle after a long night of training that still loses: its pushes swing wildly from batch to batch, its guesses are wrong, and it has settled on \(1\) move too early.

When it works, the paddle keeps learning instead of stalling on \(1\) failure, because each break points at its own cause and the fix follows from it.

  • The clip range or the number of passes is too large: the KL blows up. Lower the clip range or the passes.
  • The advantages are not normalized: \(1\) batch’s pushes dwarf another’s. Normalize the advantages in each minibatch.
  • The critic is weak: its guesses are wrong, so \(\hat A_t\) is wrong too. Fit the critic.
  • The paddle settles on \(1\) move too soon: raise \(c_H\).
  • Clipping does not strictly bound the KL. Stop the update round early when the measured KL grows past the KL target of \(0.01\), the target the adaptive penalty rule uses.

Figure 12 shows one symptom and its fix: a clip range set too large lets the KL blow up, and lowering it steadies training.

10 PPO for smooth moves

A smooth move is an amount from a range, such as a joint angle or a steering angle, instead of \(1\) of \(3\) buttons. The policy outputs that amount, and the clip only compares the move’s new density, how likely that amount is, with its old density, so the capped step carries over unchanged.

Figure 13 shows the clip comparing densities: at \(1\) joint angle the new density sits above the old one, so the ratio sits above \(1\).

11 PPO for language models

In a language model, each word the model writes is a move, and a reply is a run of moves. The clip still compares the move’s new chance with its old chance, so the same capped step trains the model.

Figure 14 shows a reply as a run of moves: each word is \(1\) move, so the clip caps each word's chance like any other move.

Reinforcement learning from human feedback uses PPO to fine-tune a language model.

People rank replies, and those rankings train the reward that PPO optimizes.

Group relative policy optimization drops the critic and scores each reply against the average reward of the group of replies to the same prompt.
