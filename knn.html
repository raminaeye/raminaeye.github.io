k-Nearest Neighbors (k-NN)

The classifier that trains nothing.

k-nearest neighbors: a classifier with no training step at all.

The laziest classifier that works

What if you skipped training entirely? No weights to adjust, no error score to lower, no passes over the training data. Nothing to fit.

You move to a new street and want to know which bakery is best. You could build a statistical model of bakeries, or ask your five nearest neighbors and go with the majority. kNN is the second option: to classify a new point, find the k training points closest to it and take a majority vote.

"Training" is just memorizing the dataset: the model is the data, and all the thinking happens at query time. The only knob is k, and it dials the boundary from jagged to smooth.

How it works: distance, then a vote

Two ingredients. First, who the neighbors are: the straight-line distance from the query to each training point, across every feature (a feature is one measured number that describes a point).

Distance first: who counts as a neighbor.
d(x, q) = √( Σj (xj − qj)² )
For each feature j, square the gap between the training point x and the query q, the Σ sign (sigma) sums the squares across every feature, and take the square root of that sum: the ordinary straight-line distance, the hypotenuse you met in school, and the number that decides who the neighbors are.

Ranking by the squared value orders the points the same way, so when only the order matters the root can be skipped.

Second, the vote: sort by distance, take the k nearest, count their labels. In NumPy (the standard Python array library) that is the whole algorithm, with X holding one training point per row, y their labels and q the query:

import numpy as np
d = np.sqrt(((X - q) ** 2).sum(axis=1))  # distance to every training point
nearest = np.argsort(d)[:k]             # the k closest
votes = np.bincount(y[nearest])         # count each label
y_hat = votes.argmax()                  # the majority wins

k is usually odd, which is not a coincidence. It dodges ties.

Gotcha, and it is a good one: with no training step, overfitting (fitting the noise in the data instead of the pattern underneath it) never shows up in a training curve. It shows up at query time, as a jagged boundary. Small k wraps the boundary around noisy points; big k averages the noise away.

And the gotcha that belongs with the distance step itself: k-NN compares raw feature values, so one feature measured in thousands drowns one measured in fractions. Normalize first, or the distance is a lie. One common way is to standardize every feature:

Put every feature on the same scale before measuring distance.
x′j = xj − μjσj
Shift feature j by its mean μj (mu) over the training points and divide by its spread σj (sigma, the standard deviation). Every feature ends up centered on 0 with spread 1, so none wins just because its numbers are bigger.

Classifying one query, step by step

The algorithm is three verbs: plot the query, find its neighbors, count the vote. Walk one query through all three, then the fourth move: change who gets to vote.

Then the vote: the neighbors decide.
ŷ = mode{yi : i ∈ Nk(q)}
Take a vote: look at the k nearest neighbors of the query q (the same q as in the distance card above, where x is a training point) and go with whatever label most of them have. ŷ is the query's verdict, yi is each voter's label, Nk(q) is the set of the k training points nearest to the query q (the voters), mode is the most common value, and ∈ means is a member of.

One prediction in one line: the query's verdict is the majority vote of its k nearest neighbors.
ŷ = mode{yi : i ∈ Nk(q)}

The k dial trades bias for variance

Same family as my data: 2D, two classes, two clusters (tight clumps of points) per class, with 400 points instead of my 3000 so the boundary re-contours live, and a few labels flipped as noise, as make_classification does. One slider, k, from 1 to 25. The diamond is the query point, and its k nearest neighbors light up with the vote.

Here is what that slider does to the boundary before you touch it.

At 3 neighbors one noisy neighbor swings the verdict, so the boundary grows inlets and islands around single points. At 17 the noise averages out into big smooth regions. The only thing that changed is how many neighbors get a say.

This is the bias-variance tradeoff with the math removed. Bias is how rigid the boundary is; variance is how much its shape wobbles with noisy points. The slider is the tradeoff, and the boundary is the receipt.

At k = 1 or 3 the query sits on an island around a few mislabeled points and turns orange; from k = 5 on its blue neighbors outvote the noise.

The setup, for the record

My data and split come from scikit-learn (a widely used Python library of ready-made machine learning methods), as in the notebook; its KNeighborsClassifier gives the same vote as the notebook's hand-written PyTorch one. The two plots use 17 neighbors (smooth) and 3 (jagged overfit).

from sklearn.datasets import make_classification
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.neighbors import KNeighborsClassifier

X, y = make_classification(n_samples=3000, n_features=2, n_informative=2,
                           n_redundant=0, n_clusters_per_class=2, random_state=2)
X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.1,  # 10% unseen test points
                                          random_state=42)
for k in (17, 3):
    clf = KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=k).fit(X_tr, y_tr)  # fit = store
    print(k, clf.score(X_te, y_te))                            # test accuracy

Five ways k-NN grows up

The plain vote treats the nearest neighbor and the farthest voter as equals. Weighted k-NN gives closer neighbors a louder voice, usually by the inverse of the distance, which quiets a far neighbor that keeps spoiling a good verdict. Voting only works for labels; for numbers, k-NN regression averages the neighbors' values instead.

Two new last steps: a weighted vote, and a mean for numbers.
ŷ = argmaxc Σi ∈ Nk(q) 1d(xi, q) · [yi = c]
Nk(q) is again the set of the k training points nearest to the query q. Each voter i adds 1 over its distance to the query to the tally of its own label. [yi = c] is 1 when voter i has label c and 0 otherwise, and argmaxc picks the label c with the biggest tally.
ŷ = 1k Σi ∈ Nk(q) yi
For numbers, the verdict is the mean of the k neighbors' values yi: same steps, but the last one is a mean instead of a count.

Euclidean distance is the default, not a law. Manhattan distance walks city blocks, one feature at a time, so it fits grid-like features. Cosine distance measures the angle between the two feature arrows, so it fits direction over magnitude, like text.

Two other ways to say who is near.
d(x, q) = Σj |xj − qj|
Manhattan: add up the gap along each feature j, where |·| is the size of a gap with its sign dropped.
d(x, q) = 1 − x · q‖x‖ ‖q‖
Cosine: x · q is the dot product, the sum of xj times qj over the features, and ‖x‖ is the length of arrow x. The fraction is the cosine of the angle between the arrows, so two arrows pointing the same way sit at distance 0, whatever their lengths.

Naive k-NN scans the whole dataset for every query, which stops being cute around a million points. A KD-tree sorts the points into nested boxes by splitting one feature at a time, and a ball tree uses nested balls of points instead; a query visits only the boxes or balls near it, so most of the scan is skipped. In scikit-learn every variant so far is one argument:

from sklearn.neighbors import KNeighborsClassifier, KNeighborsRegressor

KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=17, weights='distance')     # 1/d votes
KNeighborsRegressor(n_neighbors=17)                          # mean of values
KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=17, metric='manhattan')
KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=17, metric='cosine')
KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=17, algorithm='kd_tree')    # nested boxes
KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=17, algorithm='ball_tree')  # nested balls

Approximate methods like HNSW (Hierarchical Navigable Small World) link each point to its near neighbors in layered graphs, sparse long jumps on the top layers and dense short hops below. A query hops along those links toward the closest points instead of scanning at all, trading a little accuracy for a lot of speed.

k-NN assumes near means similar, and in high dimensions that breaks: everything is far from everything, so the nearest neighbors are barely nearer than the rest. This is the curse of dimensionality. k-NN shines with a few meaningful features, and wilts on a thousand raw ones.
