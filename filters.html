How FIR and IIR Filters Shape Magnitude and Phase

What filters do to gain and timing.

A filter keeps some frequencies of a signal and turns others down. Each frequency gets a gain (how much it is scaled) and a phase shift (how much it is delayed). FIR filters build each output from recent inputs only; IIR filters also feed back their past outputs. Both act once per sample, and designing one means choosing its weights so the gain is right where it matters, paid for in delay and computation.

1 A filter scales and shifts each sine

The filters here are LTI systems, linear and time-invariant (double the input and the output doubles; the filter never changes), so a sine goes in and the same sine comes out, scaled by the magnitude response \(\lvert H(f)\rvert\) and shifted by the phase response \(\angle H(f)\).

2 FIR filters average recent samples

Builds on the convolution (LTI Systems and Convolution, section 5)

A finite impulse response (FIR) filter makes each output a weighted sum of the newest input and a few past inputs; the weights are called taps.

This sliding weighted sum is a convolution, the same operation a convolution layer computes (CNN Basics: Convolution, Stride and Pooling).

3 IIR filters feed the output back

Builds on the decibel (Audio & Signals Basics, section 1.1)

An infinite impulse response (IIR) filter also feeds its own output back in, like an echo that adds weaker and weaker copies of the sound. Each copy is the last one times a feedback weight \(a\) between 0 and 1.

A level meter’s smoother keeps a share of its last output and adds a share of the new sample. It turns one click into an echo that shrinks by the same factor every sample.

A one-pole IIR low-pass, the simplest IIR filter, with one feedback term, called a pole.
\(\displaystyle y[n] = {b\, x[n] + a\, y[n - 1]}\)
Each output takes a share \(b = 1 - a\) of the new input \(x[n]\).
It adds a share \(a\) of the last output, so it feeds back on itself. One click comes out as an echo that shrinks by \(a\) every sample and never quite ends, hence "infinite". With \(a\) near 1 the output changes slowly and only low frequencies pass. \(a\) between 0 and 1 gives a low-pass; \(\lvert a\rvert = 1\) never fades, and \(\lvert a\rvert > 1\) grows without end. It builds on the FIR sum of Section 2 and adds feedback; Section 4 gives its frequency response.

The one-pole low-pass from the card, run on noise with lfilter.

import numpy as np
from scipy import signal

FS = 8000
a = 0.8                                   # a in the card
x = np.random.default_rng(0).standard_normal(FS)
y = signal.lfilter([1 - a], [1, -a], x)
  1. 1Sets the feedback \(a\) of the one-pole card in Section 3 and makes one second of noise.
  2. 2Runs the card: the first list holds the input weight \(1 - a\), the second the feedback weights \([1, -a]\), the sign flipped because SciPy keeps the feedback on the left side.

A low-pass passes slow changes and cuts fast ones. Its cutoff is where the gain bends down, by convention the \(-3\,\text{dB}\) point, where the power has halved and the amplitude is \(1/\sqrt{2}\) of the input’s.

4 The frequency response

Builds on the Nyquist limit (Aliasing and Anti-Aliasing Filters, section 2)

A 2-tap average passes a steady level whole and removes the fastest wiggle, at the Nyquist limit, where its two equal taps cancel.

The frequency response of an FIR filter.
\(\displaystyle H(f) = \sum_{i=0}^{M} b_{i}\, e^{-j2\pi f i/f_{s}}\)
For a sine at frequency \(f\), tap \(i\) sees it \(i\) samples late, an arrow turned by \(e^{-j2\pi f i/f_{s}}\) and scaled by \(b_{i}\). \(H(f)\) is the sum of those arrows; \(\lvert H\rvert\) is its length, the gain, and \(\angle H\) its angle, the phase shift. Where the arrows line up the frequency passes, and where they cancel it is cut. This is the DFT, taught in Fourier Transform and the FFT, applied to the taps.

The frequency response of a 31-tap FIR low-pass with freqz.

import numpy as np
from scipy import signal

FS, N_TAPS, CUTOFF_HZ = 16000, 31, 1000
b = signal.firwin(N_TAPS, CUTOFF_HZ, fs=FS)    # taps b_i
f, H = signal.freqz(b, worN=1024, fs=FS)
gain_db = 20 * np.log10(np.abs(H))
phase = np.unwrap(np.angle(H))
  1. 1Designs the 31 taps \(b_{i}\) of the FIR card in Section 2.
  2. 2Evaluates \(H(f)\), the frequency response card in Section 4.
  3. 3Turns \(H(f)\) into the gain \(\lvert H(f)\rvert\) in dB and the phase \(\angle H(f)\).

The smoother of Section 3 passes a steady level whole and turns the Nyquist limit down, more the closer \(a\) is to 1.

The frequency response of an IIR filter.
\(\displaystyle H(f) = \frac{\sum_{i} b_{i}\, e^{-j2\pi f i/f_{s}}}{1 - \sum_{k} a_{k}\, e^{-j2\pi f k/f_{s}}}\)
The top is the FIR sum over the input weights \(b_{i}\); the bottom is the same sum over the feedback weights \(a_{k}\), the weight on the output from \(k\) samples ago. The one-pole card has \(b_{0} = b = 1 - a\) and \(a_{1} = a\). SciPy's freqz evaluates this ratio for either kind.

The IIR filter used from here on is a Butterworth low-pass, the IIR design with the flattest possible passband before the gain falls away smoothly. Its order is its number of poles, the past outputs it feeds back: the filter of Section 3 has one, and order 4 has four, which cuts more steeply. SciPy builds it from second-order sections, a chain of small two-pole filters, which keeps the arithmetic stable. Figure 5 runs a 4th-order Butterworth with its cutoff at 1 kHz, on audio sampled at 16 kHz.

5 Linear phase keeps the shape

A click is many sines whose peaks meet at one instant. In Figure 6 the thin lines are three such sines, ticks mark their peaks and the thick line is their sum. Delay them all equally and the click survives; delay them unequally and it smears.

Symmetric FIR taps delay every frequency equally, so the phase is a straight line in frequency, called linear phase. The longer the filter, the longer that shared delay.

The delay of a symmetric FIR filter with \(M + 1\) taps.
\(\displaystyle \text{delay} = \frac{M}{2}\ \text{samples}\)
Symmetric taps mirror around the middle tap, so each mirrored pair adds to an arrow at the middle tap's angle. The sum always points as if it came from the middle, \(M/2\) samples late, at every frequency, which keeps every part of a wave aligned. It builds on the FIR response of Section 4, and the table in Section 7 lists this delay.

The Butterworth of Figure 5 cannot do this; its delay changes with frequency, most near the cutoff. A straight phase line delays every frequency equally, so the ringing at a pulse’s edges stays even; a bent one delays some frequencies longer and makes it lopsided (Figure 7).

6 Zero phase when you can wait

Offline, the whole recording is there, future included, so the filter can run forward and then backward over the result, which needs the signal reversed in time (Anushiravani 2016).

Forward then backward, the zero-phase filter.
\(\displaystyle \lvert H_{\text{zp}}(f)\rvert = \lvert H(f)\rvert^{2}\)
One pass drops the response at its cutoff and the second pass drops it again, so the cutoff shifts a little lower.
\(\displaystyle \angle H_{\text{zp}}(f) = 0\)
The backward pass delays every frequency by exactly what the forward pass delayed it, in the other direction, so no delay is left. It needs the future, so it cannot run live. It builds on the phase of Section 5, and Section 8 runs it as sosfiltfilt.

8 In practice

Filters run per sample on a live stream, such as a hum notch or the low-pass before decimation (keeping only every second or fourth sample), or over a whole file offline.

One noise signal through a 4th-order Butterworth low-pass, live with sosfilt and offline with sosfiltfilt.

import numpy as np
from scipy import signal

FS, ORDER, CUTOFF_HZ = 16000, 4, 1000
x = np.random.default_rng(0).standard_normal(FS)
sos = signal.butter(ORDER, CUTOFF_HZ, fs=FS, output='sos')
y_live = signal.sosfilt(sos, x)
y_off = signal.sosfiltfilt(sos, x)
  1. 1Makes one second of noise and a 4th-order Butterworth low-pass in second-order sections.
  2. 2Filters live, sample by sample, with the delay of Figure 5.
  3. 3Filters forward and backward offline, the zero phase of Section 6.

8.1 Gotchas

  • High-order or narrow IIR designs in \(b\), \(a\) form can go unstable; design with output='sos'.
  • firwin with an even tap count cannot pass Nyquist and raises an error for a high-pass.
  • When streaming in blocks, pass the returned state zf back as zi, or each block restarts from silence.
