GradNorm

Learn one volume knob per task so every task trains at the same pace. No hand-tuning.

You have seen the three diseases that break the naive sum. The first medicine treats the first two, scale and speed: GradNorm watches how training is going and turns each task's volume until nobody is falling behind.

The loudness problem

Picture several tasks sharing one trunk. Each task reports a loss, but the losses live on different planets: a depth loss near 800, a classification loss near 2. Add them up as-is and the big one shouts while the others whisper. And even after you fix the scales, one task can still dominate: its gradients are simply louder, so the shared weights move mostly for it. Hand-tuning a weight per task works, until the tasks' fortunes change mid-training and your tuned weights go stale.

This page is about the two classic fixes that turn the volume knobs for you. GradNorm learns the knobs from training dynamics, boosting whichever task falls behind. Uncertainty weighting sets them from the data itself, turning each task down in proportion to how noisy it inherently is. One watches pace, the other watches noise. Both are loss-weighting methods: they change how loud each loss is, before any gradient surgery. At the end, the question: when is turning the volume down enough, and when is it not?

The GradNorm paper

The authors were chasing a specific dream: one visual system doing full scene understanding, many perceptual tasks at once, on hardware with real limits, phones, wearables, drones. One shared network is the natural answer: it is efficient, and the shared features can regularize each other, so in the ideal limit you get the best of both worlds, more efficiency and higher performance.

But these networks are hard to train. Every task has to be balanced properly so the shared parameters converge to features useful across all of them. Earlier approaches tried to fix the imbalance in the forward pass, by engineering feature relationships or searching architectures. The authors' key point is that those approaches miss the real mechanism: a task that dominates training necessarily expresses that dominance as large gradients. The imbalance you see in the losses is, at its root, an imbalance in the backpropagated gradients.

So they attacked the root instead of the architecture: tune the gradient magnitudes directly, through the loss weights. Each weight couples linearly into its task's backpropagated gradients, which makes the weights a clean lever. The catch is that the right setting changes at every training step, and finding it by hand is what they call, in the abstract, a tedious search process that incurred exponentially more compute for each task added. GradNorm is their answer: balance training automatically, match the accuracy of exhaustive grid search, and only ever tune one knob.

How GradNorm rebalances the tasks during training

I picture K coaches all shouting advice at one athlete, the shared trunk. The loudest coach, the one with the biggest gradient, is the only one the athlete hears, and the coach whose part of the training is nearly done keeps shouting anyway. GradNorm is the moderator in the room. It puts a volume knob in front of each coach and keeps turning the knobs so that (a) everyone is equally easy to hear, and (b) a coach whose part of the job is falling behind gets turned up for a while. There is even a strength dial for how hard the moderator pushes for equal progress.

Two measurements, one rule

Every training step, each task produces a gradient: a to-do list of changes to the shared weights that would help that task. If task 1's list is full of small numbers and task 2's is full of huge ones, the sum looks like task 2's list. GradNorm's answer: adjust the knobs a little every step using two measurements, taken at the last shared layer W, the spot where the fight actually happens (about 5% extra training cost on NYUv2).

Quantity 1: how loud is this task right now?

Each task's loudness at each step: the length of the gradient of its weighted loss, measured at the last shared layer. The gradient of a weighted loss is just the weight times the gradient of the loss, a constant passes straight through, so loudness is knob times raw gradient length. Turning the knob up makes the task louder by exactly that much. That is the lever GradNorm pulls. The average loudness across tasks is the reference volume everyone is compared against.

Quantity 2: how far along is this task?

Current loss over initial loss: unit-free progress. The division is the point: a depth loss near 800 and a classification loss near 2 cannot be compared directly, but "how much of my journey is left?" means the same thing for both. A small ratio means the task learned fast, hence the name inverse training rate.

Quantity 3: is this task behind the others?

Each task's progress ratio divided by the group average. A value of 1 means on pace; 0.5 means ahead; 1.5 means behind and needing attention. The ratio by itself does not tell you who needs help: if every task sits at 0.75, nobody is behind. The problem is tasks falling out of sync, and you only spot that by comparing each task to the group. Nice side effect: if all tasks move at the same speed, every value stays at 1 and GradNorm does nothing. It only acts when tasks drift apart.

The target and the adjustment

Each task gets a target loudness: the average loudness across tasks, boosted for whoever is behind. Two fixes in one rule. The average pulls everyone toward the same volume, curing the scale problem. The boost turns the volume up for tasks that are behind, curing the speed problem. A strength dial sets how hard the boost pushes.

Then a penalty: how far is each task's actual loudness from its target? Ordinary gradient descent on this penalty, but the things being adjusted are the knobs, not the network. Two details turn this from a guess into a thermostat:

  1. Only the weights get updated (the network is held fixed for this step). Since loudness is knob times raw gradient length, raising a task's knob raises its loudness by the same factor: cheap, one small nudge per step.
  2. The weights are rescaled to sum to K after every update. Without this, the penalty has a cheat: shrink all weights and every norm shrinks, so the penalty falls without anything being balanced. The fixed budget stops the cheat: one task can only get louder if another gets quieter, and the overall learning speed stays the same, so comparisons with baselines stay fair.

GradNorm is a thermostat, not a guess. Every step visits four stations: sense (measure loudness and progress) → compare (set targets) → correct (nudge the weights) → renormalize (clamp the budget). Because the loop re-measures after every correction, no lucky initial setting of the weights is ever needed.

Algorithm 1: per training step

  1. Forward pass; compute each task's loss and the weighted total.
  2. Measure each task's loudness at the last shared layer W, and the mean loudness across tasks.
  3. Measure each task's progress ratio (current loss over initial loss) and its standing relative to the group average.
  4. Form the penalty from the gaps between actual and target loudness, and update only the weights by gradient descent on it.
  5. Renormalize the weights to sum to K.
  6. Update the network on the weighted total as usual.
# after the forward pass: losses = [L_1..L_K]; w = trainable task weights
G      = [norm(grad(w[i] * losses[i], W)) for i in range(K)]  # loudness per task
ratio  = losses / losses_0            # progress so far, unit-free
r      = ratio / mean(ratio)           # who is behind the pack? (r > 1 = behind)
target = mean(G) * r ** alpha         # average loudness, boosted for laggards
L_grad = sum(abs(G - target))         # how far each norm is from its target
w = w - lr_w * grad(L_grad, w)        # nudge ONLY the weights
w = w * K / sum(w)                    # fixed budget: one task up, another down

The thermostat in one step: a task sitting below its target gets its weight raised, a task sitting above gets its weight lowered. The scale term pulls everyone toward the same average loudness while the rate term boosts whoever is falling behind.

The quiet task stalls without rebalancing

The paper's own version of this toy uses a 100× output scale and reports curves rather than single numbers. With fixed equal weights, the quiet task stalls far short of its solo run while the loud task is hurt too: the interference is not zero-sum. GradNorm turns the loud task's weight down and the quiet one's up.

In this replay the gap does not drift into triple digits: it starts there, at 300:1 with the default scale, in both runs. The difference is how fast it collapses. GradNorm has it down to 16:1 by step 50 while equal weights still sits at 112:1, and that early rebalancing is what frees the quiet task: by step 100 its loss is 0.653 with GradNorm while it is still stalled at 0.979 under equal weights.

The knobs, one by one

KnobWhat it doesHow to set itIf you get it wrong
α How hard to push for equal progress: the strength dial on the behind-task boost. At 0 the progress meter switches off (fixes scale only); 1.5 is the NYUv2 value. Start at 0.5 for similar tasks, 1 to 1.5 for dissimilar ones. Results are not very sensitive, treat it as a coarse dial. Too small: a task that is always behind never gets volume. Too large: weights swing back and forth and training swings with them.
λ_w How fast the knobs move: the learning rate of the weights themselves (the network's own rate is separate). 10 to 100× smaller than the network's rate (the paper uses 0.025 with Adam for the weight updates on NYUv2). When in doubt, go small. Too big: weights overcorrect, loudness chases its own tail. Too small: balance arrives after the early, most damaged part of training.
W The reference layer where loudness is measured: the last shared layer by default. Keep the default: deepest point still shared by all tasks, one extra backward pass per task. Earlier layers catch tasks before their effects have mixed; the heads measure the wrong thing entirely.

When to reach for it

Reach for it when your tasks have very different loss scales or learning speeds. The classic case is dense prediction (segmentation + depth + normals).

Costs: one extra backward pass per task to get the per-task norms at the last shared layer, plus a second-order term through the penalty. Modest in practice.

Keep in mind: GradNorm is a heuristic with no convergence guarantee, and it says nothing about gradient direction. Two perfectly balanced gradients can still point against each other.

GradNorm sets the volume by watching pace: who is falling behind, right now. But pace is a symptom of training dynamics. What if the right volume is knowable before training dynamics even enter, from the data itself? Some tasks are just noisier than others, and a noisy task should count less. That is the next method's whole idea.
