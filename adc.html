ADC Fundamentals

Your brain speaks analog, continuous voltage that can take any value. Your computer only speaks integers. The ADC is the translator between the two, and if you don't understand how that translation works, you will misread every neural signal you record. Here is how I think about it. Fair warning: this is a topic I am still learning, so treat this page as my working notes, not gospel.

Left to right: the electrode picks up microvolts, the amplifier multiplies them into the ADC's volt range, the anti-alias filter removes what sampling would corrupt, the ADC samples in time and rounds in amplitude, and out come integer codes. Every section below is one of these boxes.

What an ADC does

Here is the whole page in one paragraph. An ADC turns continuous voltage into integer codes. Bit depth decides how fine the stairs are (the LSB), and that sets your quantization noise floor (the SNR). Sampling rate plus an anti-aliasing filter decide whether you capture the signal or a distorted ghost of it. Everything below is just the details.

ADC

The brain produces analog signals, continuous voltage that can take any value. The ADC, analog to digital converter, is the component that does this translation.

Every ADC has a reference voltage, V_ref. It defines the top and bottom of what the ADC can measure. The ADC is differential, so a span of 2.4V means it can measure from -1.2V to 1.2V. The ADC can take voltage within the range -1.2V to 1.2V, and assigns each interval of voltage a digital code. Anything beyond this region gets saturated; the ADC output just flatlines at its maximum digital code.

Quantization rounds the continuous voltage to the nearest available code. If analog input is a ramp, ADC is a staircase. Each stair step corresponds to one LSB (Least Significant bit). The finer the steps, the closer the digital version is to the real signal.

The ADC resolution, number of bits, determines how precisely you can measure small voltage changes. That range is the reference voltage that determines how large a signal can be measured before saturation, clipping. These two together set the quantization granularity, the smallest detectable change.

If the ADC has 10 bits of resolution it can represent 1024 values. Each bin would correspond to one discrete code, integers from -512 to +511.

LSB (Least Significant Bit)

The LSB represents the voltage difference between two adjacent digital codes. The LSB is the bridge between physical voltage and digital code. Mathematically: the LSB is the full voltage span the ADC can see, divided by the number of codes.

At ±1.2 V and 10 bits that is a few millivolts per code. Measured in volts, this step size is what we call the LSB.

A wider V_ref stretches each step; more bits multiplies the step count and shrinks every step. It is the one number that ties the analog world to the digital one.

Amplifier (Gain)

The neural signals produce much smaller voltages, thousands of times smaller, so if you directly connect to the ADC it would read as zero because each step is 2.34mV. That’s where the amplifier, the gain, comes in. The amplifier simply multiplies the electrode voltage by a constant factor.

The amplifier brings the neural signals into a range the ADC can digitize accurately. While the ADC-side LSB is 2.34mV per code, the LSB at electrode level is smaller because the amplifier multiplies that voltage by the gain. At 250x gain, the electrode-side LSB is 9.375μV per code: the ADC-side LSB divided by the gain.

The gain affects both range and resolution equally. With a smaller LSB you can detect smaller voltages, but the input range that fits without saturating shrinks too, a clipping risk. The amplifier must make sure the largest expected electrode signal stays within what the ADC can see.

The maximum electrode voltage you can measure without saturation is inversely proportional to the gain: what the ADC sees must stay within the reference voltage, so crank the gain and the measurable voltage at the electrode shrinks in lockstep.

So far: the ADC converts continuous voltage, analog signals, into discrete digital signals. V_ref is the max voltage the ADC can see, quantization is rounding to the nearest integer, and the LSB is how wide each step is.

Changing the LSB directly changes the measured voltage range. At the ADC input the max is about the reference voltage, ±1.2V, no matter the gain; gain only changes what that means at the electrode. Smaller LSB means higher resolution but a smaller maximum voltage before saturation.

Quantization Error

Because ADC converts a continuous analog voltage to a discrete digital code, it must round the input to the nearest quantization level. This rounding introduces a quantization error. If the LSB is 4.7μV, then any voltage between 7.05μV and 11.75μV rounds to the 9.4μV code. The max quantization error is half an LSB: 2.35μV. So even a noiseless perfect signal carries 2.35μV of uncertainty because of digitization.

If you plot the quantization error for a slowly increasing signal it would look like a saw-tooth wave, ramps linearly between -½ LSB to +½ LSB, because the ADC snaps to the next step. For a real neural signal the waveform continuously changes at ADC step boundaries, so each crossing is where a sawtooth cycle resets.

If you overlay these sawtooth error cycles but with slightly shifted phases they no longer line up, it blurs together like random noise. There is no bias in quantization error, it is zero, and the probability of any particular error value is roughly constant. That is a definition of a uniform random variable, hence we treat quantization error as uniform noise, with variance equal to the LSB squared over twelve:

The mean is 0. The rounding error swings half a step either way and averages out to zero, spread evenly like a flat distribution. For a flat distribution the variance is the step squared over twelve, so the LSB sets the noise floor: shrink the step and the quantization noise falls with its square.

σq = LSB/√12

The sawtooth resets at every step crossing. The histogram is recomputed from the error inside the zoomed window on every draw and stays flat at every bit depth: that flatness is why quantization error gets treated as uniform noise.

How to play
  • The point: Rounding error is a sawtooth that ramps up and resets at every step boundary. It lands on every value equally often, which is why quantization error gets treated as uniform noise, with standard deviation LSB over the square root of 12.

Signal to Noise Ratio (SNR)

The signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) compares the strength of your desired signal to the background noise level. In decibels, take ten times the log of that power ratio.

For a full-scale sine wave through an ADC, that ratio works out to a famous rule of thumb: about 6 dB per bit, plus 1.76 dB.

In plain words: each extra bit buys about 6 dB, which means twice the resolution, or half the quantization noise voltage. One bit doubles the codes, which halves the noise voltage, and halving the voltage is 6 dB. The 1.76 is a shape factor: the head start the sine wave's shape gives you over the noise's shape.

Sampling Rate and Aliasing

So far we discussed the amplitude axis controlled by the LSB. The time axis is controlled by the sampling rate. The sampling rate tells you how many samples per second the ADC can take. The signal bandwidth links to this sampling rate through the Nyquist rule: the sampling rate must be at least twice the highest frequency in the signal.

Here the highest frequency component of your analog signal must stay below the Nyquist frequency, which is half the sampling rate. If you sample lower than that, the high frequency part of the signal folds back into lower frequencies and you get aliasing.

Sampling keeps one value every sample period, which is the inverse of the sampling rate. This is similar to multiplying the continuous signal by a train of delta spikes spaced by the sample period. In the frequency domain that multiplication corresponds to repetition of the signal's spectrum at multiples of the sampling rate: periodicity in one domain is repetition in the other. If those repeats overlap, the frequencies mix.

Multiplying the time domain by this train of spikes convolves the spectrum with the train's own spectrum, which replicates your original spectrum at each spike location.

The red wave is what the ADC thinks it saw. Drag the signal past the Nyquist line and watch 600 Hz become 400 Hz. Nothing about the dots changed. That is why aliasing is scary: the samples are telling the truth and lying at the same time.

Say the ADC sampling rate is 1 kHz, that means we sample every 1 ms. If our analog signal was a sine wave at 600 Hz, each cycle would last 1.67 ms. Because the ADC can only represent up to the Nyquist frequency of 500 Hz, anything above that mirrors back around 500 Hz.

In our example that lands at 400 Hz. Two different analog frequencies produce identical sample sequences when sampled at 1 kHz. Another way to look at aliasing is through phase: a sine wave of frequency f_sig completes one full 360° rotation per period, and the period is one over the signal frequency.

At 1 kHz with a 600 Hz sine wave, we won't land on the same phase each time: the phase advances more than 180° between samples, skipping ahead in cycles, so it shows up as a lower-frequency mirror. The phase jumps around so much between samples that the ADC's reconstruction sees a lower frequency mirror. Move more than 180° per sample and you skip ahead too far: the samples look like a slower reverse motion, at 400 Hz.

Higher sampling rate means more data, higher power and better temporal precision. The quantization noise is spread over the full Nyquist band, so increasing the sampling rate spreads the noise thinner per Hz, slightly improving SNR per bandwidth.

Anti-Aliasing Filter

If you can’t increase the sampling rate, you can still avoid aliasing using anti-aliasing filtering. Before the ADC samples your analog signal the system inserts an analog low-pass filter, called anti-aliasing filter, that removes all frequencies above the Nyquist frequency. This filter chops off the high frequency parts of the signal so they can’t fold back.

analog signal → low-pass filter → ADC → digital samples

Where the filter sits: between the analog world and the sampler. Kill the high frequencies here and they can never fold.

The anti-aliasing low-pass cutoff is a little below Nyquist. Because filtering is not perfect, we typically oversample slightly so the filtering design constraint is easy for digital downsampling later.

Filters have a transition band and it doesn’t suddenly go from 100% pass to 100% block, it rolls off gradually. A filter’s cut off is the point where the output power has fallen to half of its input power. Half power is -3 dB, about 0.7x the amplitude.

The roll-off is the rate at which the filter attenuates the signals beyond the cutoff and it’s measured in dB per decade. A decade is 10x frequency.

It’s how sharp the transition band is. The filter order adds another stage that makes the roll-off steeper. Higher order means steeper cutoff, stronger rejection of unwanted frequencies but also more complexity, more power and more phase distortion. If the Nyquist is close to the signal bandwidth, you need a filter that attenuates everything beyond Nyquist very strongly, that’s a higher-order filter.

Let’s take a look at our aliasing example again. At a 500 Hz cutoff the filter is only down -3 dB. Strong 600 Hz components would still leak through with full strength and alias down to 400 Hz, so you need to set the cutoff lower so the attenuation is great, about -40 dB. The actual cutoff depends on the filter and its order. A first order filter attenuates at -20 dB per decade and each additional order adds another -20 dB/decade.

If you want at least -40 dB of attenuation by 500 Hz, start the roll-off at 400 Hz, where the response is -3 dB. For a Butterworth low-pass filter, higher order means a steeper collapse past the cutoff, which is exactly why you pay for higher order when Nyquist sits close to your signal. The tool below computes the response live: drag the cutoff and watch the interferer's attenuation change.

Remember that using higher order filters comes with side effects, phase shift. If your signal contains many frequencies at once then each component might get delayed by a different amount and when you combine them the timing is no longer aligned. The waveform shape is altered even if the spectrum looks fine.

Common IIR filters like Butterworth have non-linear phases outside their transition bands. The higher the order the more non-linearity and more total phase delay. That is why sometimes it is wiser to use two lower-order filters cascaded than one higher order filter.

We’re typically interested in signals up to 250 Hz. For example high gamma signals are around 70-200 Hz. At 1 kHz sampling rate, with 500 Hz Nyquist frequency, we can capture this band using a high-order filter. The timing of HGA can be slightly skewed because the filter's group delay ripples can extend well into 200 Hz.

Okay, so that is ADC in a nutshell, at least how I understand it so far. Bit depth sets how many codes you get, and together with V_ref that gives you the LSB, the size of each stair step. Gain is what shrinks the electrode LSB down to microvolts so tiny neural signals don’t get lost between steps. Quantization error is stuck at half a step no matter how clean your signal is, and each extra bit buys you 6 dB of SNR.

Then the time axis: sample faster than twice your highest frequency or the high stuff folds back as aliases, and if you can’t sample faster, put an anti-aliasing low-pass filter in front and accept the phase cost. I’m still learning this, so take all of it as working notes.
