Learning from rewards

Many tasks give no labelled answers, only a reward after each try, as in a game, a robot or a chat reply. How can a learner get better from those rewards alone? Learning from rewards like this is called reinforcement learning (RL).

The running example is my banana collector, built in the Unity game engine, a learner in a square arena that should gather yellow bananas and avoid blue ones.

Figure 1 shows the arena the whole page keeps coming back to, a square room where one agent learns by stepping. The yellows are the bananas to gather, the blues are the ones to avoid, and their pay is all the agent ever hears. The agent starts far from the yellows, so its first moves are mostly guesses.

Once trained, the collector gathers yellows quickly and steps around blues.

1 The reinforcement learning loop

The agent is the learner that picks the moves. A state is the agent’s situation right now; a move, or action, is one choice the agent can make in a state; a reward is the number the world hands back after each move, good or bad. Here the agent is the banana collector. It is paid for each yellow banana it eats and charged for each blue one, but it is not told in advance which steps pay; it finds out only by stepping.

RL, short for reinforcement learning, is learning which moves to make from rewards rather than from labelled answers. In the arena most steps meet no banana, so most steps bring no reward at all. The environment is everything outside the agent that responds to its moves and hands back a reward. A policy is the agent’s rule for choosing a move in each state; it may always pick the same move or pick by chance. The collector sees the bananas around it and must decide which way to step from each spot.

The world can be random too, so the same move in the same state does not always lead to the same next state or reward. New bananas drop in new places, so the collector’s path changes from one episode to the next.

Figure 2 shows the loop the agent repeats for its whole training, one move answered by a reward and a next state. The move goes down to the world, and the reward and the next state come back up, so the two arrows matter more than the two boxes. The collector steps toward a yellow banana, the world hands back a gain and a new spot, and the old spot's guess rises a little.

2 The banana collector environment

A discrete action is one move from a fixed short list. An episode is one run from the start until it ends. Here an episode is one run of the arena until its clock runs out. The collector reads the arena through a list of sensor readings, one list per step.

The banana collector's world is small. It sees the arena only through 37 sensor readings and chooses from only four moves, so every score on this page is about those readings and those moves. The pay is just as small, a gain for a yellow banana and a loss for a blue one, and the whole method is about turning that tiny pay into good play.

The loop in code starts with a policy that picks every move by chance.

from unityagents import UnityEnvironment

env = UnityEnvironment(file_name="Banana.app")

3 Why copying an expert is not enough

Behaviour cloning is copying an expert’s recorded moves as labels and training on them. The expert’s recordings show only a few routes through the arena, and the rest of it stays unseen. Language models are first trained this way, by copying example replies, before reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) improves them, as Aligning Language Models with RL shows. In the arena no one has recorded which step is best from each spot, so the collector has only its rewards to learn from.

Figure 3 shows why copying an expert cannot teach this collector: no one recorded a right answer for every step. The top half needs labels the arena never had; the bottom half is all the collector gets, a reward after each try. A step that eats a yellow banana earns a gain, and that gain is the only lesson the step teaches.

4 Why RL is hard

A trajectory is the states, moves and rewards of one episode, in order. The return is all the reward still to come, with later rewards counted a little less. A yellow banana sits in the open while another hides behind a blue one, so the collector must choose which to head for first.

Figure 4 shows why a reward that arrives later is worth less than one that arrives now. The bars under the steps fade the further ahead they are. Take the collector choosing between a yellow banana one step away and a better route three steps on: the nearer banana counts more.

Equation 1. The collector has to choose between a banana now and a better route later. A delivery robot choosing between a quick drop-off now and a fuller route later faces the same choice.

Example. Take a run where the collector eats a yellow banana now and another three steps later: the later banana counts less, shrunk once for each step of delay.

Intuition. The sum does not stop at two rewards: it runs on, each reward shrunk once more per step.
The mathematics. This line says the sum runs on: plus \(\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\gamma^{2}}\) times \(\htmlStyle{color:#7d6608}{r_{t+3}}\), dot dot dot, to the end of the episode. In sum form, \(\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{G_t}\) equals the sum over \(k\) of \(\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\gamma}\) to the \(k\) times \(\htmlStyle{color:#7d6608}{r_{t+k+1}}\). \(k\) counts steps of delay, so \(k\) equals zero is the next reward at full worth. \(\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\gamma^{2}}\) is the discount applied twice, once per step of delay, and \(\htmlStyle{color:#7d6608}{r_{t+3}}\) is the reward three steps after the move.
Example. Take a run that earns bananas at the first, third, and last steps: each later banana joins the sum shrunk once more per step of delay.
3\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{G_t}} = \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#7d6608}{r_{t+1}}} + \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\gamma}}\, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{G_{t+1}}}\)
Intuition. The return can be written in one step: the next reward, plus the same return counted from the step after.
The mathematics. This line says \(\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{G_t}\) equals \(\htmlStyle{color:#7d6608}{r_{t+1}}\) plus \(\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\gamma}\) times \(\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{G_{t+1}}\). \(\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{G_{t+1}}\) is the return from the next step, the same later rewards, each with one fewer discount applied. Example. Take a run with no banana for three steps, then a yellow one: the return from the first spot is just that later banana, shrunk once per step.
Take the collector standing one step from a yellow banana, with a blue banana just past it. The return from that spot counts the yellow banana it is about to eat at full worth, then the blue banana after it shrunk once by the discount, then every banana after that shrunk once more per step, until the clock ends the episode.

Figure 5 shows the discount at work, how a reward loses worth with every step of delay. The three buttons set the discount to small, middle, or large, and the bars redraw, shrinking from left to right as the delay grows. With the discount small, far rewards count for almost nothing, so the bars past a few steps flatten.

Credit assignment is working out which earlier moves earned a reward that arrives much later. In the arena a yellow banana can be eaten many steps after the turn that led to it, and that turn still deserves the credit.

Exploration means trying moves to learn what they do; exploitation means playing the move that looks best so far. A collector that only repeats its first good path never finds the yellow cluster behind it.

5 Scoring states and moves with values

5.1 Values of states and moves

A spot is worth the return to expect from standing on it, and a move is worth the return to expect after making it. An expected value is the average over everything that can happen by chance, weighted by how likely each outcome is, so the score already allows for luck. Equation 2 writes both scores down, one for spots and one for moves.

Figure 6 shows what a state's value looks like on the floor: darker spots are worth more. The dark corner sits beside the yellows and the pale corner beside the blues, so the shading is the collector's whole knowledge about where to stand. A spot one step from a yellow banana is worth more than one step from a blue one.

Equation 2. Once the return scores a whole run, the agent needs to compare spots and moves before it moves. A warehouse robot that must pick which aisle to enter next needs the same scores.

1\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{V}}^{\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\pi}}}(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s}}) = \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\mathbb{E}}}_{\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\pi}}}\big[\, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{G_t}} \mid \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s_t}} = \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s}} \,\big]\)
Intuition. A spot is worth what the runs from it earn on average, so spots can be compared before moving.
The mathematics. This line says \(\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{V}^{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\pi}}(\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s})\) equals the expected value under \(\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\pi}\) of \(\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{G_t}\), given \(\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s_t}\) is \(\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s}\). \(\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{V}^{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\pi}}\) is the state value under the policy \(\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\pi}\), the rule for choosing moves. \(\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\mathbb{E}}_{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\pi}}\) with \(\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\pi}\) below marks the expected value: take each run from this spot, multiply its return by its chance under the policy, and add them up. The square brackets hold \(\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{G_t}\), the return from Equation 1. The bar with \(\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s_t}\) equals \(\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s}\) reads starting from state \(\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s}\).
Example. Take a spot one step from a yellow banana and two steps from a blue one: most runs from there earn the yellow first.
2\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{Q}}^{\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\pi}}}(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s}}, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a}}) = \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\mathbb{E}}}_{\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\pi}}}\big[\, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{G_t}} \mid \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s}}, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a}} \,\big]\)
Intuition. A move is worth what the runs after it earn on average, so moves from one spot can be compared.
The mathematics. This line says \(\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{Q}^{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\pi}}(\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s},\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a})\) equals \(\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\mathbb{E}}_{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\pi}}\) of \(\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{G_t}\), given the spot \(\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s}\) and the move \(\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a}\). \(\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{Q}^{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\pi}}\) is the move value under the policy, the score for a move. The bar pins the start to spot \(\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s}\) and move \(\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a}\), one of the collector's four moves played first. Example. Take a spot where two moves both reach a yellow banana, but one passes a blue one first: the safer move scores higher.
Take the collector on the spot one step from a yellow banana. Stepping toward it eats the banana on the next move, and the runs after that step branch from the spot past the banana, while stepping away leaves the banana for later runs, so the two moves carry different values even though they start from the same spot.

Before training, the agent has no idea which spots lie near yellows.

Best play is the policy that earns the most return; \(Q^{*}\) is each move’s value under it. Nobody hands the collector that policy; it has to find it by stepping.

5.2 Improving a guess after one step

The collector does not know \(V\) for any spot, so it keeps a guess for each spot and improves it.

The collector needs something to aim its guesses at, and waiting for the episode to end is slow. In the arena the clock can end the run long after a spot was left, so waiting for the end can take many steps. Equation 3 gives two aims: one that waits for the whole return, and one that learns after a single step.

Figure 7 shows the two ways to build a target, and the difference is only when the update happens. Monte Carlo waits for the whole return, so its target is exact but known only at the end, while one-step TD updates after a single step, using the next spot's guess. A yellow banana eaten gets a TD target at once, while its Monte Carlo target waits for the clock to end the episode.

Equation 3. The agent's guesses need something to aim at. A self-driving car that must judge whether a turn was good needs the same choice.

1\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#196f3d}{y}}^{\text{MC}}_t = \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{G_t}}\)
Intuition. A target is the value a guess is pulled toward. The Monte Carlo target is the return the run really earned, so it is exact for that run, but it is known only when the episode ends.
The mathematics. This line says \(\htmlStyle{color:#196f3d}{y}^{\text{MC}}_t\) equals \(\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{G_t}\). MC on top stands for Monte Carlo, and \(t\) below is the step the target belongs to. \(\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{G_t}\) is the return from Equation 1, the score for the run starting at that step: the banana rewards still to come, added up with the discount.
Example. Take a run where the collector eats two yellow bananas before the clock ends the episode: the Monte Carlo target is the full discounted score of that run.
2\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#196f3d}{y}}^{\text{TD}}_t = \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#7d6608}{r_{t+1}}} + \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\gamma}}\, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{V}}(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s_{t+1}}})\)
Intuition. One step's reward plus a guess for the next spot: a target leaning on a guess.
The mathematics. This line says \(\htmlStyle{color:#196f3d}{y}^{\text{TD}}_t\) equals \(\htmlStyle{color:#7d6608}{r_{t+1}}\) plus \(\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\gamma}\) times \(\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{V}(\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s_{t+1}})\). \(\htmlStyle{color:#196f3d}{y}^{\text{TD}}_t\) is the TD target at step t; TD means temporal difference, the difference between two guesses a step apart. \(\htmlStyle{color:#7d6608}{r_{t+1}}\) is the reward just seen; \(\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\gamma}\) is the discount; and \(\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{V}(\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s_{t+1}})\) is the guess for the next spot, with no policy mark. The walkthrough in Figure 9 reuses this target as its first line. The two lines say one thing: a target is the return the episode earned, or one step's reward plus the discounted guess for the next spot.
Example. Take a step where the collector eats nothing: the TD target is the discounted guess for the spot it lands on.
Take a step where the collector eats a yellow banana and lands one step from another. The Monte Carlo target waits until the clock ends the episode and counts every banana that run earns, while the TD target adds the yellow banana just eaten to the discounted guess for the spot it landed on, and is ready at once. The two targets agree only when the guess for the new spot already matches what the runs from there really earn.

Under the policy, a state is worth what the agent expects from it, and the agent improves its guesses one step at a time instead of waiting for the episode to end. Each step brings a small surprise, and each surprise moves a guess a little. Equation 4 writes that whole step down: the target, the surprise, and the move.

Intuition. The surprise is the gap between target and guess: positive when the step went better than guessed.
The mathematics. This line says \(\htmlStyle{color:#b03a2e}{\delta_t}\) equals \(\htmlStyle{color:#196f3d}{y}^{\text{TD}}_t\) minus \(\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{V}(\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s_t})\). \(\htmlStyle{color:#b03a2e}{\delta_t}\) is the TD error at step t, the target minus the current guess. \(\htmlStyle{color:#196f3d}{y}^{\text{TD}}_t\) is the TD target from Equation 3, and \(\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{V}(\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s_t})\) is the agent's current guess for the spot it stands on, with no policy mark. Take the collector standing one step from a yellow banana with a low guess for that spot. It steps toward the banana and eats it, so the target comes out above the old guess, the surprise is positive, and the guess for the spot it stood on moves part of the way toward the target. Step after step, the guesses for the spots near the banana rise until the surprise averages out to nothing.

After many steps the guesses for spots near yellows end up higher than those near blues.

6 Explore early, exploit late

If the agent keeps a score for every move, it faces a choice at every step: play the best-scored move, or try one it knows less about. The best-looking move can hide a better route the agent never tried, and yellows the top-scored move never turns toward can stay unseen for a whole episode. Equation 5 writes the rule that balances the two.

Equation 5. Values tell the agent which move looks best, but the best-looking move is only as good as the scores behind it, and untried moves have no scores at all. A streaming service choosing which film to recommend faces the same balance.

1\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a_t}} = \arg\max_{\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a}}}\, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{Q}}(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s_t}}, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a}})\)
Intuition. The greedy move is the best-scored move.
The mathematics. This line says \(\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a_t}\) equals argmax over \(\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a}\) of \(\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{Q}(\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s_t},\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a})\): the move played has the largest score. \(\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a_t}\) is the move at step t, one of the collector's four moves. argmax over \(\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a}\) picks the move whose score is largest, scanning every move the agent can make. \(\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{Q}(\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s_t},\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a})\) is the agent's current move score for move \(\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a}\) in the spot \(\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s_t}\), the score Equation 2 defined. This policy is greedy: it always takes the best it knows, which is why it needs the second line.
Example. Take the collector at a crossing with one move scored above the rest: the greedy line plays that move.
2\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a_t}} = \text{a random move} \quad \text{with chance } \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\epsilon}}\)
Intuition. Sometimes the collector ignores the scores and tries a random move, to find better routes.
The mathematics. This line says \(\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a_t}\) equals a random move, with chance \(\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\epsilon}\). \(\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\epsilon}\) is the explore share, the chance of a random move: any move, picked with no regard to its score. Raising it tries more and uses what is known more slowly; lowering it may never find a better route. Together the two lines say one thing: with chance one minus epsilon play the best move, and with chance epsilon play at random. This rule is called epsilon-greedy. The code below runs this rule, and the race in Figure 8 implements it.
Example. Take the collector on a spot where every move scores low: a random step can stumble on a yellow banana no move had scored.
Take the collector at a crossing where one move leads toward a yellow banana and the others lead away. Most of the time it plays the best-scored move toward the banana, and once in a while it tries another move at random instead. An agent can also shrink \(\epsilon\) a little after every episode, so early on it mostly explores and late in training it mostly plays its top-scored move; Q-Learning shows the rule.

def epsilon_greedy(q_values, eps):
    if np.random.rand() < eps:
        return np.random.randint(len(q_values))
    return np.argmax(q_values)

  2. 2Otherwise play the top-scored move.

Exploring is easiest to watch on a small floor where every step can be seen. A warehouse robot looks for its charging dock; the ledge is the strip of floor between the start and the dock. It starts in one bottom corner and the dock sits in the other. Each move’s reward is minus the battery it uses, so the best total is the one closest to zero. Every move costs a little battery; stepping onto the ledge costs a lot and puts the robot back at the start; only reaching the dock ends the episode. The cheapest way to the dock is the shortest path that never steps onto the ledge. A step onto the ledge is a fall. The shortest route runs right beside the ledge, so one random step there is a fall.

9 Reward hacking

Reward hacking is when the agent earns the reward without doing what the designer meant. In the boat racing game CoastRunners, an OpenAI agent learned to circle and hit the same targets again and again instead of finishing the race.

Reward shaping adds extra pay for progress toward the goal. The safe kind also takes the pay back when the agent moves away, so going round a loop earns no net bonus.

A reward model is a network trained to score answers the way people would. A chat model trained against such a score can learn replies that please the score more than the people it stands for, as Aligning Language Models with RL shows.

The tool runs in a small practice arena with one yellow banana and a few blue ones; a banana does not come back once eaten, and a clock ends the episode. In this arena the designer pays a small bonus whenever a step brings the agent closer to the yellow banana.

Equation 7. The reward is the only pay the agent hears, so what the designer writes here decides what the agent learns. A delivery company that pays its drivers per delivery faces the same gap: pay for deliveries, and drivers rush; pay for careful driving, and they slow down.

Intuition. The meant pay counts bananas: yellows add, blues take away.
The mathematics. This line says \(\htmlStyle{color:#7d6608}{r_{t+1}}\) equals \(\htmlStyle{color:#b7950b}{\text{yellow}_{t+1}}\) minus \(\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{\text{blue}_{t+1}}\): the meant pay is the yellow count minus the blue count. \(\htmlStyle{color:#7d6608}{r_{t+1}}\) is the reward after the move at step t, the meant pay. \(\htmlStyle{color:#b7950b}{\text{yellow}_{t+1}}\) is one when the step ate the yellow banana and zero otherwise, and \(\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{\text{blue}_{t+1}}\) is one when the step ate a blue banana and zero otherwise. The minus sign takes the blue count off the yellow count, so eating a blue banana cancels a yellow one.

Intuition. The given pay adds a bonus for getting closer to the meant pay.

Example. Take a step where the collector eats a yellow banana after stepping closer: the shaped pay counts the banana plus the bonus.

The meant pay is nothing, since no banana was eaten, but the shaped pay adds the bonus for getting closer, so the agent is paid for progress even before it eats. That is the bonus a looping agent games: it can earn the bonus again and again by stepping closer, then away, then closer, without ever eating.

10 Scores, chances or one fixed output

An observation is what the agent senses at one moment. Here an observation is the arena’s sensor readings, which can be less than the full state. A spot with a blue banana just out of sight and a spot without one can give the collector the same readings.

A stochastic policy gives each move a chance and draws its move, instead of always playing the same move in the same state. A deterministic policy always plays the same move in the same state. The trained banana agent compares move scores and plays the top one. Equation 8 writes the drawing down.

Some agents, like a robot arm’s, output one amount every time instead of picking from a list of moves.

Figure 12 shows the three ways a policy can pick a move: by scores, by chances, or by one fixed output. Scores pick the tallest bar, chances draw one bar by lottery, and one fixed output is the same arrow every time. Take the banana agent on one spot, comparing its four move scores and playing the tallest bar.
