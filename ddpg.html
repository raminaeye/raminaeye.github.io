Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG)

An actor network outputs continuous torques and a critic network scores them.

DQN (Deep Q-Network) chooses from a short list of actions. The banana agent had four (forward, backward, turn left, turn right); DQN scores each one and takes the biggest.

A robot arm needs a continuous action: how hard to twist each joint, any real number from −1 to 1, so there are infinitely many actions to score. The running example is the RF-DDPG repo, a DDPG solution for the Unity ML-Agents Reacher task, where a double-jointed arm keeps its hand on a floating target.

DDPG uses two networks. An actor turns the state into torques, and a critic scores that state and those torques. The critic learns its scores from rewards as DQN does, and the actor moves its torques in the direction the critic scores higher.

All twenty arms act every step, but learning runs in bursts (Figure 1).

1 The Reacher arm

The Reacher environment is a double-jointed arm bolted to a table, one segment driven by the shoulder and one by the elbow. The job is to keep the hand on a small floating ball for a whole episode, not to touch it once.

Every step the agent gets thirty-three numbers: how bent each joint is, how fast it swings, where the segments are, and where the target sits.

It answers with four torques, two per joint, one for each axis it can turn about, each any real number from −1 to 1. That is a continuous action space: infinitely many settings, recomputed every step.

Episodes run a fixed number of steps. A step with the hand on the target pays +0.1, any other step pays 0, and the episode score is the total.

The repo trains twenty identical arms in parallel in one Unity build; Unity runs the physics and Python runs the networks. brain_name is the key of the arms in the output of env; s is (20, 33), one row per arm, and actions is (20, 4).

import numpy as np
NUM_ARMS = 20

out = env.reset(train_mode=True)
info = out[brain_name]
s = info.vector_observations
scores = np.zeros(NUM_ARMS)
while True:
    actions = agent.act(s)
    out = env.step(actions)
    info = out[brain_name]
    scores += info.rewards
    s = info.vector_observations
    if np.any(info.local_done):
        break
  1. 1Resets all 20 arms and reads their states.
  2. 2Steps the arms until any episode ends, adding up rewards.
When the task counts as solved: the average score over the twenty arms, held over a hundred episodes, and what that score means in steps on target.
average score ≥ 30
This is the solved bar. Each episode's score is first averaged over the twenty arms, then over the last 100 episodes, so one lucky arm or one lucky episode cannot pass it.
on-target steps ≈ 300.1 = 300
Each on-target step pays 0.1, so an average of 30 means about 300 steps per episode with the hand on the ball. The bar is really a holding time.

Three things make it hard. Most steps pay 0, so the agent must work out which of hundreds of past torques earned each +0.1: the credit assignment problem. A slightly wrong torque fails quietly, drifting the hand a millimeter off. And the arm never starts in the same pose twice, so the policy must work from any 33-number state.

Early in training the hand moves in circles and scores under 1; late in training it stays on the ball and scores 35 to 38 (Figure 11).

2 Why argmax fails for continuous actions

DQN chooses with one rule: take the action with the biggest Q value. That needs a finite list of actions.

Two ways to choose an action: DQN scores every action on a list and keeps the best, DDPG has a network output the move directly.
a = argmaxa Q(s, a)
This is DQN's choice. Q(s, a) is the score of move a in state s, and argmax returns the move with the biggest score. With four buttons that is four scores to compare. With four continuous torques there are infinitely many moves, so there is no list to take the biggest of.
104 = 10,000 combos
Splitting each of the 4 torques into 10 levels gives 10 to the power 4 combinations, and every extra torque multiplies the count by 10 again (the curse of dimensionality). The levels also throw away precision: 0.61 versus 0.62 on the elbow can decide whether the hand holds the ball.
a = μ(s)
This is DDPG's choice. The actor μ is a network that maps the state s straight to the four torques a. Choosing a move costs one forward pass, with no list and no search.

The DDPG paper (Lillicrap et al., 2016) keeps DQN's machinery, the Q-network, the replay buffer (a memory of past steps that training samples from at random) and the target network (a slow copy whose values hold still), and adds an actor trained with the deterministic policy gradient: the actor improves by following the gradient the critic hands it.

It is actor-critic and model-free (it learns from rewards alone, with no model of how the world responds). One algorithm, architecture and set of hyperparameters (settings chosen before training) solved more than 20 simulated physics tasks, among them cartpole swing-up, dexterous manipulation, legged locomotion and car driving. Its policies were competitive with a planner that had full access to the dynamics, and for many tasks it learned straight from raw pixels.

3 Actor and critic networks

DDPG splits the job in two. The actor is a policy network: state in, four torques out. The critic is a Q-network: a state-action pair in, one score out.

The layer sizes come from the repo. The actor is a plain stack: 33 state numbers in, three hidden layers, 4 numbers out. Its last layer is a tanh, which squashes every output into [−1, 1], so it cannot ask for an impossible torque. Clipping would have zero gradient outside the range; tanh is smooth, so gradients keep flowing. The repo also applies batch normalization to the input state, and in the actor after the first two hidden layers; the snippets leave it out.

import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F

S, A = 33, 4
L = nn.Linear
actor = nn.Sequential()
actor.append(L(S, 256))
actor.append(nn.ReLU())
actor.append(L(256, 128))
actor.append(nn.ReLU())
actor.append(L(128, 64))
actor.append(nn.ReLU())
actor.append(L(64, A))
actor.append(nn.Tanh())

The critic adds the action late. The state goes through a 256-unit layer first, then torch.cat joins the 4 torques to it, giving 260 numbers, and the head turns them into one score.

class Critic(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__()
        self.fs = L(S, 256)
        self.f1 = L(256 + A, 128)
        self.f2 = L(128, 64)
        self.out = L(64, 1)

    def forward(self, s, a):
        h = F.relu(self.fs(s))
        h = torch.cat([h, a], 1)
        h = F.relu(self.f1(h))
        h = F.relu(self.f2(h))
        return self.out(h)
  1. 1Builds the layers, with S, A and L from the actor snippet; the action joins after the first layer.
  2. 2Encodes the state, appends the action (now 260 numbers per row), and outputs one Q value per row.

Deterministic means the actor outputs the same torques for the same state, with no sampling. A stochastic policy samples from a distribution and explores for free, but it jitters, and an arm should hold still. DDPG adds exploration separately, as noise on the actions.

Each network has roughly 50,000 parameters, just over 100,000 together, plus their target copies.

4 The actor-critic loop

DDPG runs one loop: the actor turns the state into torques, the world returns a reward and the next state, the critic scores the state and torques, and the critic's gradient goes back to the actor.

Figure 5. State, torques, world, reward, critic, gradient: each lap feeds the next. Not drawn: the replay buffer between the world and the critic, and the critic's target copy.

5 The critic update

The critic learns like DQN's Q-network, by a Bellman backup: a move's value should equal the reward it just earned plus the discounted value of where it leaves the arm. Gamma, the discount factor, shrinks that future part. One recorded step (state, torques, reward, next state) is a transition, and the next value comes from the target actor and target critic, not from a search.

One critic update: the target networks set a target value for the move, and the live critic is trained to match it.
y = r + γ·Q′(s′, μ′(s′))
Read it inside out. From the next state s′, the target actor μ′ picks the next move, and the target critic Q′ evaluates that pair. The target y is the reward r just seen plus γ times that value, so a move is worth what it paid now plus a discounted share of what it leads to. This is the same Bellman target as DQN, with the target actor standing in for the max over moves. The primes mark the target networks, so the target holds still while the critic learns.
loss = E[(y − Q(s,a))2]
The loss is E, the average over the minibatch, of the squared gap between y and the live critic's guess Q(s,a) for the move actually taken. Averaging means one odd transition cannot yank the critic around; squaring makes a big miss pull much harder than a small one. When the loss reaches zero, the critic's scores agree with reward plus discounted next score everywhere it was trained.

In PyTorch, over a minibatch (a small random set of transitions from the replay buffer); r has shape (B, 1) for a batch of B.

with torch.no_grad():
    a2 = actor_target(s_next)
    q2 = critic_target(s_next, a2)
    y = r + gamma * q2
loss = F.mse_loss(critic(s, a), y)
critic_opt.zero_grad()
loss.backward()
critic_opt.step()
  1. 1The target actor picks the next action a2 and the target critic scores it (critic card in section 5, line 1).
  2. 2Moves the critic toward y by mean squared error (line 2).

The critic never picks an action; it only judges pairs. Choosing is the actor's job.

6 The actor update

The actor never sees a target action. Instead, ask the critic which way the torques should move to score higher. The critic is differentiable, so the gradient of Q with respect to the action answers exactly, and the actor's weights shift so its torques slide that way.

The chain rule turns that into one line, the deterministic policy gradient theorem:

The actor's update: change the weights so the torques move the way that raises the critic's score.
∇θJ ≈ E[∇aQ(s, μ(s))·∇θμ(s)]
Read it as a sentence: move the weights in the direction that moves the torques in the direction that raises the score. ∇ is the gradient, the direction of steepest increase. ∇aQ says how the critic's score changes when the torques change, which way is uphill. ∇θμ says how the actor's torques change when its weights θ change. Multiplying them, the chain rule, gives how the score changes when the weights change. E is the average over a batch of states from the replay buffer, and ∇aQ is taken at the actor's own action, a = μ(s). J is the actor's objective, the critic's score of its own moves, and ≈ is there because the states come from the buffer and the climb follows the critic's estimate, not the true return: a wrong critic sends the actor confidently the wrong way.

In code, the actor minimizes minus Q: the first line scores the actor's own torques with the critic, and backward multiplies the two factors by the chain rule. Only the actor's optimizer steps, so the critic is not changed here.

q = critic(s, actor(s))
actor_loss = -q.mean()
actor_opt.zero_grad()
actor_loss.backward()
actor_opt.step()
  1. 1The actor's loss is minus the critic's score of its action (actor card in section 6, line 1); the mean is the card's E.
  2. 2Updates only the actor.

The actor goes through the critic because the Unity physics can be run but not differentiated. The critic is a learned, differentiable model of how good an action is, trained on real rewards by the Bellman backup.

Early on the critic's scores are close to random, and the actor follows them anyway: a wrong critic teaches the actor a wrong action. The stabilizers in Target networks, replay buffer and noise limit this.

7 Target networks, replay buffer and noise

DDPG relies on three stabilizers, each preventing one failure (Figure 7).

7.1 Target networks

The critic's target is built from its own predictions, so if the critic changes fast, its target changes fast too. Slow target copies of both networks follow the live ones instead; lerp_ applies the card's blend to every weight, and the same loop runs for the actor pair.

The slow copy: each update, the target weights move a tiny fraction of the way toward the live weights.
θ′ ← τθ + (1−τ)θ′
The arrow means "is replaced by": the target weights θ′ become a blend of a share τ of the live weights θ and the rest, 1−τ, of their own. With τ a tenth of a percent, each target weight moves only a hair per update, so the target value in the critic card barely moves between steps. A larger τ tracks the live networks faster and brings back the moving target; τ = 1 copies them outright.
TAU = 1e-3
live = critic.parameters()
tgt = critic_target.parameters()
with torch.no_grad():
    for t, p in zip(tgt, live):
        t.lerp_(p, TAU)

DQN copied its target network in full every 10,000 updates (Mnih et al., 2015); DDPG makes the copy continuous and small.

7.2 Replay buffer

Every transition from every arm, with its episode-end flag d, goes into one memory, and training samples random minibatches from it. Random sampling breaks the link between consecutive steps, each transition is reused many times, and twenty arms fill the buffer twenty times faster than one.

import random
from collections import deque

BUFFER_SIZE, BATCH = 100_000, 256
buf = deque(maxlen=BUFFER_SIZE)
buf.append((s, a, r, s_next, d))
batch = random.sample(buf, BATCH)

7.3 Ornstein-Uhlenbeck noise

A deterministic actor left alone repeats its first guess forever, so during training DDPG adds Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) noise to the torques. Gaussian (white) noise is independent every step, so torques stutter; OU noise remembers its last value and is pulled back toward zero, so it wanders smoothly:

Exploration noise for the torques: a smooth random wander around zero, added to the actor's output during training.
x ← x − κ·x + σ·ξ
x is the noise, one value per torque, and it carries over from step to step. The term κ·x pulls it back toward zero, harder the further it has wandered. σ sets the size of each fresh kick, and ξ is a new standard normal random number every step. Because x keeps most of its last value, consecutive steps drift together instead of twitching; a larger κ makes the wander shorter, a larger σ makes it wider. The DDPG paper and the repo use κ = 0.15 (the paper calls it θ) and σ = 0.2.
a = μ(s) + x
The torques sent to the arm are the actor's output μ(s) plus the noise x. Noise is on during training, so the arm tries nearby torques the actor would not choose, and typically off at test time, so the arm can hold still.

For an arm with inertia (a moving part keeps moving), smooth noise tries coherent strategies ("push left for a while, see what happens") instead of vibrating in place. The DDPG paper chose OU noise for this reason, and the repo keeps it.

8 One DDPG training update

One training update has five parts: sample, compute the target, update the critic, update the actor, update the targets. It trains on stored experience, never only on the latest step. This is off-policy learning: the networks learn from experience gathered by older versions of the actor, which is why the replay buffer can be reused.

One DDPG training update in four lines: the target network sets the target value, the critic measures its miss, the actor climbs the critic's slope, and the target networks move slowly toward the live networks.
y = r + γ·Q′(s′, μ′(s′))
From the next state, the target actor picks the next move, then the target critic evaluates that pair. The target y is the reward just seen plus the discounted value of that pair. It comes from the target networks so it holds still while the critic learns.
loss = E[(y − Q(s,a))²]
The critic's step pushes down E, the average over the batch of the squared gap between the target value and the live critic's guess for the move actually taken. Averaging limits the effect of one odd transition, and squaring makes big mistakes count more than small ones.
∇θJ ≈ E[∇aQ(s, μ(s))·∇θμ(s)]
The actor moves its weights so its torques raise the score: how the score changes with the torques, at the actor's own torques, times how the torques change with the weights, averaged over the batch. The step is approximate because the actor climbs the critic, a learned model, not the real return; the environment has no gradient, and the critic supplies one.
θ′ ← τθ + (1−τ)θ′
The target weights become a blend of a share τ of the live weights and the rest, 1−τ, of their own. Each update moves them a tenth of a percent of the way toward the live values, so the targets stay stable.

10 The Reacher training run

The real run is the RF-DDPG repo on the Unity ML-Agents Reacher task, with the settings in Figure 10.

One arm's score varies a lot from episode to episode. Twenty arms, each with its own exploration noise, average that variation out and fill the buffer with more varied experience.

At test time the actor runs without noise and outputs four new torques every step.

11 DDPG in practice

The repo runs one update function 64 times every 256 environment steps, a quarter of an update per step, with the settings in Figure 10; Stable-Baselines3 has a widely used PyTorch DDPG. Each update costs forward passes of both target networks on 256 next states, a forward and backward pass of the critic on 256 transitions, a pass through the actor and critic with a backward pass into the actor, and two blends of target weights.

ddpg_update runs one update on a sampled batch, using the actor, the critic, their target copies and their optimizers from the sections above.

import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F
import torch.nn.utils as U

GAMMA, TAU, CLIP = 0.99, 1e-3, 1.0
tgts = actor_target, critic_target
nets = actor, critic

def soft_update(tgt, live):
    dst = list(tgt.parameters())
    src = list(live.parameters())
    with torch.no_grad():
        for t, p in zip(dst, src):
            t.lerp_(p, TAU)

@torch.no_grad()
def td_target(r, s_next, done):
    a2 = actor_target(s_next)
    q2 = critic_target(s_next, a2)
    keep = 1 - done
    return r + GAMMA * q2 * keep

def ddpg_update(batch):
    s, a, r, s_next, done = batch
    y = td_target(r, s_next, done)
    q = critic(s, a)
    critic_loss = F.mse_loss(q, y)
    critic_opt.zero_grad()
    critic_loss.backward()
    w = critic.parameters()
    U.clip_grad_norm_(w, CLIP)
    critic_opt.step()
    q = critic(s, actor(s))
    actor_loss = -q.mean()
    actor_opt.zero_grad()
    actor_loss.backward()
    actor_opt.step()
    for t, n in zip(tgts, nets):
        soft_update(t, n)
  1. 1Sets the constants, pairs each target network with its live network, and defines soft_update.
  2. 2Builds the target y from the target networks; keep is 0 at the end of an episode (update card in section 8, line 1).
  3. 3Critic step on the loss in line 2, with gradient clipping.
  4. 4Actor step up the critic's score (line 3).
  5. 5Soft-updates both target networks (line 4).

Three mistakes break this update.

  • The actor's backward also writes gradients into the critic; they are harmless only because the critic's gradients are zeroed before its next step.
  • Clip actions to [−1, 1] after adding noise; tanh bounds the actor's output, not the noisy action.
  • Store the episode-end flag and multiply the future by 1 − done; without it the last step takes value from the next episode.

12 DDPG failure modes

Each failure has a cause, a sign you can see while training, and a fix.

  • Critic overestimation. The actor climbs toward torques the critic overrates. Sign: the critic's Q estimates keep rising while the score stays flat. Fix: TD3's twin critics (Section 13).
  • Targets that move too fast. A large τ, or a hard copy every update. Sign: the critic loss swings and the score falls back after rising. Fix: τ near the paper's 1e-3.
  • Too little exploration. σ near 0. Sign: the arm repeats one motion and the score stays near 0. Fix: OU noise with σ around 0.2 during training.
  • Noise left on at evaluation. Sign: the trained arm jitters around the target and scores below its real policy. Fix: turn the noise off when you evaluate.

13 TD3, SAC and multi-agent DDPG

TD3 (Twin Delayed DDPG) trains two critics and builds the target from the lower score, which keeps an inflated score out of the target and reduces the critic's overestimation of how good a move is. It also updates the actor less often than the critics, and adds a little noise to the target action so the critic cannot exploit sharp, spurious peaks (Fujimoto et al., 2018).

TD3's target: evaluate the next move with two critics and keep the lower value, after nudging that move with a little noise.
y = r + γ·mini=1,2 Q′i(s′, μ′(s′) + ε)
Q′1 and Q′2 are the two slow target critics, and min keeps the lower of their values. One critic's overestimate is then dropped unless the other critic makes the same mistake, so inflated scores stop feeding into the target. ε is a small clipped noise (in the paper drawn from N(0, 0.2) and clipped to ±0.5) added to the target actor's next move μ′(s′), so the target averages over nearby moves and a sharp, spurious peak in the critic cannot be exploited. The rest is DDPG's target.

SAC (Soft Actor-Critic) drops determinism. The policy stays stochastic and earns an entropy bonus (entropy measures how spread out its choices are), so exploration lives in the objective instead of being bolted on as OU noise. Haarnoja et al. (2018) report better sample efficiency and stability than DDPG on simulated locomotion tasks such as Hopper, Walker and Humanoid.

Multi-agent DDPG is the subject of the Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning page. Its repo uses self-play: one shared actor-critic pair drives both rackets. The MADDPG paper gives each agent its own actor and its own centralized critic, which sees every agent's actions.

DDPG fits continuous actions, a cheap simulator, and a controller that should hold still at test time; for a short list of discrete actions, DQN fits better.

Reacher has one learner in a fixed world. What changes when the other player is learning too? The Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning page answers it with two tennis rackets.
