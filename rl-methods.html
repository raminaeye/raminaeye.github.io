Value, policy, or both

Every RL method runs the reinforcement learning loop (see a state, act, get a reward and the next state); what differs is what the agent keeps to pick its move. A value is a guess of how much future reward to expect; a policy is the rule that picks the move. A few methods also learn, or are handed, a model of the world that predicts what each move will do, and plan with it; methods without one are model-free.

Value-based methods

A value-based agent keeps a score, a Q-value, for every move in every state: the future reward it expects from playing that move and playing well after. It mostly plays the move with the highest score, trying a random move with a small chance epsilon to explore (epsilon-greedy), and after each step it nudges the played move's score toward a better guess, the same update as the toy banana step on the RL page:

Q-learning moves the played score a little toward what the step revealed.
target = r + γ · max Q(s′, a′)
r is the reward the move earned and s′ the state it led to. max Q(s′, a′) is the best score on offer there, the max taken over every move a′ available in s′. The discount γ, a number just below 1, shrinks it a little for the delay.
Q(s, a) ← Q(s, a) + α · (target − Q(s, a))
The arrow means "replace with". The score of move a in state s moves a fraction α, the learning rate, of the way toward the target.
Q = np.zeros((n_states, n_moves))         # the table: one score per move
a = Q[s].argmax() if random.random() > eps else random.randrange(n_moves)
s2, r = env.step(a)
target = r + gamma * Q[s2].max()
Q[s, a] += alpha * (target - Q[s, a])

Q-learning (Watkins and Dayan 1992) keeps these scores in a table, one row per state.

Q-learning and SARSA

SARSA, named for the five things one update uses (State, Action, Reward, next State, next Action), changes one term of the Q-learning target (Sutton and Barto 2018, section 6.4):

Q-learning and SARSA differ only in which next move the target scores.
targetQ = r + γ · max Q(s′, a′)
Q-learning scores the best move in s′, whatever move comes next. It learns the values of always playing the best move while it is really exploring, so it is off-policy: it learns about a policy other than the one making its moves.
targetSARSA = r + γ · Q(s′, a′next)
a′next is the move epsilon-greedy really picks in s′, random moves included. SARSA learns the values of the policy it runs, so it is on-policy.
a2 = Q[s2].argmax() if random.random() > eps else random.randrange(n_moves)
target_q = r + gamma * Q[s2].max()        # Q-learning: the best next move
target_sarsa = r + gamma * Q[s2, a2]      # SARSA: the move it will play next

Sutton and Barto's cliff walk (Example 6.6) shows the difference. Every step costs −1, a step into the cliff costs −100, and both agents explore with epsilon 0.1. Q-learning settles on the shortest route along the edge, where its random moves sometimes take it over; SARSA's targets count those random moves, so it settles on a safer route.

Learning rule

Routes come from each rule's settled scores; the average counts the random moves made on the way.

Deep Q-networks

DQN, deep Q-network (Mnih et al. 2015), replaces the table with a neural network that outputs one score per move; my banana agent is one (the DQN page). Two fixes keep it from oscillating or diverging:

  • Experience replay. Training samples random batches from a replay buffer, a memory of past steps, so nearly identical steps do not arrive in a row. Those steps came from older policies, so replay needs an off-policy method.
  • Target network. A frozen copy of the network computes the targets and is recopied every C updates (C = 10,000 in the paper), so targets hold still between copies.

Among noisy scores the highest is usually one that came out too high, so the max makes Q-learning and DQN overestimate (van Hasselt et al. 2016). Double DQN fixes the target:

Double DQN splits picking the next move from scoring it.
targetDQN = r + γ · max Q−(s′, a′)
Q− is the frozen copy. The same scores both pick the best move and value it, so a score that came out too high is the one picked and used.
a* = argmax Q(s′, a′)
The learning network Q picks the move. argmax returns the move a′ with the highest score, not the score itself.
targetDouble = r + γ · Q−(s′, a*)
The frozen copy values that move. A score inflated in one network is less often inflated in the other, so the target is inflated less often.

Dueling DQN (Wang et al. 2016) changes the network instead, splitting it into two streams:

A dueling network adds a state value and a per-move bonus.
Q(s, a) = V(s) + A(s, a) − meana′ A(s, a′)
V(s) is the value of the state; A(s, a), the advantage, is how much better move a is than the others. Subtracting the mean advantage over all moves a′ makes the split unique, since otherwise any amount could shift between V and A. V learns from every step, which helps when many moves score about the same.

My banana network uses both (the DQN page); the RL page's three-algorithm comparison shows why scoring every move needs a short list of moves.

Policy-based methods

A policy-based agent skips the scores. Its network turns the state into a probability for each move, and the agent rolls its move from them. It learns once per episode, one run from the start until the world resets. Then every move becomes more likely in proportion to the return that followed it, the reward collected from that move on. That is REINFORCE (Williams 1992), written as the loss the code makes smaller:

REINFORCE makes each move more likely in proportion to the reward that came after it.
loss = −Σt log π(at | st) · Gt
π(at | st) is the probability the policy gave the move it made at step t (the bar reads "given"). Gt is the return from step t on, each later reward shrunk once per step of delay by the discount γ. log, the logarithm, grows whenever that probability grows. Σt adds over the episode. The minus sign makes it a loss: moves before a large return get pushed up, moves before a negative one get pushed down.
dist = torch.distributions.Categorical(logits=policy(states))  # one row per step
log_probs = dist.log_prob(actions)        # log pi(a_t | s_t) for the moves played
returns, G = [], 0.0
for r in reversed(rewards):               # walk back from the last step
    G = r + gamma * G                     # the return from this step on
    returns.insert(0, G)
loss = -(log_probs * torch.tensor(returns)).sum()
loss.backward()

The weakness is noise: Gt sums every reward to the episode's end, each partly luck, so the same move can get a large push in one episode and a negative one in the next. Two additions help (Sutton and Barto 2018, section 13.4; Mnih et al. 2016):

A baseline steadies the push, and an entropy bonus keeps the policy exploring.
loss = −Σt log π(at | st) · (Gt − b(st))
The baseline b(st) is any guess that depends on the state but not on the move. On average it changes nothing about the push, so the method still heads the same way; it only shrinks how much the push swings from episode to episode. A learned guess of the return from st is the usual choice.
H(π(· | s)) = −Σa π(a | s) · log π(a | s)
H, the entropy, measures how spread out the move probabilities are: largest when every move is equally likely, zero when one move is certain. The dot in π(· | s) stands for all moves at once, and Σa adds over them.
losstotal = loss − β · Σt H(π(· | st))
β is a small weight. Subtracting the entropy rewards keeping options open, so the policy does not lock onto one move too early.

Set the baseline to a learned value V(s) and it becomes a second network: that is actor-critic.

Actor-critic methods

The actor is the REINFORCE policy. The critic is the learned baseline V(s), and the actor asks whether a move beat it, not whether its return was big.

In one actor-critic update the critic's guess sets the bar, and the actor's move is judged against it.
A(s, a) = Q(s, a) − V(s)
The advantage again, the dueling network's per-move bonus: how much better move a is than the critic expects from state s.
A ≈ G − V(s)
≈ reads "is estimated by". One estimate: the return G that came after the move, minus the guess. Positive if the move beat the guess, negative if it fell short.
A ≈ δ = r + γ · V(s′) − V(s)
Another: one real reward plus the critic's guess from the next state. δ is the TD error (temporal difference), how much one step changed the guess. It is less noisy than G but only as good as the critic.
actor loss = −log π(a | s) · A
The REINFORCE line with the advantage in place of the return: the move gets more likely only if it beat the guess.
critic loss = (G − V(s))2
The miss, squared so either direction counts. Making it smaller pulls the guess toward the real return.

In the code, the detach call keeps the actor's line from training the critic.

returns = torch.tensor(returns)           # G for every step, from the REINFORCE loop
values = critic(states).squeeze(-1)       # V(s) for every step
advantage = returns - values              # A = G - V(s)
dist = torch.distributions.Categorical(logits=actor(states))
actor_loss = -(dist.log_prob(actions) * advantage.detach()).mean()
critic_loss = ((returns - values) ** 2).mean()
(actor_loss + critic_loss).backward()

A3C, asynchronous advantage actor-critic (Mnih et al. 2016), runs many workers on copies of the world, all updating one shared network; seeing different situations at once steadies learning without a replay buffer. Its G is a few real rewards plus the critic's guess, and it adds the entropy bonus. A2C, advantage actor-critic, is the synchronous version that waits for every copy before one update. Both are on-policy.

PPO clipping

Reusing one on-policy batch for several updates saves data, but plain REINFORCE-style updates then move the policy destructively far (Schulman et al. 2017). PPO, proximal policy optimization, caps how far one batch can move the policy:

PPO stops rewarding a change once the new policy leaves a band around the old one.
ratio = πnew(a | s)πold(a | s)
How much more likely (above 1) or less likely (below 1) the policy being trained makes the move than πold, the policy that collected the batch.
clipped = clip(ratio, 1 − ε, 1 + ε)
clip holds the ratio inside the band from 1 − ε to 1 + ε. ε here is the band's half-width, 0.2 in the paper, not the exploration chance.
objective = min(ratio · A, clipped · A)
min keeps the smaller of the plain and clipped terms, the pessimistic choice. Training makes the objective larger; the loss is its negative.
ratio = torch.exp(new_log_probs - old_log_probs)   # pi_new / pi_old
clipped = torch.clamp(ratio, 1 - eps, 1 + eps)
loss = -torch.min(ratio * adv, clipped * adv).mean()

The paper's full loss adds the critic's loss and an entropy bonus. PPO drives RLHF in Aligning Language Models with RL.

Continuous actions

A continuous action is any real number in a range rather than a pick from a list, like my Reacher arm's four joint torques; no list of move scores can cover them. DDPG, deep deterministic policy gradient (Lillicrap et al. 2016), has a critic that scores a state together with an action, Q(s, a), and an actor μ(s) that outputs one action and nudges it toward a higher score. It is off-policy, with replay and frozen target copies (the DDPG page).

DDPG inherits Q-learning's overestimation. TD3, twin delayed DDPG (Fujimoto et al. 2018), changes the target:

TD3 smooths the next action, then trusts the lower of two critics.
ã = μ−(s′) + clip(noise, −c, c)
μ− is the frozen copy of the actor. Small random noise, clipped to stay within c of zero (spread 0.2, c = 0.5 in the paper), is added to its action, so the target cannot rest on one narrow, too-high peak of the critic.
target = r + γ · min(Q1−(s′, ã), Q2−(s′, ã))
Two critics learn side by side; the target takes the smaller of their frozen copies' scores, which pushes against overestimation instead of with it.

Third, the actor and frozen copies update once per two critic updates (what came after DDPG).

SAC, soft actor-critic (Haarnoja et al. 2018), has a stochastic actor, one that outputs a spread of actions, and puts the entropy into the goal:

SAC is paid for reward and for keeping its actions spread out.
objective = Σt E[rt + α · H(π(· | st))]
E, the expectation, averages over the states and actions the policy meets. H is the entropy from the policy-based section, now earned at every step. α, the temperature, sets how much spread is worth against reward.

It is off-policy with replay, and in the paper it trained stably across random seeds.

What the agent stores

The same world and the same route, stored as a score for every move, the move itself, or both.

Model-based methods

A model predicts the next state and reward from a state and a move; planning uses it to look ahead or to practise without the real world. Dyna (Sutton 1991) adds n imagined Q-learning steps from a remembered model after each real one (Dyna-Q, Sutton and Barto 2018, section 8.2):

a = epsilon_greedy(Q, s)
s2, r = env.step(a)                                       # one real step
Q[s, a] += alpha * (r + gamma * Q[s2].max() - Q[s, a])
model[(s, a)] = (r, s2)                                   # remember what happened
for _ in range(n):                                        # n imagined steps
    (ps, pa), (pr, ps2) = random.choice(list(model.items()))
    Q[ps, pa] += alpha * (pr + gamma * Q[ps2].max() - Q[ps, pa])
s = s2

In the book's maze (Example 8.1), 5 or 50 imagined steps per real step found a good route in far fewer episodes. AlphaZero (Silver et al. 2018) uses a game's rules for a tree search, a look-ahead over move sequences that a network steers, and learns from games against itself. MuZero (Schrittwieser et al. 2020) learns the model instead:

AlphaZero searches with the real rules; MuZero searches with a learned model.
(p, v) = f(s)
AlphaZero: from a position s, one network f gives p, a probability for each move, and v, the predicted result. The rules produce each next position in the search.
z0 = repr(observation)
MuZero: a representation network repr turns what the agent sees into z0, a hidden state (a vector of numbers).
(r, zk+1) = dynamics(zk, a)
The dynamics network stands in for the rules: from hidden state zk and move a, it predicts the reward and the next hidden state.
(p, v) = f(zk)
The same move probabilities and value as AlphaZero, read from the hidden state. The hidden state only has to predict rewards, moves and values, never to redraw the screen.

MuZero matched AlphaZero at Go, chess and shogi and set a new best on 57 Atari games. World Models (Ha and Schmidhuber 2018) train a small controller entirely inside a learned model's dream, then move it to the real game, though it can learn to exploit the dream's flaws. Dreamer (Hafner et al. 2020) also learns in imagination, passing gradients of predicted values back through imagined trajectories in a compact learned state.

Choosing a method

Two facts usually decide: whether actions are a short list (discrete) or real numbers, and what a real step costs.

Few states, fits in a tableQ-learning, or SARSA if exploring mistakes are costlySARSA plans around its own random moves, as on the cliff
Short list of moves, many numbers or pixels inDQN with Double and DuelingOff-policy, so replay reuses every step
Continuous actions, real steps costlyTD3 or SACOff-policy with replay, and steadier than DDPG
Many cheap copies of a simulatorPPO or A2CSimple on-policy updates from fresh data; PPO's clip keeps each update small
