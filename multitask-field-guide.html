The Multitask Field Guide

The practice half of multitask learning: diagnose which disease you have, batch jointly, admit only the losses that must shape the trunk, and know what no optimizer can fix.

The Introduction to Multitask Learning page names the three diseases, scale, speed, and direction, and teaches the math kit, the geometry that tells them apart. This page is what you do with that: three lines of logging tell you which disease you actually have, and the rest is running one shared trunk, the part of the model all the tasks share, well.

Diagnose your failure mode first

Three lines of logging tell you which of the three diseases you actually have. Only then do you pick the fix.

  1. Each task's loss, compared with training that task alone. A gap here is negative transfer, the joint model doing worse than the task trained alone, by definition. The other two measurements explain why the gap is there.
  2. Each task's gradient norm at a shared layer (the size of that task's gradient on the shared trunk). If one is orders of magnitude bigger than the others, you have a scale problem, and the fix is to rebalance how loudly the tasks speak.
  3. The cosine between each pair of task gradients. Often negative means a direction problem, and the fix is to remove the fighting component from the update. Once plain gradient descent is trapped in a ravine, a long narrow valley in the loss landscape where the walls are steep but the floor barely slopes toward the exit, the two gradients can keep fighting for the rest of the run. The conflict never resolves itself.

Both measurements are one line of arithmetic, and both come straight from the math kit on the introduction page. The norm reading is the loudness check: if one task's arrow on the shared trunk is far longer than the others, it shouts far louder, and in the plain sum the quiet gradients are a rounding error. That is the scale disease, and the fix is rebalancing the tasks' loudness.

For direction, the number is the cosine from the same math kit: 1 means the same direction, 0 means perpendicular, -1 means exactly opposed.

A cosine near -1 means nearly exactly opposed: their average is a coin flip decided by whoever is louder. That is the direction disease, and the fix is removing the fighting component from the update.

The same three diseases show up as symptoms first. This is the table I keep taped to the wall:

Five multitask mistakes to avoid

Five mistakes I have watched teams make, including mine:

  1. Watching only the average loss. A great average can hide a task that was quietly sacrificed. I always report per-task numbers too, ideally the average per-task drop against single-task baselines, the runs where each task trains on its own, which give me the comparison numbers I measure the joint run against.
  2. Cutting gradients in the task-specific heads, the final output layers each task keeps for itself. Heads share no parameters, so there is nothing to fight over. Combine gradients on the shared trunk only.
  3. Forgetting to renormalize after reweighting. Renormalizing means rescaling the weights back to a fixed total after reweighting. Without it, the weights slowly change the overall learning speed, and the comparison against baselines stops meaning anything.
  4. Running full K-task surgery (K is the number of tasks) on a 50-task problem. By surgery I mean editing gradient directions, cutting the part of one gradient that fights another, rather than changing how loud each loss is. K backward passes, each one the reverse run through the network that computes one task's gradients, per step is the real price of per-pair direction surgery. Use pair subsampling, checking only a sample of task pairs each step instead of every pair, or a cheaper approximation.
  5. Treating every conflict as poison. The tragic triad (conflict arriving together with high curvature, how sharply the loss landscape bends, and a large gap in gradient sizes) says conflict mostly hurts when it comes with a loudness gap and high curvature. Mild conflict between equals is often harmless.

Multitask deployment: what actually matters

"Our tasks have disjoint datasets. Does this still apply?" Yes, and this is the most common misconception I run into. Every task can have its own dataset, with no example shared between datasets, and its own head; the gradients still meet in one place, the shared backbone, which is the shared trunk under another name. Conflict is a property of gradients on shared parameters, not of shared data.

The per-task dataset, per-task head, shared-trunk setup is the standard case these methods were built on. They would only become irrelevant if the tasks shared nothing at all, and then it would not be multitask learning. To convince a skeptic, log the cosine similarity between pairs of task gradients on the shared trunk for a few hundred steps. If the cosines go negative, and they almost always do, the conflict is real.

"What if one task's batch is much smaller?" A small batch makes a task's gradient jittery: averaged over 8 examples instead of 128, the direction swings step to step, even though it is honest on average. Plain averaging just absorbs the noise. Loudness rebalancing occasionally turns a knob on a bad reading but self-corrects. Direction surgery sometimes cuts against a fight that was not real, which is wasteful, not dangerous.

Steering toward the worst-off task is the most sensitive: its whole decision is "who is worst off right now," and a jittery signal sometimes makes the small-batch task look worst-off when it simply had an unlucky batch. Keeping each step near the plain average caps how far one noisy step can pull the update. The fixes, in rough order of importance:

  1. Oversample the small task. Going from 8 to 32 samples per step already helps a lot. No algorithmic patch beats a steadier signal.
  2. Smooth the decision signal. Keep an exponential moving average of each task's gradient, a running average that weights recent gradients most and forgets old values slowly (decay 0.9 to 0.99), decide on the smoothed gradients, step with the real ones. This composes cleanly with worst-off-task steering: it keeps the protection and the guarantee but stops reacting to one unlucky batch.
  3. Tighten the leash. If you cannot oversample, keep every step closer to the plain average instead of chasing the worst-off task aggressively.
  4. Log the variance, not just the average. If the small task's gradient norm swings wildly over a few dozen steps, you have confirmed a shaky signal before it confuses the algorithm.

Batch jointly every step

Three layouts, and only one of them lets the methods do their job:

  1. Joint batch: all tasks every step. This is the standard, and my recommendation. Each task produces a loss and a gradient on the shared trunk at the same weights at the same moment, which is exactly what "do these gradients fight?" and "which task is worst off?" require.
  2. Round robin: one task per step. The methods become no-ops: no pair to de-conflict, no worst-off task to find. Worse, round robin does not remove the conflict. It spreads it across time, as the backbone zigzags between tasks, the same pacing that traps plain gradient descent. It removes the diagnostic, not the disease.
  3. Random task per step. Same problem as round robin, shuffled. Incompatible with gradient-combining methods.

Within a joint batch, I prefer equal shares per task, oversampling small datasets so every gradient is measured with equal reliability. The whole point is to let the gradients decide the weighting, which only works if every gradient is trustworthy.

If repeating a small dataset worries you, cap the repetition (for example, no task repeats its data more than three times per epoch (one full pass through a dataset) of the largest) rather than shrinking its share. And if a joint batch does not fit in memory, accumulate: run each task's sub-batch, the slice of that task's own data processed in this round, store its gradient, and combine once all tasks have reported. The direction-fixing methods need all task gradients before combining anyway, so accumulation changes where you pay the memory cost, not whether you pay it.

Only bring losses that must shape the trunk

A common situation: each team arrives with its own mature single-task model, trained with half a dozen losses. Should all of them come into multitask training? No. The backbone is the only shared, one-shot resource in the whole system. After multitask training, a team takes the backbone, freezes it, holding its weights fixed so they stop training, and fine-tunes its own head privately, training that already trained head a little more on the team's own job, where it can use whatever losses it wants. So each loss faces one question: does this loss need to shape the shared features, or can it do its job later, on the frozen backbone, inside my own head?

Extra losses are expensive: every added loss is another gradient pulling the trunk; the cost is paid by every team, not just the one who brought it; and the loss weights a team tuned for its single-task model no longer mean anything once loudness rebalancing or worst-off-task steering is involved. The practical filter is the frozen-head test:

  1. Baseline. Multitask-train with each task contributing exactly one loss, its main one. Measure every task's metric.
  2. Frozen-head check. Freeze the backbone; each team fine-tunes its head with its full private stack. Measure again.
  3. Gap analysis. If frozen-head fine-tuning reaches the target, the extra losses were head-level and stay private. If the metric is stuck no matter what the head does, the backbone never learned something the task needs, and that loss belongs in multitask training.
  4. Admit one loss at a time, and re-run. Confirm it closes the gap, and check the other tasks' metrics did not degrade.

Tips that save real time: start with one loss per task, no exceptions. The minimal baseline is the control group that makes every later decision measurable. Freeze the backbone before you argue. Keep the receipt when a loss joins: the other tasks' metrics and the conflict rate, the share of steps where a pair's cosine is negative, before and after. Pre-combine a task's main loss, the loss for the answer the task is actually scored on, and its auxiliary losses, extra helper losses that support training but are not that scored answer, with fixed weights from its single-task tuning history, so the multitask trainer sees one voice per task. And let the conflict measurements referee team debates. Re-run the frozen-head check after big changes. The verdict on a loss is not permanent.

When the trunk is too small, no optimizer can save it

This is the part I consider just as important as the methods. Gradient-level fixes redistribute a shortage; they do not remove it. If the shared trunk is simply too small to hold all the tasks, or the tasks genuinely work against each other, no optimizer can fix that. The limit is then set by the architecture (wider trunks, softer sharing, where tasks share only part of the trunk and keep the rest private, task-specific modules, small private parts each task keeps besides the shared trunk) and by task grouping (which tasks should share a trunk at all, so tasks that fight can be split into groups with separate trunks).

Optimization methods decide how well I use the sharing I chose; they cannot choose it for me. When a multitask setup is failing, my first question is no longer which optimizer to try. It is whether the tasks belong together and whether the trunk has room for them. The optimizer is the second question, not the first.

When the diagnosis points at the training itself, the fixes live on the method pages: the introduction's summary, Three diseases, and the pages that fix them, lines them all up side by side.
