k-Nearest Neighbors (k-NN)

The classifier that trains nothing.

k-nearest neighbors: the classifier that trains nothing.

The laziest classifier that works

What if you skipped training entirely? No weights, no loss function, no gradient descent, no 1000 epochs. Nothing to fit.

You move to a new street and want to know which bakery is best. You could build a statistical model of bakeries. Or you could ask your five nearest neighbors and go with the majority. kNN is the second option, formalized: to classify a new point, find the $k$ training points closest to it and take a majority vote.

It is the laziest classifier that works. "Training" is just memorizing the dataset, the model is the data, and all the thinking happens at query time. That laziness is also its charm: there is nothing to tune except $k$, and as you are about to see, that one knob is the entire bias-variance tradeoff made visible.

How it works: distance, then a vote

Two ingredients. First, a way to say who the neighbors are:

$$ d(x, q) = \lVert x - q \rVert $$ $$ = \sqrt{(x_1-q_1)^2 + (x_2-q_2)^2} $$

Read it term by term. $q$ is the query point, the new stranger. $x$ is a training point. $x_1 - q_1$ is how far apart they are along feature 1; square it, do the same for feature 2, add, square root. In 2D it is the hypotenuse you met in school; in $n$ dimensions it is the same idea with more terms. Check it: $q = (0, 0)$, $x = (3, 4)$. Then $d = \sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{25} =$ 5.

Second, the vote:

$$ \hat{y}(q) = \text{the majority label of } k \text{ nearest} $$

Walk it through. $k = 5$. The 5 nearest points to the query have labels blue, blue, orange, blue, orange. Blue wins 3 to 2, so the query is blue. That is the whole algorithm: sort by distance, take $k$, count. ($k$ is usually odd, which is not a coincidence. It dodges ties.)

Gotcha, and it is a good one: kNN has no training step, so overfitting cannot show up as a loss curve doing something funny. It shows up at query time, as a jagged boundary. Small $k$ listens to every neighbor, including the noisy ones, so the boundary wraps itself around individual points. Big $k$ averages the noise away and the boundary goes smooth. The toy below is one slider, $k$, and that sentence is everything it does.

How to play

  • The point: One slider, k, morphs the boundary from jagged overfit to smooth. Bias and variance you can see.
  • What you'll see: The plot opens uncomputed: bare points and the query diamond, no boundary. On Play, k sweeps 1 to 25 in 25 steps with the boundary contouring live at every step: spiky islands wrapped around individual points melt into big smooth regions, and the query diamond's vote readout flips and settles as its electorate grows.
  • k (neighbors who vote): Drag it by hand and watch the boundary re-contour a beat later. Try parking at 3 and counting the jagged inlets around single points, then drag to 25 and watch them melt into smooth blobs.
  • Click to move the query: Click anywhere on the plot and the diamond jumps there; its k nearest neighbors light up with lines and the readout shows the vote split. Try the contested ground between two clusters and watch a small k and a large k disagree.
  • Reset: Clears the boundary back to the uncomputed start: bare points, the query diamond back on its contested ground, k back at 17. Try it after a sweep and watch the regions vanish so you can classify again.

What happens: bias and variance, visible

At $k = 3$ the boundary is jagged: little inlets and islands wrapped around individual points, including the noisy ones sitting in the wrong cluster. The notebook's own comment on this exact plot was "smaller k more overfitting," and now you can see what it meant. With 3 votes, one noisy neighbor swings the verdict.

At $k = 17$ the boundary is smooth: big clean regions, the noise averaged out. Same data, same distance, same vote. The only thing that changed is how many neighbors get a say.

This is the bias-variance tradeoff with the math removed. Small $k$: low bias, because the boundary can bend into any shape the data suggests, but high variance, because the shape wobbles with every noisy point. Large $k$: high bias, because the boundary can only draw smooth blobs, but low variance, because one weird point cannot move it. The slider is the tradeoff, and the boundary is the receipt.

For the record, the notebook's setup: make_classification with 3000 points, 2 features, 2 clusters per class, a 90/10 train/test split, and the two plots at $k = 17$ (smooth) and $k = 3$ (jagged overfit). The toy uses the same recipe with 800 points so every step re-contours live.

What's next: another way to vote

Recap: kNN classifies by asking nearby points to vote. Distance is Euclidean, the verdict is the majority, and $k$ dials the boundary from jagged to smooth. No training, no weights, just stored data and a vote at query time.
