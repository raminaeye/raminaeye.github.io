AdamW: Decoupled Weight Decay for Adam

Why L2 regularization and weight decay are the same for SGD but not for Adam, and how AdamW decays every weight at the same rate.

1 What AdamW fixes

Weight decay (Hanson and Pratt 1988) pulls every weight a little toward zero on each step, a standard way to keep a large model from overfitting (Regularization and Data Augmentation). For years Adam (Kingma and Ba 2015) implementations did this by adding an L2 penalty to the loss, because for SGD the two are the same thing. Loshchilov and Hutter (2019) showed that under Adam they are not: the penalty passes through Adam’s per-weight divide, so weights with large gradients are barely regularized. Their fix, decoupled weight decay, is AdamW.

AdamW is the standard optimizer for training transformers and large language models: LLaMA trains with AdamW and weight decay 0.1 (Touvron and others 2023), and most vision transformers and diffusion models use it too.

2 Adam’s divide, in one line

Adam’s step, from Adam, section 6
\({\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\theta_t} = \htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\theta_{t-1}} - \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{\alpha}\,\frac{\htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{\hat m_t}}{\sqrt{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\hat v_t}} + \epsilon}}\)
Adam divides every weight’s step by \(\sqrt{\hat v_t}\), that weight’s recent typical gradient size. Whatever enters the gradient \(g_t\) passes through that divide. \(\hat m_t\) is the bias-corrected direction average (Adam, section 5).

3 Two ways to decay, and why they split under Adam

The card walks from the two definitions to the point where Adam separates them.

Two ways to pull weights toward zero
\({\displaystyle \begin{aligned} \htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\theta_{t+1}} &= (1-\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\lambda})\,\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\theta_t} \\ &\quad - \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{\alpha}\,\nabla f_t(\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\theta_t}) \end{aligned}}\)
Weight decay: every step, shrink each weight by the small fraction \(\lambda\) (lambda), then take the ordinary gradient step on the loss \(f_t\). Large weights shrink more in absolute size, so the model is pushed toward small weights, which usually generalize better.
\({\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#c0392b}{g_t} = \nabla f_t(\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\theta_{t-1}}) + \htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\lambda'}\,\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\theta_{t-1}}}\)
L2 regularization: add \(\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda'\lVert\theta\rVert^{2}\) to the loss instead. Its gradient is \(\lambda'\theta\), so the gradient the optimizer sees is the loss gradient plus a pull toward zero (Regularization and Data Augmentation).
\({\displaystyle \begin{aligned} \htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\theta_t} &= \htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\theta_{t-1}} - \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{\alpha}\,\htmlStyle{color:#c0392b}{g_t} \\ &= (1-\htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{\alpha}\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\lambda'})\,\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\theta_{t-1}} \\ &\quad - \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{\alpha}\,\nabla f_t(\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\theta_{t-1}}) \\ \htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\lambda'} &= \frac{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\lambda}}{\htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{\alpha}} \end{aligned}}\)
For plain SGD the two are the same: multiply out the step and the L2 pull becomes a shrink by \(\alpha\lambda'\). Choosing \(\lambda'=\lambda/\alpha\) gives exactly weight decay. This is why the two names were used for one thing.
\({\displaystyle \begin{aligned} \htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\theta_t} &= \htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\theta_{t-1}} - \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{\alpha}\,\frac{\htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{\hat m_t}}{\sqrt{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\hat v_t}} + \epsilon} \\ &\text{with } \htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\lambda'}\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\theta}\text{ inside } \htmlStyle{color:#c0392b}{g_t} \end{aligned}}\)
For Adam they differ. The L2 pull is inside \(g_t\), so it is averaged into \(\hat m_t\) and then divided by \(\sqrt{\hat v_t}\): a weight with large gradients gets its pull divided by a large number and is barely decayed, while a weight with small gradients is decayed hard. The regularization now depends on gradient history, not on the weight’s size.
\({\displaystyle \begin{aligned} \htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\theta_t} &= \htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\theta_{t-1}} - \eta_t\,\htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{\alpha}\,\frac{\htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{\hat m_t}}{\sqrt{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\hat v_t}} + \epsilon} \\ &\quad - \eta_t\,\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\lambda}\,\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\theta_{t-1}} \end{aligned}}\)
AdamW (Loshchilov and Hutter 2019) takes the decay out of the gradient. Adam steps on the loss alone, and the decay \(\lambda\theta\) is applied directly, outside the divide, so every weight shrinks at the same rate. \(\eta_t\) (eta) is the learning-rate schedule’s multiplier, which scales both parts together.

7 Decay and the schedule

The decay in PyTorch’s form, and a decay that does not depend on run length
\({\displaystyle \begin{aligned} \htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\theta_t} &= \htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\theta_{t-1}} - \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{\alpha_t}\,\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\mathrm{wd}}\,\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\theta_{t-1}} \\ &\quad - \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{\alpha_t}\,\frac{\htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{\hat m_t}}{\sqrt{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\hat v_t}} + \epsilon} \end{aligned}}\)
PyTorch’s AdamW multiplies its weight_decay setting, written \(\mathrm{wd}\) here, by the scheduled learning rate \(\alpha_t\), so the decay follows the schedule: strong while the rate is high and fading as it decays. This matches the paper’s \(\eta_t\lambda\), so \(\mathrm{wd}\) is the paper’s \(\lambda\) divided by the base rate.
\({\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\lambda} = \htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\lambda_{\text{norm}}}\,\sqrt{\frac{b}{B\,T}}}\)
Loshchilov and Hutter also propose normalized weight decay: the best \(\lambda\) gets smaller when a run takes more weight updates, so they set it from a normalized \(\lambda_{\text{norm}}\) using the batch size \(b\), the number of training examples \(B\) and the number of epochs \(T\). Fewer, larger batches mean fewer updates, so each update can decay more.

So PyTorch’s default weight_decay of 1e-2 gives a per-step shrink of only the learning rate times 1e-2. The practical rule that follows: when you change the learning rate, the decay changes with it, so retune them together (Learning Rate Schedules).

8 When to use it

Use AdamW whenever you want weight decay with an adaptive optimizer: transformers, large language models, vision transformers and diffusion models all train this way. Plain Adam with its weight_decay argument is almost never what you want, because that is the L2 penalty of section 3. Without any decay, AdamW and Adam are the same optimizer. For SGD the two forms are equivalent, so the choice only matters for adaptive optimizers.

9 When it runs and what it carries

AdamW runs once per training step, after backward(), and carries exactly Adam’s state: exp_avg, exp_avg_sq and step per weight tensor (Adam, section 12). The decay itself has no state. Each parameter group can carry its own weight_decay, which is how biases and normalization gains are left undecayed.

10 AdamW in PyTorch

The two constructors look alike; only where the decay acts differs.

import torch
from torch.optim import Adam
from torch.optim import AdamW
model = torch.nn.Linear(10, 1)
p = list(model.parameters())
lr, wd = 1e-3, 1e-2  # α, wd
decay = dict(weight_decay=wd)
# Adam: wd·θ joins the grad
adam = Adam(p, lr, **decay)
# AdamW: decay outside it
adamw = AdamW(p, lr, **decay)
  1. 1A small model, the learning rate \(\alpha\) and the decay setting \(\mathrm{wd}\).
  2. 2Adam’s weight_decay is L2: it adds \(\mathrm{wd}\,\theta\) to the gradient, the \(\lambda'\theta\) of the card in section 3, which Adam then divides.
  3. 3AdamW’s weight_decay is decoupled: the step \(\alpha_t\,\mathrm{wd}\,\theta\) outside the divide.

Large-model recipes decay only the weight matrices. The settings here are LLaMA-like settings.

import torch
model = torch.nn.TransformerEncoderLayer(512, 8)
decay, no_decay = [], []
for name, p in model.named_parameters():
    (no_decay if p.ndim < 2 else decay).append(p)   # biases, norm gains
opt = torch.optim.AdamW(
    [{"params": decay, "weight_decay": 0.1},
     {"params": no_decay, "weight_decay": 0.0}],
    lr=3e-4, betas=(0.9, 0.95))
  1. 1One transformer layer as the model.
  2. 2Split parameters: matrices are decayed; biases and normalization gains, which are vectors, are not.
  3. 3Two parameter groups with their own decay, and LLaMA-like betas.

11 Failure modes and tips

Most AdamW trouble is about where the decay goes and how big it is.
symptomcausefix
regularization does nothing on busy weightsAdam(weight_decay=...) used, which is L2switch to AdamW
the model underfits after a learning-rate changePyTorch’s decay is scaled by the rate, so the effective decay moved tooretune weight_decay with the rate
norm layers or biases drift to zerodecay applied to every parameterput them in a group with weight_decay zero
the best decay changes with run lengthmore updates compound the same per-step shrinkscale it with the normalized decay in the card above
