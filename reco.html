Recommender Systems

People who liked this.

What does "people who liked this also liked that" actually mean? It means taste has a shape. Put every movie as a point in user space: two movies are close if the same users rated them the same way. Or put every movie as a point in genre space: two movies are close if they share genres. Either way, "recommend" becomes "find the nearest neighbors." That is all a recommender is: geometry plus a similarity score.

My notebook (in the Machine-Learning-Concepts repo) tries three geometries on MovieLens, a public collection of movie ratings: similarity from ratings, similarity from genres, and learned embeddings, short lists of numbers learned per user and movie. They do not always agree, and the disagreements are the interesting part.

Three ways to say similar

Way one: similar raters. Compare two movie columns of the user-by-movie rating matrix R, zeros where nobody rated. Way two: similar content. Compare two binary genre vectors from the genre matrix G, 1 if the movie has the genre and 0 if not. Both use the same score, cosine similarity:

Cosine similarity: how far two movie vectors point the same way.
cos(a, b) = a · b‖a‖ · ‖b‖
a and b are two movies' vectors (rating columns or genre bits). The top is their dot product: multiply matching entries and add. The bottom divides by both lengths, so the score reads only direction, never size: 1 means they point the same way.
import numpy as np

def cosine(a, b):
    return a @ b / (np.linalg.norm(a) * np.linalg.norm(b))

# R columns and G rows are indexed differently, so each has its own index
cosine(R[:, ts_col], R[:, ts2_col])  # way one, ratings: 0.573
cosine(G[ts_row], G[ts2_row])        # way two, genres: 1.0 (all five shared)

On the notebook's data, Toy Story 2 is Toy Story's closest match by way one. The notebook calls it "content-based," but no content is involved, just who rated what: it is collaborative filtering in a content-based costume. Way two is the real content-based method.

Way three: learn the geometry. Instead of hand-picking the vectors, learn them. Every user and every movie gets a 20-number embedding plus one learned bias number, trained by gradient descent so that a dot product plus biases predicts the rating.

The rating table is mostly empty

A real ratings table has thousands of users and movies, and each user rated a handful, so almost every cell is empty. "Recommend" means guessing those empty cells.

Cosine struggles here: two movies often share no raters at all, and then the comparison says nothing. What you want is a model that learns from the ratings that exist and generalizes to the ones that do not.

Learn the taste vectors

Matrix factorization gives every user a short taste vector and every movie a short vibe vector, plus a user bias and an item bias.

The one line that runs this whole section: predict a rating.
r̂ui = pu · qi + bu + bi
The predicted rating r̂ui of user u for movie i is the dot product of taste pu and vibe qi (high when they line up), plus bu, because some people rate everything high, plus bi, because some movies are just widely loved.

Nothing is hand-picked: gradient descent fits all the vectors and biases to the ratings you have. This is exactly the notebook's forward():

def forward(self, user_ids, item_ids):
    user_embeds = self.user_embed(user_ids)
    item_embeds = self.item_embed(item_ids)
    user_bias = self.user_bias(user_ids)
    item_bias = self.item_bias(item_ids)
    dot_product = (user_embeds * item_embeds).sum(dim=1)
    return dot_product + user_bias.squeeze() + item_bias.squeeze()

The two thin tables are the whole model: a short row per user and per movie (4 numbers here, 20 in the notebook) stands in for the full table.

Comparing users directly stops working when the table is empty. The fix that stuck: learn short vectors for users and movies, and predict ratings with dot products (Koren et al.).

Score one rating, step by step

Here is one prediction, step by step: user 608 and Toy Story, which user 608 rated 2.5 stars. Four steps, no similarity search. The vectors are a toy with 4 numbers each; the notebook learns 20 per user and movie.

One predicted rating in one line: the user's taste dotted with the movie's vibe, plus both biases.
r̂ui = pu · qi + bu + bi
Predict the rating: dot the user's taste vector with the movie's vibe vector, then adjust for how generous the user is and how popular the movie is.
pu the user's taste vectorqi the movie's vibe vector· the dot product: their affinitybu the user's rating habitbi the movie's general appealr̂ui the predicted rating

No other user or movie is consulted: the prediction is built from this user's and this movie's own learned numbers.

Factorization fills the blanks

Factorization fills a blank by prediction, not lookup: the one line from Learn the taste vectors, applied to a pair that never met. Recommending is ranking one user's blanks:

# P: users x 20 taste vectors, Q: movies x 20 vibe vectors, b_u, b_i: biases
scores = Q @ P[u] + b_u[u] + b_i         # predicted rating for every movie
scores[R[u] > 0] = -np.inf              # skip the movies u already rated
recommend = np.argsort(-scores)[:k]      # top k blanks, highest first

Two honest similarities disagree

The toy holds one movie fixed and builds two top-5 lists for it side by side, one from rating-cosine (way one) and one from genre-cosine (way two), live from a real 8-movie by 6-user slice of the notebook's MovieLens data. The slice is small, so its cosines differ from the full data (Toy Story to Toy Story 2 reads 0.997 here); what carries over is the disagreement. Entries in only one list get an only here marker.

The hybrid adds the content back

The learned model and the genre similarity capture different things, so the notebook combines them: a learned model plus the genre-cosine score as an extra signal. Learned taste plus stated content.

The scoreboard is RMSE, scored on ratings held out of training. One rating in five is held out, and every user keeps the rest in training:

Root mean squared error: the typical miss, in stars.
RMSE = √1N Σu,i (r̂ui − rui)²
Each error, prediction r̂ui minus true rating rui, is squared so big misses hurt more, averaged over the N held-out ratings, then square-rooted back to rating units. Lower is better. Without the root it is the MSE training loss.

The setup in code, on MovieLens small-latest, as in the notebook. One epoch is one pass over the rated cells in shuffled batches of 128, and Adam is a gradient descent method that adapts each weight's step size.

df = movies.merge(ratings, on="movieId")       # movies.csv + ratings.csv
R = torch.tensor(df.pivot_table(index="userId", columns="title", values="rating")
                 .fillna(0).to_numpy(), dtype=torch.float32)  # blanks = 0
u, i = torch.where(R > 0)                       # every rated cell: real user and movie index
test = torch.rand(len(u)) < 0.2                 # hold out 1 rating in 5, not whole users
loader = DataLoader(TensorDataset(u[~test], i[~test], R[u[~test], i[~test]]),
                    batch_size=128, shuffle=True)  # train on the other 4 in 5
opt = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=0.001)  # model: forward() above, dim 20
loss_fn = torch.nn.MSELoss()
for epoch in range(50):
    for bu, bi, br in loader:
        loss = loss_fn(model(bu, bi), br)
        opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()

The hybrid trains its own fresh model and optimizer for 20 full-batch steps. The notebook's earlier split held out whole users and then scored them with other users' vectors, so its old RMSE numbers did not measure the model. They are gone until the fixed notebook is rerun.

When it helps and when it does not

Reach for it when you have plenty of ratings per user and per movie. The vectors are only as good as the ratings that trained them.

Keep in mind the cold start: a new user or a new movie has no ratings, so it has no vector, and the model has nothing to say. Genre similarity still works on day one: a new movie arrives with its genres attached.

And the item bias is honest about a real skew: widely loved movies get recommended to everyone. If you want surprising picks instead of popular ones, the raw prediction needs re-ranking: sorting the list again by a criterion that favors surprising picks.

Taste has a shape, and the ratings are enough to learn it: two short vectors, one dot product, two biases.
