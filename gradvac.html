GradVac

Nudge each task pair back to its own healthy level of cooperation, not to zero.

PCGrad cuts every fight to exactly zero. GradVac asks whether zero is the right target: each pair of tasks gets its own target cosine, learned from how that pair usually cooperates, and sagging pairs are nudged back up to it.

Is zero the right target?

Surgery is all-or-nothing: it either cuts the whole shadow or leaves the pair untouched. But there is a quieter assumption hiding inside the rule, and I did not notice it at first. Read the rule again: if the cosine is negative, project until it is zero. Every pair gets the same target: zero. No questions asked. The rule never asks what the pair's relationship was before the fight, or whether these two tasks are friends or strangers.

That is a strange thing to assume about a hundred tasks of any kind. Some task pairs genuinely cooperate, and forcing them to zero would not fix a fight, it would destroy a friendship. The question is whether we can tell the difference, and the answer turns out to be yes, if we keep a little memory of what each pair normally looks like.

Here is the intuition I keep coming back to. Surgery happens after the fight breaks out. A vaccine holds each pair at its healthy level so the fight never starts. That is where the name comes from: Gradient Vaccine, GradVac. And it turns out PCGrad is literally the special case where the target is zero for every pair: set every pair's target to zero and GradVac's nudge becomes PCGrad's cut. The tool below shows that crossover live.

The paper behind the vaccine

The paper

Zirui Wang, Yulia Tsvetkov, Orhan Firat, Yuan Cao

Gradient Vaccine: Investigating and Improving Multi-task Optimization in Massively Multilingual Models.

ICLR 2021 · arXiv:2010.05874

The authors were working at a different scale of multi-task learning: one model handling tens or hundreds of languages, like multilingual translation or multilingual BERT. The appeal is real: one model is far cheaper to train and deploy than one per language, and high-resource languages can transfer knowledge to low-resource ones.

But nobody was really studying how these models optimize. The standard practice was to train them monolithically: one language-agnostic objective over the concatenated corpus of every language, as if task relatedness did not matter. The authors argue this ignores how languages interact and can induce negative interference, and it leaves the whole optimization process a black box: you cannot see which languages help each other and which fight.

So they set out to open the black box through the geometry of the loss. They asked three questions, paraphrased from the introduction: do typologically similar languages enjoy more similar loss geometries? If so, does a more similar gradient trajectory mean less interference and better model quality? And can we deliberately encourage more geometrically aligned updates? The answers were yes, and that is what the rest of this page is built on.

What the authors measured first

Before fixing anything, the authors recorded what PCGrad never looks at: the cosine between every pair of task gradients, checkpoint by checkpoint, over entire training runs. Three things showed up.

  1. The pattern of cosines mirrors the language family tree. Romance languages align with each other, Slavic with Slavic. You can see the clusters with the naked eye.
  2. Alignment pays. In controlled two-language models, higher gradient cosine went hand in hand with better translation quality.
  3. Alignment is not one fixed number. It changes from layer to layer and drifts over training.

So every task pair has its own natural level of cooperation: strongly positive for related tasks, near zero for distant ones. French and Spanish's gradients naturally agree, so PCGrad's alarm never goes off. And if it did, its target of zero would be a step backwards, blocking the very cooperation that lets related languages help each other. PCGrad can stop a fight, but it cannot notice that two friends have started cooperating less than they used to. Sure enough, PCGrad did nothing on most steps of multilingual training: no cosines were negative, yet a lot of help was being left on the table.

Each pair gets its own target

The fix: keep a running estimate of what "normal" looks like for each pair, a slow-moving average of the cosines seen so far, tracked separately for each group of layers. Call the current cosine \(\phi_{ij}\) and the pair's target \(\hat{\phi}_{ij}\):

\[ \hat{\phi}_{ij}(t) = (1 - \beta) \cdot \hat{\phi}_{ij}(t-1) + \beta \cdot \phi_{ij}(t) \]

Every piece: \(\hat{\phi}_{ij}(t)\) is the pair's target after batch \(t\), the "normal" this pair is held to. \(\phi_{ij}(t)\) is the cosine measured on this batch. \(\beta \approx 10^{-2}\) is how fast the target learns: each batch moves the target only \(\beta\) of the way toward the new measurement, so one noisy batch cannot yank it around.

Whenever the current cosine dips below this target, GradVac nudges \(\mathbf{g}_i\) toward \(\mathbf{g}_j\) by just enough to bring the pair back to normal: mix in a little of the other gradient, \(\mathbf{g}_i' = \mathbf{g}_i + a \cdot \mathbf{g}_j\), with \(a\) solved so the new cosine equals \(\hat{\phi}_{ij}\) exactly. If the pair is as aligned as usual, \(a = 0\) and nothing happens. If alignment starts to sag, the early warning sign of a fight that has not happened yet, \(\mathbf{g}_i\) gets a small dose of the other task's gradient, pulling the pair back before the cosine can turn negative.

How big should the dose \(a\) be? Just big enough: GradVac picks the smallest \(a\) that brings the pair exactly back to its target, no more. The paper works out that dose in closed form:

\[ \mathbf{g}_i' = \mathbf{g}_i + \frac{\|\mathbf{g}_i\| \left(\phi^T_{ij} \sqrt{1 - \phi_{ij}^2} - \phi_{ij} \sqrt{1 - (\phi^T_{ij})^2}\right)}{\|\mathbf{g}_j\| \sqrt{1 - (\phi^T_{ij})^2}} \cdot \mathbf{g}_j \]

Term by term: \(\phi^T_{ij}\) is the target (my \(\hat{\phi}_{ij}\) above), where the pair should sit. \(\phi_{ij}\) is the current cosine, where the pair sits now. \(\|\mathbf{g}_i\|\) and \(\|\mathbf{g}_j\|\) scale the dose to the arrows' sizes. The bracket in the numerator measures the sag: when the current cosine equals the target, it collapses to zero, so \(a = 0\) and nothing happens. Read it aloud with real numbers, one computation per line, using the demo's defaults below:

  1. Directions: \(\mathbf{g}_1 = (2.2, 0.6)\) points at \(15.3^{\circ}\). \(\mathbf{g}_2\) sits \(115^{\circ}\) further around, at \(130.3^{\circ}\), with length \(1.8\): \(\mathbf{g}_2 = 1.8 \cdot (\cos 130.3^{\circ}, \sin 130.3^{\circ}) = (-1.163, 1.374)\).
  2. Lengths: \(\|\mathbf{g}_1\| = \sqrt{2.2^2 + 0.6^2} = \sqrt{5.2} \approx 2.280\). \(\|\mathbf{g}_2\| = 1.8\).
  3. Dot product: \(2.2 \cdot (-1.163) + 0.6 \cdot 1.374 = -2.559 + 0.824 \approx -1.735\).
  4. Cosine: \(-1.735 / (2.280 \cdot 1.8) = -1.735 / 4.104 \approx -0.423\). (Shortcut: rotating one vector by \(115^{\circ}\) gives \(\cos 115^{\circ} \approx -0.423\) directly.)
  5. The pair's target is \(0.30\). Since \(-0.423 < 0.30\), the pair is sagging: GradVac acts. Plug into the dose formula: \(a \approx 0.896\).
  6. Nudge: \(\mathbf{g}_1' = \mathbf{g}_1 + 0.896 \cdot \mathbf{g}_2\). Check: \(\cos(\mathbf{g}_1', \mathbf{g}_2) = 0.300\), exactly the pair's own target, not zero.
  7. The PCGrad check: drag the target down to \(0\) in the tool below and the same pair gets \(a = 0.535\), the exact projection PCGrad applies. Target zero is surgery; any other target is the vaccine.

For the curious: where the dose comes from. Mixing \(a \cdot \mathbf{g}_j\) into \(\mathbf{g}_i\) rotates \(\mathbf{g}_i\) toward \(\mathbf{g}_j\) inside the plane the two gradients span. The Law of Sines in that plane gives the exact \(a\) that lands the angle on the target, no iteration needed.

The algorithm, per training step

One step of GradVac, exactly as the paper describes it:

  1. Forward pass; compute each task loss \(L_i\).
  2. Compute each task gradient on the shared parameters, \(\mathbf{g}_i\).
  3. For each pair \((i, j)\), measure the current cosine \(\phi_{ij}\) and update its running target: \(\hat{\phi}_{ij} \leftarrow (1 - \beta)\hat{\phi}_{ij} + \beta\phi_{ij}\).
  4. For each pair below its target, nudge \(\mathbf{g}_i\) toward \(\mathbf{g}_j\) by adding just enough of \(\mathbf{g}_j\) to bring the pair back: \(\mathbf{g}_i \leftarrow \mathbf{g}_i + a \cdot \mathbf{g}_j\).
  5. Step the network with the sum of the edited gradients.
# grads = [g_1..g_K]; target[i,j] = running target cosine, init 0
for i, j in pairs:
    cos_ij = grads[i] @ grads[j] / (norm(grads[i]) * norm(grads[j]))
    target[i,j] = (1 - beta) * target[i,j] + beta * cos_ij  # slow running target
    if cos_ij < target[i,j]:                # fighting below their normal?
        a = solve_for_cosine(grads[i], grads[j], target[i,j])  # restoring blend
        grads[i] = grads[i] + a * grads[j]  # nudge, not a full cut
d = sum(grads)                              # combine the edited gradients

Does it pay?

On small-scale WMT translation, GradVac beat the plain baseline and all three of GradNorm, MGDA, and PCGrad: on average and on every single language, which multitask methods rarely manage. On a 25-language-pair production benchmark the gains held up and were largest exactly where interference bites hardest: the high-resource languages. It also improved multilingual BERT on named-entity recognition.

And there is a free side product I keep coming back to: the pairwise-cosine log itself costs a few lines of code and draws me a map of how my tasks relate while I train, which is why the Field Guide puts it on the diagnose-first checklist. Even if you never vaccinate a single pair, watching the cosines is worth it.

The knobs

Three, and the paper's defaults all worked well. The averaging speed \(\beta\) (\(10^{-2}\) was best): slow enough that one noisy batch cannot yank a target around, fast enough to follow real drift over training. Which tasks to vaccinate: only the low-resource ones, since they have the most to gain from borrowed alignment. And granularity: one target per layer group beat one target for the whole model, matching the finding that alignment varies by layer.

Practical notes

  1. Reach for surgery when your task gradients often fight (negative cosines). Log the cosines first to check. Reach for the vaccine when fights are rare but cooperation is uneven: some pairs help each other far more than others. Both work with any model and wrap around any optimizer. Surgery has no hyperparameters at all.
  2. Costs: both need K backward passes and K stored gradients per step. With many tasks that is most of your budget. The fix is the subsampling trick used by CAGrad-Fast: apply the cuts or nudges to a random handful of tasks per step. GradVac additionally stores one running target per pair per layer group, which is tiny next to the gradients.
  3. Keep in mind: surgery's answer depends on the cut order, hence the shuffle. The edited update can be shorter than the original, slowing things when fights are rare. And like MGDA, surgery gives you no say over which trade-off it lands on. The vaccine's targets need a warm-up: early in training the running averages have seen nothing yet, so do not trust the first few hundred steps of "normal".
  4. Sum or average? The PCGrad paper sums. Averaging gives the same direction at a different size, which your learning rate absorbs either way. And on steps where no pair fights, every cut is skipped and surgery is exactly plain gradient descent: late in training, when tasks settle into agreement, that is most steps.

Direction is handled softly now: each pair is nudged back to its own healthy level, never forced to a universal zero. But nothing on this page promises anything about the task that ends up worst off. What if, instead of nudging pairs back to normal, we demanded a guarantee on the worst task? That is the next page.
