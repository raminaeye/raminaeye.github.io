Self-Supervised Learning Failure Modes and Checks

Most methods in this series train an encoder to map views (edited copies) of one photo to matching embeddings, and masked modeling rebuilds hidden patches (Pretext tasks); each fails whenever an easier answer exists.

Overlapping receptive fields let the network cheat

A receptive field is the part of the input an output sees. If two overlap or touch, the network can match shared pixels, such as a continuing edge, and learn no content.

The patch-position task needed a gap, jitter and a color fix

Doersch et al. (2015) gave a network two 96 px patches and asked where the second sat relative to the first, one of eight neighboring positions, each patch through its own copy of the network. Three shortcuts appeared:

  • Edges crossing the border: fixed by a 48 px gap.
  • Lines lining up across the gap: fixed by jitter, a random shift of up to 7 px.
  • Chromatic aberration, a lens's color fringe that gives away position: fixed by removing color cues.
overlapgap
gap used by Doersch (half a patch)

Only a wide gap plus jitter leaves content as the clue (toy, after Doersch 2015).

Predicting further ahead leaves the overlap behind

CPC (Contrastive Predictive Coding, van den Oord et al. 2018) predicts future steps of speech audio. The nearest steps share input with the current one, and predicting only the nearest steps exploits the signal's local smoothness.

Crops and masks have their own low-level cues

SimCLR's overlapping crops share a color histogram, so it adds color distortion (augmentations); a few masked patches are easy to fill from neighbors, so MAE hides 75% (Masked Image Modeling).

Collapse squeezes embeddings to a point or a slice

Complete collapse maps every input to one embedding. Dimensional collapse uses only a few directions (Jing et al. 2022): the loss looks fine, but a linear probe scores poorly.

Negatives do not prevent it: SimCLR left several of its 128 directions unused. The batch covariance C shows how embedding numbers vary together; a near-zero singular value (spread along one main direction) marks an unused direction.

The covariance of a batch of embeddings; its near-zero singular values reveal unused directions.
C = 1N Σi=1N (zi − z̄)(zi − z̄)T
zi is image i's embedding, z̄ (z bar) the batch mean and N the batch size; the superscript T turns the column into a row, so each term is a square table.

Batch size, temperature and augmentation decide success

SimCLR's negatives (images pushed away) come from its batch; its temperature τ sets how sharply the loss focuses on the closest ones.

What to do about each setting.
SettingRule
Batch sizeSmall batch, few negatives: use a queue (MoCo) or no negatives.
TemperatureTune it: best was 0.1 in SimCLR and 0.2 in MoCo v2 (MoCo used 0.07 by default).
AugmentationToo weak lets shortcuts in (crops alone: BYOL 72.5% to 59.4%, SimCLR 67.9% to 40.3%); too strong causes dimensional collapse.
Non-image dataAugmentations must keep the sample's identity; in my experience most failed projects fail here.

BatchNorm can leak information across a batch

BatchNorm shares one mean and variance across the samples on a GPU (graphics processor), so MoCo "cheated" by matching views through it. MoCo shuffles keys across GPUs; SimCLR averages over all its TPU devices (SyncBatchNorm on GPUs).

A loss that drops too fast can mean a shortcut

A loss that falls fast while kNN accuracy (a vote of each image's nearest training images) stays flat signals a shortcut: SimSiam without stop-gradient hits its lowest loss at chance accuracy; MoCo without shuffled BatchNorm passes 99.9% on its own task as kNN drops.

A probe score is not the whole story

Fine-tuning (training the encoder too) can rank methods unlike a probe. MoCo v2 tried a projection head (a small multilayer perceptron, MLP) and extra blur, and scored the linear probe by ImageNet top-1 and detection (finding and boxing objects) on PASCAL VOC, a standard photo benchmark, by AP50, average precision counting a box as found when it overlaps the true box by at least half:

Settings that usually work

  • With only about 10k images, label them instead (my rule of thumb).
  • Probe the encoder, not the projection head.
  • Compare methods under one protocol, such as DINO's 20-neighbor vote, which needs no tuning.

What I watch during training

Five cheap checks catch most failures early; two come from Wang and Isola: alignment (how close positive pairs sit) and uniformity (how evenly embeddings spread).

All five in one listing; alignment and uniformity follow Wang and Isola's code:

import torch, torch.nn.functional as F
Z = F.normalize(encoder(x), dim=1)                     # [n, d] one batch
spread = Z.std(dim=0).mean()                           # about 1/sqrt(d) healthy, near 0 collapsed
s = torch.linalg.svdvals(Z - Z.mean(0))                # spread along each direction
eff_rank = torch.exp(torch.special.entr(s / s.sum()).sum())  # 1 to d directions in use
z1, z2 = F.normalize(encoder(x1), dim=1), F.normalize(encoder(x2), dim=1)  # two views
align = (z1 - z2).norm(dim=1).pow(2).mean()            # lower: positive pairs closer
uniform = torch.pdist(z1).pow(2).mul(-2).exp().mean().log()  # at most 0, lower: more even
bank = F.normalize(encoder(train_x), dim=1)            # [M, d] stored features
q = F.normalize(encoder(val_x), dim=1)                 # [m, d]
pred = torch.mode(train_y[(q @ bank.T).topk(20, dim=1).indices], dim=1).values
knn_acc = (pred == val_y).float().mean()
