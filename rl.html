Learning from rewards

Many tasks give no labelled answers, only a reward after each try, as in a game, a robot or a chat reply. How can a learner get better from those rewards alone? Learning from rewards like this is called reinforcement learning (RL).

The running example is my banana collector, built in the Unity game engine, a learner in a square arena that should gather yellow bananas and avoid blue ones.

Figure 1 shows the arena the whole page keeps coming back to: a square room, yellow bananas to gather, blue ones to avoid, and one agent learning by stepping. The yellows and blues are the only pay the agent ever hears about, so every idea on this page is about how a learner turns that pay into better moves. The agent starts far from the yellows, so its first moves are mostly guesses.

Once trained, the collector gathers yellows quickly and steps around blues.

Figure 2 is the road map for the whole series, so you can see where this page sits and what comes after it. This page is the first stop, the basics, and every later page teaches one piece of what this page sketches: how good a choice is, how to choose directly, how to handle amounts, how to learn the world, how several learners share one world, and how chat models learn from people. When a later section on this page names a method, its page is one chip on this map.

1 The reinforcement learning loop

The agent is the learner that picks the moves. A state is the agent’s situation right now; a move, or action, is one choice the agent can make in a state; a reward is the number the world hands back after each move, good or bad. Here the agent is the banana collector. It is paid for each yellow banana it eats and charged for each blue one, but it is not told in advance which steps pay; it finds out only by stepping.

RL, short for reinforcement learning, is learning which moves to make from rewards rather than from labelled answers. In the arena most steps meet no banana, so most steps bring no reward at all. The environment is everything outside the agent that responds to its moves and hands back a reward. A policy is the agent’s rule for choosing a move in each state; it may always pick the same move or pick by chance. The collector sees the bananas around it and must decide which way to step from each spot.

The world can be random too, so the same move in the same state does not always lead to the same next state or reward. New bananas drop in new places, so the collector’s path changes from one episode to the next.

Figure 3 shows the loop the agent repeats for its whole training: the agent picks a move, the world answers with a next state and a reward, and the reward teaches the agent. The two arrows matter more than the two boxes, because the loop is the whole method: everything on this page is one of those arrows made precise. Each time round, the agent's guesses about its spots get a little better, which is the learning the rest of the page explains.

2 The banana collector environment

A discrete action is one move from a fixed short list. An episode is one run from the start until it ends. Here an episode is one run of the arena until its clock runs out. The collector reads the arena through a list of sensor readings, one list per step.

The banana collector's world is small. It sees the arena only through 37 sensor readings and chooses from only four moves, so every score on this page is about those readings and those moves. The pay is just as small, a gain for a yellow banana and a loss for a blue one, and the whole method is about turning that tiny pay into good play. The settings sit in Table 2.

The loop in code starts with a policy that picks every move by chance.

3 Why copying an expert is not enough

Behaviour cloning is copying an expert’s recorded moves as labels and training on them. The expert’s recordings show only a few routes through the arena, and the rest of it stays unseen. Language models are first trained this way, by copying example replies, before reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) improves them, as Aligning Language Models with RL shows. In the arena no one has recorded which step is best from each spot, so the collector has only its rewards to learn from.

Figure 4 shows why copying an expert cannot teach this collector: supervised learning needs a right answer for every step, and here nobody recorded one. The top half needs labels the arena never had; the bottom half is all the collector gets, a reward after each try. That gap is the reason the whole page exists: with no right answers to copy, the agent has to learn from rewards alone.

4 Why RL is hard

A trajectory is the states, moves and rewards of one episode, in order. The return is all the reward still to come, with later rewards counted a little less. A yellow banana sits in the open while another hides behind a blue one, so the collector must choose which to head for first.

Figure 5 shows why a reward that arrives later is worth less than one that arrives now: the bars under the steps fade the further ahead they are. The collector has to choose between a banana now and a better route later, so it needs one number that weighs near and far rewards together. That number is the return, and Equation 1 writes it down.

Equation 1. The collector has to choose between a banana now and a better route later. A delivery robot choosing between a quick drop-off now and a fuller route later faces the same choice, and the return is how it weighs the two.

\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{G_t}} = \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#7d6608}{r_{t+1}}} + \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\gamma}}\, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#7d6608}{r_{t+2}}}\)
This line says G-t equals r-t-plus-one plus gamma times r-t-plus-two. G-t is the return from step t, the score for the run starting there: the banana rewards still to come, added up with the discount, plus 1 for each yellow banana the collector eats and minus 1 for each blue one, until the clock ends the episode. r-t-plus-one is the reward right after the move at step t, plus 1 if the collector just ate a yellow banana, minus 1 for a blue one, zero otherwise. gamma is the discount, the share of a reward's worth kept for each step of delay, between none and all. r-t-plus-two is the reward one step further on, shrunk once by the discount.
\(\displaystyle \qquad +\; \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\gamma^{2}}}\, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#7d6608}{r_{t+3}}} + \cdots\)
This line says the sum runs on: plus gamma-squared times r-t-plus-three, dot dot dot, to the end of the episode. In sum form, G-t equals the sum over k of gamma to the k times r-t-plus-k-plus-one. The sum runs k equals zero, one, two, dot dot dot, to the end of the episode, and k counts steps of delay, so k equals zero is the next reward at full worth. gamma-squared is the discount applied twice, once for each step of delay, so a reward three steps away keeps only a twice-shrunk share of its worth. r-t-plus-three is the reward three steps after the move, plus 1 for a yellow banana, minus 1 for a blue one, zero otherwise.
\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{G_t}} = \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#7d6608}{r_{t+1}}} + \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\gamma}}\, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{G_{t+1}}}\)
This line says G-t equals r-t-plus-one plus gamma times G-t-plus-one. G-t-plus-one is the return from the next step, the same rewards the first two lines add after their first reward, each with one fewer discount applied: the banana rewards still to come, plus 1 for each yellow banana and minus 1 for each blue one, until the clock ends the episode, and this one-step form is the form Equation 2 reuses to define a state's value. This shrinking is what the discount tool in Figure 6 implements. Together the three lines say one thing: the return is the next reward plus the discounted return from the step after, however far the episode runs.
Take the collector standing one step from a yellow banana, with a blue banana just past it. The return from that spot counts the yellow banana it is about to eat at full worth, then the blue banana after it shrunk once by the discount, then every banana after that shrunk once more per step, until the clock ends the episode.

Figure 6 shows the discount at work: each bar is how much a reward that many steps ahead still counts, and the bars shrink from left to right. The three buttons set the discount, small, middle or large, and the bars redraw each time; raising it keeps the later bars tall, so far rewards still matter, while lowering it makes them fade fast. That fading is what makes the collector prefer a banana now over a better route later, unless the discount is large enough to wait. The bars are the discount from Equation 1, applied once more for each step of delay.

Credit assignment is working out which earlier moves earned a reward that arrives much later. In the arena a yellow banana can be eaten many steps after the turn that led to it, and that turn still deserves the credit.

Exploration means trying moves to learn what they do; exploitation means playing the move that looks best so far. A collector that only repeats its first good path never finds the yellow cluster behind it.

5 Scoring states and moves with values

5.1 Values of states and moves

A spot is worth the return to expect from standing on it, and a move is worth the return to expect after making it. An expected value is the average over everything that can happen by chance, weighted by how likely each outcome is, so the score already allows for luck. Equation 2 writes both scores down, one for spots and one for moves.

Figure 7 shows what a state's value looks like on the floor: darker spots are worth more, because the agent expects more return from them. The dark corner sits beside the yellows and the pale corner beside the blues, so the shading is the collector's whole knowledge about where to stand. Equation 2 writes that shading down as a number for every spot.

Equation 2. Once the return scores a whole run, the agent needs to compare spots and moves before it moves. A warehouse robot that must pick which aisle to enter next needs the same scores.

\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{V}}^{\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\pi}}}(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s}}) = \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\mathbb{E}}}_{\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\pi}}}\big[\, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{G_t}} \mid \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s_t}} = \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s}} \,\big]\)
This line says V-pi of s equals the expected value under pi of G-t, given s-t is s. V-pi is the state value under the policy, written pi, the agent's rule for choosing moves. E with the pi below it marks an expected value, an average over everything that can happen by chance, both the moves the policy draws and what the world does, and the expected value is built as a sum: take each possible run from this spot, multiply its return by its chance under the policy, and add them up. The square brackets hold what is averaged, the return. G-t is the return from Equation 1: the banana rewards still to come, added up with the discount, plus 1 for each yellow banana the collector eats and minus 1 for each blue one, until the clock ends the episode. The bar with s-t equals s reads "starting from state s", so the average is taken over runs that start in this spot. Figure 7 draws these values on the floor, darker where the value is higher.
\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{Q}}^{\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\pi}}}(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s}}, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a}}) = \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\mathbb{E}}}_{\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\pi}}}\big[\, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{G_t}} \mid \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s}}, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a}} \,\big]\)
This line says Q-pi of s and a equals the expected value under pi of G-t, given the spot s and the move a. Q-pi is the move value under the policy, the score for a move. The bar with s, a pins the start: s is the spot the collector stands on, and a is one of its four moves played first. The average is built the same way as the line above: take each possible run after that spot and that move, multiply its return G-t by its chance under the policy, and add them up. A move's value is what the agent compares when it chooses, and the sections below teach how it learns these scores one step at a time. Together the two lines say one thing: a value is the return to expect, from a state or after a move.
Take the collector on the spot one step from a yellow banana. Stepping toward it eats the banana on the next move, and the runs after that step branch from the spot past the banana, while stepping away leaves the banana for later runs, so the two moves carry different values even though they start from the same spot.

Before training, the agent has no idea which spots lie near yellows.

Best play is the policy that earns the most return; \(Q^{*}\) is each move’s value under it. Nobody hands the collector that policy; it has to find it by stepping.

5.2 Improving a guess after one step

The collector does not know \(V\) for any spot, so it keeps a guess for each spot and improves it.

The collector needs something to aim its guesses at, and waiting for the episode to end is slow. In the arena the clock can end the run long after a spot was left, so waiting for the end can take many steps. Equation 3 gives two aims: one that waits for the whole return, and one that learns after a single step.

Figure 8 shows the two ways to build a target, and the difference is only when the update happens. Monte Carlo waits for the whole return, so its target is exact for that run but known only at the end; one-step TD updates after a single step, using the guess for the next spot. The trade is exactness against speed, and Equation 3 writes both targets down.

Equation 3. The agent's guesses need something to aim at. A self-driving car that must judge whether a turn was good needs the same choice.

\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#196f3d}{y}}^{\text{MC}}_t = \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{G_t}}\)
This line says y-M-C-t equals G-t. y is a target, the value a guess is pulled toward; MC on top stands for Monte Carlo, and t below is the step the target belongs to. G-t is the return from Equation 1, the score for the run starting at that step: the banana rewards still to come, added up with the discount. The target is exact for that run, because it is the return the episode really earned, but it is known only when the episode ends, and it differs from run to run.
\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#196f3d}{y}}^{\text{TD}}_t = \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#7d6608}{r_{t+1}}} + \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\gamma}}\, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{V}}(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s_{t+1}}})\)
This line says y-T-D-t equals r-t-plus-one plus gamma times V of s-t-plus-one. y-T-D-t is the TD target at step t, and TD stands for temporal difference, the difference between two guesses a step apart. Putting your own guess inside the target like this is called bootstrapping. r-t-plus-one is the reward just seen, plus 1 for a yellow banana and minus 1 for a blue one, gamma is the discount from Equation 1, and V of s-t-plus-one is the agent's own current guess for the next spot, with no policy mark because it is a guess, not a true value. This target builds on the first two cards: the return from Equation 1 and the value from Equation 2. It is ready at once, but it leans on a guess that may be wrong. The walkthrough card in Figure 10 reuses this target as its first line. Together the two lines say one thing: a target is either the return the episode really earned, or one step's reward plus the discounted guess for the next spot.
Take a step where the collector eats a yellow banana and lands one step from another. The Monte Carlo target waits until the clock ends the episode and counts every banana that run earns, while the TD target adds the yellow banana just eaten to the discounted guess for the spot it landed on, and is ready at once. The two targets agree only when the guess for the new spot already matches what the runs from there really earn.

Under the policy, a state is worth what the agent expects from it, and the agent improves its guesses one step at a time instead of waiting for the episode to end. Each step brings a small surprise, and each surprise moves a guess a little. Equation 4 writes that whole step down: the target, the surprise, and the move.

Equation 4. Targets and values are now on the table. A chess program that updates its judgment of a position after each move follows the same rule.

\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#196f3d}{y}}^{\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\pi}}}_t = \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#7d6608}{r_{t+1}}} + \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\gamma}}\, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{V}}^{\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\pi}}}(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s_{t+1}}})\)
This line says y-pi-t equals r-t-plus-one plus gamma times V-pi of s-t-plus-one. y-pi-t is the target at step t under the policy pi, and it is the same shape as the TD target in Equation 3, but with the true values V-pi in place of the agent's guesses. r-t-plus-one is the reward just seen, plus 1 for a yellow banana and minus 1 for a blue one, gamma is the discount, and V-pi of s-t-plus-one is the true value of the next spot, the return the agent would expect from there if it kept following its policy.
\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{V}}^{\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\pi}}}(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s_t}}) = \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\mathbb{E}}}_{\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\pi}}}\big[\, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#196f3d}{y}}^{\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\pi}}}_t \mid \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s_t}} \,\big]\)
This line says V-pi of s-t equals the expected value under pi of y-pi-t, given s-t. V-pi of s-t is the true value of the spot the agent stands on. E with pi below it marks the expected value, and the expected value is built as a sum: take each possible next move and next spot, multiply that outcome's target by its chance under the policy, and add them up. The square brackets hold what is averaged, the target from the first line of this card. The bar with s-t reads "starting from s-t". This is the Bellman equation, the one-step return averaged, and it is the promise the agent's guesses are trying to keep.
\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#b03a2e}{\delta_t}} = \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#196f3d}{y}}^{\text{TD}}_t - \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{V}}(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s_t}})\)
This line says delta-t equals y-T-D-t minus V of s-t. delta-t is the TD error at step t, the target minus the current guess. y-T-D-t is the TD target from Equation 3, and V of s-t is the agent's current guess for the spot it stands on, with no policy mark because it is a guess, not a true value. A positive error means the step went better than guessed, so the guess should rise; a negative error means it went worse, so the guess should fall; when they match, the error is zero and the guess stays.
\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{V}}(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s_t}}) \leftarrow \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{V}}(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s_t}}) + \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\eta}}\, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#b03a2e}{\delta_t}}\)
This line says V of s-t gets replaced by V of s-t plus eta times delta-t. V of s-t on the left is the new guess for the spot, and the arrow reads "replace with", so the old guess is replaced by itself plus a step. eta is the learning rate, the share of the error the guess takes in, and delta-t is the TD error, the target minus the current guess. Raising the learning rate learns faster and swings more, lowering it is steadier and slower. Repeated over many steps, the guesses settle where the error averages out to nothing, and then they satisfy the Bellman equation, which says a state's value is the average of its target. The walkthrough card in Figure 10 reuses this update as its third line. Together the four lines say one thing: aim at the true target, measure the surprise, and move the guess part of the way there, step after step.
Take the collector standing one step from a yellow banana with a low guess for that spot. It steps toward the banana and eats it, so the target comes out above the old guess, the surprise is positive, and the guess for the spot it stood on moves part of the way toward the target. Step after step, the guesses for the spots near the banana rise until the surprise averages out to nothing.

After many steps the guesses for spots near yellows end up higher than those near blues.

6 Explore early, exploit late

If the agent keeps a score for every move, it faces a choice at every step: play the best-scored move, or try one it knows less about. The best-looking move can hide a better route the agent never tried, and yellows the top-scored move never turns toward can stay unseen for a whole episode. Equation 5 writes the rule that balances the two.

Equation 5. Values tell the agent which move looks best, but the best-looking move is only as good as the scores behind it, and untried moves have no scores at all. A streaming service choosing which film to recommend faces the same balance.

\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a_t}} = \arg\max_{\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a}}}\, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{Q}}(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s_t}}, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a}})\)
This line says a-t equals argmax over a of Q of s-t and a: the move played is the move with the largest score. a-t is the move at step t, one of the collector's four moves. argmax over a picks the move whose score is largest, scanning every move the agent can make. Q of s-t and a is the agent's current move score for move a in the spot s-t, the score Equation 2 defined. The policy this line writes is greedy, it always takes the best it knows, which is why it needs the second line.
\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a_t}} = \text{a random move} \quad \text{with chance } \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\epsilon}}\)
This line says a-t equals a random move, with chance epsilon. epsilon is the explore share, the chance of a random move, and "a random move" means any of the agent's moves, picked with no regard to their scores. Raising it means more trying and slower use of what the agent knows; lowering it uses what it knows and may never find a better route. Together the two lines say one thing: with chance one minus epsilon play the best-scored move, and with chance epsilon play any move at random. This rule is called epsilon-greedy, greedy for the best move with a small chance of exploring. The code just below runs exactly this rule, and the race in Figure 9 implements it.
Take the collector at a crossing where one move leads toward a yellow banana and the others lead away. Most of the time it plays the best-scored move toward the banana, and once in a while it tries another move at random instead. That random step is how it can stumble on a banana it had never scored, and the scores it learns from the stumble are what the greedy move uses next time.

An agent can also shrink \(\epsilon\) a little after every episode, so early on it mostly explores and late in training it mostly plays its top-scored move; Q-Learning shows the rule.

def epsilon_greedy(q_values, eps):
    if np.random.rand() < eps:
        return np.random.randint(len(q_values))
    return np.argmax(q_values)
  1. 1With chance eps, draw a random move.
  2. 2Otherwise play the top-scored move.

Figure 9 shows two warehouse robots learning the same floor by the rule from Equation 5, one with a fixed epsilon and one whose epsilon shrinks after every episode. The three buttons set the fixed robot's epsilon, small, some or a lot, and Play runs both robots for 200 episodes while Reset starts them over; each floor shows the robot's path, and the readout under each floor says whether it has settled or is still wandering. Watch the ledge between start and dock: the fixed robot still wanders and falls, while the shrinking robot settles on the short route beside it.

Exploring is easiest to watch on a small floor where every step can be seen. A warehouse robot looks for its charging dock; the ledge is the strip of floor between the start and the dock. It starts in one bottom corner and the dock sits in the other. Each move’s reward is minus the battery it uses, so the best total is the one closest to zero. Every move costs a little battery; stepping onto the ledge costs a lot and puts the robot back at the start; only reaching the dock ends the episode. The cheapest way to the dock is the shortest path that never steps onto the ledge. A step onto the ledge is a fall. The shortest route runs right beside the ledge, so one random step there is a fall.

Figure 11 shows the trained agent at work: it heads straight for the yellow bananas and curves around the blue ones instead of wandering. My trained agent learns a score for each move with a neural network, the method taught in Deep Q-Networks (DQN), plus refinements to how those scores are computed. Over training the agent’s total per episode climbs as it learns to head for yellows.

9 Reward hacking

Reward hacking is when the agent earns the reward without doing what the designer meant. In the boat racing game CoastRunners, an OpenAI agent learned to circle and hit the same targets again and again instead of finishing the race.

Reward shaping adds extra pay for progress toward the goal. The safe kind also takes the pay back when the agent moves away, so going round a loop earns no net bonus.

A reward model is a network trained to score answers the way people would. A chat model trained against such a score can learn replies that please the score more than the people it stands for, as Aligning Language Models with RL shows.

The tool runs in a small practice arena with one yellow banana and a few blue ones; a banana does not come back once eaten, and a clock ends the episode. In this arena the designer pays a small bonus whenever a step brings the agent closer to the yellow banana.

Equation 7. The reward is the only pay the agent hears, so what the designer writes here decides what the agent learns. A delivery company that pays its drivers per delivery faces the same gap: pay for deliveries, and drivers rush; pay for careful driving, and they slow down.

\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#7d6608}{r_{t+1}}} = \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#b7950b}{\text{yellow}_{t+1}}} - \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{\text{blue}_{t+1}}}\)
This line says r-t-plus-one equals yellow-t-plus-one minus blue-t-plus-one: the meant pay is the yellow count minus the blue count. r-t-plus-one is the reward after the move at step t, the meant pay. yellow-t-plus-one is one when the step ate the yellow banana and zero otherwise, and blue-t-plus-one is one when the step ate a blue banana and zero otherwise. The minus sign takes the blue count off the yellow count, so eating a blue banana cancels a yellow one.
\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#7d6608}{r^{\text{shaped}}_{t+1}}} = \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#7d6608}{r_{t+1}}} + \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{c}}\, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{b_{t+1}}}\)
This line says r-shaped-t-plus-one equals r-t-plus-one plus c times b-t-plus-one: the pay the agent is actually given equals the meant reward plus the bonus for getting closer. r-shaped-t-plus-one is the shaped reward, the pay after shaping, and "shaped" on top marks it as the changed pay. c is the bonus size, the pay for one step closer, and b-t-plus-one is one when the step brought the agent closer to the yellow banana and zero otherwise. The bonus pays only for getting closer and never charges for moving away. The reward tool in Figure 12 implements this shaped pay. Together the two lines say one thing: the meant pay counts bananas, the given pay adds a bonus for progress, and the bonus is what gets gamed.
Take a step that brings the collector closer to the yellow banana without eating it. The meant pay is nothing, since no banana was eaten, but the shaped pay adds the bonus for getting closer, so the agent is paid for progress even before it eats. That is the bonus a looping agent games: it can earn the bonus again and again by stepping closer, then away, then closer, without ever eating.

Figure 12 shows two agents in the same practice arena, one paid for bananas only and one paid a bonus for getting closer to the yellow banana. The three buttons set the bonus from Equation 7, none, small or large, and Play runs both agents for 4,000 episodes while Reset starts them over; each panel shows its agent's arena and a readout saying what it learned. With a large bonus, watch the right agent: it steps in and back out beside the yellow banana without eating it, because every step closer pays. With a small bonus, or none, eating is still worth more, because each later bonus counts a little less.

10 Scores, chances or one fixed output

An observation is what the agent senses at one moment. Here an observation is the arena’s sensor readings, which can be less than the full state. A spot with a blue banana just out of sight and a spot without one can give the collector the same readings.

A stochastic policy gives each move a chance and draws its move, instead of always playing the same move in the same state. A deterministic policy always plays the same move in the same state. The trained banana agent compares move scores and plays the top one. Equation 8 writes the drawing down.

Some agents, like a robot arm’s, output one amount every time instead of picking from a list of moves.

Figure 13 shows the three ways a policy can pick a move, and the choice is the last thing this page teaches. Scores pick the tallest bar, chances draw one bar by lottery, and one fixed output is the same arrow every time. The trained banana agent uses scores, a chat model that samples its next word uses chances, and a robot arm that outputs one force uses the fixed output.

Equation 8. The page has shown policies that pick the top-scored move and policies that sometimes pick at random. It is the last equation on the page, and it opens the door to the pages that learn the policy itself. A chat model choosing its next word faces the same choice.

\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a}} \sim \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\pi}}(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a}} \mid \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s}})\)
This line says a is drawn from pi of a given s: the move is drawn at random from the policy's chances for this spot. a is the move the agent makes, one of the collector's four moves in the arena. The drawn-from sign reads 'drawn at random from', so the move is not picked but drawn, like a lottery. pi of a given s is the policy's chance of move a in state s, the bar reading 'given', so each move's chance depends on the spot the agent stands on. Figure 13 draws this in its middle panel, chances with one drawn. The trained banana agent does not draw this way, it plays its top-scored move from Equation 5, but the policy-gradient pages next on the road learn a policy of exactly this shape. So the whole equation says this: the move is drawn at random from the policy's chances for the spot it stands on.
Take the collector on a spot where the policy gives one move most of the chance and the others a little each. Most draws pick the favored move, but now and then a draw picks another, which is how a policy of this shape explores without a separate random branch. A chat model choosing its next word works the same way: it gives every word a chance and draws one.
