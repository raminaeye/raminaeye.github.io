The paper behind the diplomat

The paper

Bo Liu, Xingchao Liu, Xiaojie Jin, Peter Stone, Qiang Liu

Conflict-Averse Gradient Descent for Multi-task Learning.

NeurIPS 2021 · arXiv:2110.14048

The intuition

Here is how I think about CAGrad: it is a diplomat on a leash. The leash is a circle of radius \(c\) times \(\lVert g_0 \rVert\) drawn around the average gradient \(g_0\). The update is not allowed to stray farther than that from the consensus direction, because staying close is what keeps the convergence proof alive. Inside the circle, the diplomat spends all of its limited freedom on a single goal: help the worst-off task as much as possible.

Turn c to 0 and the circle shrinks to a point, which is plain gradient descent. Let c grow very large and the leash comes off, which is MGDA. The useful range is in between.

The problem

Remember the scoring rule: the dot product \(\langle g_i, d \rangle\) scores a candidate step \(d\). Positive means task \(i\)'s loss goes down. CAGrad judges a step by its worst score across all tasks, then picks the step whose worst score is the best:

\[\max_d \min_i \langle g_i, d \rangle \quad \text{subject to} \quad \|d - g_0\| \le c\|g_0\|\]

Inside a ball around the average gradient, find the direction that helps the least-helped task the most.

Every symbol, one at a time:

  • \(d\) is the update we are choosing, the step the optimizer will actually take.
  • \(i\) ranges over the tasks, \(i = 1, \dots, K\).
  • \(\langle g_i, d \rangle\) measures how much the update \(d\) helps task \(i\): the dot product of task \(i\)'s gradient \(g_i\) with \(d\). Positive means task \(i\)'s loss goes down.
  • \(\min_i\) takes the worst score across all tasks, so the inner minimum is the least-helped task's score.
  • \(\max_d\) picks the update whose worst score is the largest: the direction that helps the least-helped task the most.
  • \(g_0\) is the average gradient, the plain gradient-descent direction.
  • \(\lVert d - g_0 \rVert \le c\lVert g_0 \rVert\) is the leash: \(d\) must stay inside a ball of radius \(c\lVert g_0 \rVert\) centered on the average gradient.
  • \(c\) controls the leash length. \(c = 0\) pins \(d\) to \(g_0\), which is plain gradient descent. Large \(c\) lets \(d\) roam, which is MGDA.

    Read it from the inside out. Pick any candidate d. Let every task score it and keep only the lowest score. Choose the d with the highest lowest score. If a step exists that helps every task, the best worst score is positive and every task improves.

    Four choices, four reasons

    Every piece of that problem statement is doing a job:

    1. Why judge a step by its worst task? The average score is exactly what betrays a quiet task: a loud task can outvote a hurt one and the average never notices. Taking the minimum is a weakest-link rule. Betrayal has nowhere to hide.
    2. Why the ball around g0? Without it the rule turns into MGDA, which wanders as far from the average as it takes to keep the peace, with no promise about where it ends up. The ball is what buys the proof: any d within c||g0|| of g0 keeps a positive fraction of g0's downward push on the average loss.
    3. Why radius c||g0||, not a fixed number? So the leash grows and shrinks with the terrain. Early in training gradients are large and a fixed radius would be uselessly tight; late in training it would be uselessly loose. A fixed fraction keeps the diplomat's freedom constant in the only units that matter, and it makes c unit-free, so no rescaling of the losses can game it.
    4. Why solve the dual? The primal searches over d in the full parameter space, millions of dimensions, at every step. The dual searches over K blend weights, and K is 2 to 50. Same answer, vastly less work: the solve is negligible next to a backward pass for small K.

    The update, in plain words

    Searching over every possible d directly is hopeless: d lives in the full parameter space, millions of dimensions. But the answer has a simple shape. The best update is the average gradient plus one bounded nudge:

    \[d^* = g_0 + \frac{\sqrt{\phi}}{\lVert g_w^*\rVert}\, g_w^*\]

    Term by term, in plain words. \(g_0\) is the consensus: the plain average of the task gradients. The nudge has a direction and a length. The direction is \(g_w^*/\lVert g_w^*\rVert\), the best blend of task gradients scaled to length 1: it points at the compromise that helps the worst-off task most. The length is \(\sqrt{\phi} = c\lVert g_0\rVert\), exactly the leash length. So the update is the consensus plus one step of fixed length \(c\lVert g_0\rVert\) toward the best compromise, and it always lands exactly on the ball's boundary.

    Let me work it once with real numbers so it is not just symbols. Take \(g_1 = (3, 1)\), \(g_2 = (-1, 2)\), and \(c = 0.5\).

    1. The consensus: \(g_0 = \left(\frac{3 + (-1)}{2}, \frac{1 + 2}{2}\right) = (1, 1.5)\), with length \(\lVert g_0\rVert = \sqrt{3.25} \approx 1.803\).
    2. The leash: \(\phi = c^2\lVert g_0\rVert^2 = 0.25 \times 3.25 = 0.8125\), so the nudge length is \(\sqrt{\phi} \approx 0.901\).
    3. The best blend: score each blend with \(F(w) = g_w^T g_0 + \sqrt{\phi}\,\lVert g_w\rVert\) (the blend's score; where it comes from is in the note below).
      \(w = 0\): \(F = (-1)(1) + (2)(1.5) + 0.901 \times \sqrt{5} \approx 4.016\).
      \(w = 0.5\): \(F \approx 4.875\).
      \(w = 1\): \(F \approx 7.350\).
      The smallest is at \(w^* = 0\), so \(g_w^* = (-1, 2)\) and \(\lVert g_w^*\rVert = \sqrt{5} \approx 2.236\).
    4. The update: \(d^* = (1, 1.5) + \frac{0.901}{2.236}(-1, 2) = (1, 1.5) + 0.403 \times (-1, 2) = (0.597, 2.306)\).
    5. The check: \(\langle d^*, g_1 \rangle = 0.597 \times 3 + 2.306 \times 1 \approx 4.10\), and \(\langle d^*, g_2 \rangle = 0.597 \times (-1) + 2.306 \times 2 \approx 4.01\). The worst score is about 4.0, positive, so both tasks improve. And \(\lVert d^* - g_0\rVert \approx 0.901\): the full leash length, so the update sits exactly on the ball's boundary.

    For the curious: where the formula comes from. Judging a step by its worst task is the same as judging it by its worst blend of tasks, because a blend can never score below its worst ingredient. That turns the inner minimum over tasks into a minimum over blend weights, and standard duality lets the min and the max swap places. The inner problem over d then solves exactly, leaving a small problem over the K blend weights: \(w^* = \arg\min_w F(w)\) with \(F(w) = g_w^T g_0 + \sqrt{\phi}\,\lVert g_w\rVert\) and \(\phi = c^2\lVert g_0\rVert^2\). Since K is 2 to 50, this solve is negligible next to a backward pass, and it is exactly what the code below computes.

    One honest footnote on the formula. The published Algorithm 1 stops at the d* above. The reference implementation's toy example (github.com/Cranial-XIX/CAGrad, toy.py) then divides by (1 + c), returning d*/(1 + c). That rescale is a toy.py convention, not part of the published algorithm. The race below uses it, so the numbers match the reference code; when you read the paper, expect the unrescaled d*.

    One training step

    1. Forward pass; compute each task loss Li.
    2. Compute each task gradient gi and their average g0 = (1/K)Σgi.
    3. Solve the dual: over all blends w in the simplex, minimize F(w) = gwTg0 + c||g0|| ||gw||, where gw = Σwigi.
    4. Form the update d = (g0 + c||g0|| gw*/||gw*||) / (1 + c) from the winning blend w*. The division by (1 + c) is the reference repo's toy.py convention, labeled as such above.
    5. Step the network with d as usual.
    import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize

def cagrad_combine(grads, c=0.4):
    G = np.stack(grads); g0 = G.mean(axis=0); K = len(G)
    phi = c**2 * (g0 @ g0)
    def F(w):                       # the dual objective
        gw = w @ G
        return gw @ g0 + np.sqrt(phi) * np.linalg.norm(gw)
    w = minimize(F, np.full(K, 1/K), bounds=[(0, 1)] * K,
                 constraints={'type': 'eq', 'fun': lambda w: w.sum() - 1}).x
    gw = w @ G
    # last line: the reference repo's toy.py convention (not in the published Algorithm 1)
    return (g0 + c * np.linalg.norm(g0) * gw / np.linalg.norm(gw)) / (1 + c)

    The last line is the diplomat on a leash in one expression: the consensus direction, plus one bounded act of independence.

    One knob, two classics

    The knob c is a dial between two older methods. At c = 0 the ball shrinks to a point: CAGrad is plain gradient descent. As c grows large, the dual is dominated by the ||gw|| term, which is exactly MGDA's shortest-blend rule. The new territory is in between: more careful about conflict than plain descent, more purposeful than MGDA.

    MGDA is Ozan Sener and Vladlen Koltun's method: Multi-Task Learning as Multi-Objective Optimization, NeurIPS 2018, arXiv:1810.04650. The Pareto page walks through its shortest-blend rule step by step.

    How to set c: I start at 0.2 to 0.5. If the worst task is still being ignored (its loss flatlines while the others fall), I raise c, which buys that task a louder voice. If the average loss improves too slowly, I lower c, since I am straying too far from the objective. Too small (c near 0) and I inherit GD's stalls; too large and I inherit MGDA's scattered landings, and above c = 1 the proof is gone. The good news is the sweet spot is wide: on the toy, c = 0.5 and c = 0.8 descend from every start, while c = 0.2 still stalls from the two hardest starts at 20,000 steps.

    The guarantee, translated

    Σt ||∇L0(θt)||² ≤ 2(L0(θ0) - L0*) / (α(1 - c²))

    The squared steepness of the average loss, added over all steps, never exceeds a fixed budget. So the steepness must shrink to zero: a stationary point of L0.

    The assumptions in plain words: H-Lipschitz gradients means the landscape never curves too sharply (a smoothness budget). Bounded below means the loss has a floor it cannot fall under. Step size α ≤ 1/H means steps small enough for that curvature. The budget makes sense too: the numerator is how far above the floor you start, and the denominator says bigger steps buy progress while a bigger c costs you some.

    1. You keep your objective. Unlike MGDA or PCGrad, the proven destination is a stationary point of L0 itself, not some arbitrary trade-off chosen by the starting point.
    2. Balancing has a price. The guarantee weakens by (1 - c²): more conflict-avoidance, slower worst-case progress.
    3. The guarantee only holds for c < 1, which is why the recommended range sits comfortably below 1 (c = 0.4 on NYUv2, 0.2 on Cityscapes).

    What the replay shows

    1. GD stalls. Its trail dies in the ravine on three of five starts, with final average losses of 2.49, 1.69, and 3.50, miles above the minimum -15.09. On the trapped runs the two task gradients fight on about half of all steps and never stop: conflict is not a passing storm, it is the climate of the trap.
    2. MGDA and PCGrad escape. MGDA reaches the low-loss region on all five runs, PCGrad on four of five. But they stop wherever they first touch the Pareto set, with final average losses anywhere from about -10 to -15.1, decided by the start, not by the task.
    3. CAGrad (c = 0.5) keeps descending. Two of five runs land exactly on the star (-15.09); the other three are still on their way down, at -12.7 to -15.0, when the 20,000 steps run out.
    4. The c-dial confirmed. c = 0 reproduces GD's runs exactly, c = 10 behaves like MGDA with scattered landings, and c = 0.2 still stalls on the two hardest starts at 20,000 steps. The middle of the dial is the new territory.

    Practical notes

    1. Reach for it when you want PCGrad-style conflict handling and a real objective to stand behind, especially when runs from different starting points need to behave the same way.
    2. Costs: K backward passes plus one tiny solve per step. With many tasks, CAGrad-Fast works on a random handful of tasks per step and the guarantee survives: about 2x and 5x faster than PCGrad on the 10- and 50-task Meta-World benchmarks, with similar results.
    3. Keep in mind: the proof assumes smooth losses and small steps. Real networks are messier, so treat c as a knob you tune even though it has a theoretical meaning.
    4. The hidden knob: how many tasks per step. With many tasks, the K backward passes are the real cost. Treat the CAGrad-Fast subset size like a batch size: bigger is steadier and costlier, smaller is noisier and faster.
