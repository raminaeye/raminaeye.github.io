Multilayer Perceptrons (MLP)

When a line is not enough.

One line, four blobs, no chance

Here is a question I love. Four blobs of dots at the corners of a square. Top-left and bottom-right are blue, top-right and bottom-left are orange: the classic XOR layout. Your model gets one straight cut. One line. Can it separate them?

No. And that is not a trick, it is the whole point. Draw any straight line you like: it always strands two same-color blobs on opposite sides. Logistic regression draws the best straight line it can and manages 41%, worse than a coin flip. If your data will not sit still for a straight cut, your model needs bends.

The blobs are not random decoration. Four Gaussian blobs in an XOR layout, 1,200 points, 8% of the labels flipped: the same blob family the kNN notebook builds with make_classification(..., n_clusters_per_class=2). Everything on this page trains on those blobs.

The humblest bend

The fix starts with the humblest possible bend: the ReLU.

$$ \text{relu}(z) = \max(0, z) $$

Read it: negative in, zero out. Positive in, straight through. It is a hinge. Flat on the left, a ramp on the right, one kink at zero. Check it: $\text{relu}(-2) = 0$ and $\text{relu}(3) = 3$.

Now here is the move. A sum of hinges is a piecewise line, and you get to choose where every kink goes. Try it on the curve below, $y = x^2$: a plain line stalls with the MSE stuck at 1.45 (a line is a line, it cannot bend). One hinge buys you 1.18. Three hinges, 0.19. Eight hinges, 0.01, and the blue polyline is sitting on the dashed curve. Same data, same least-squares fit. The only difference is the bends.

Play with it. Each slider stop adds one more hinge, and the blue polyline is the best fit those hinges can manage:

How to play

  • The point: A curve is just line pieces with bends. Every ReLU is one bend, and stacking more bends lets the fit hug $y = x^2$ tighter.
  • What you'll see: The plot opens with just the dashed parabola, no fit yet. Press Play and the hinge count marches from 1 to 8; the blue polyline starts as a shallow V, grows kinks at the red ticks, and settles onto the dashed parabola while the MSE readout collapses.
  • Hinges: Draws the best fit for that bend budget by hand; try 1 and watch the best possible fit still miss badly, then drag to 8 and watch the kinks multiply until the blue line and the dashed curve are barely distinguishable.
  • Reset: Puts the hinge count back at 3 and clears the fit back to the bare parabola. Try it after a run and watch the blue line vanish so you can grow it again.

That is the whole idea of a neural network, minus the buzzwords. One hinge bends once. A layer of hinges bends everywhere at once, each in its own direction. Stack layers and the bends compose into curves, curls, and eventually the boxy little fences around our four blobs.

Hinges with learned positions

A one-hidden-layer MLP is just hinges with learned positions and learned weights. Written out:

$$ h = W_2 \cdot \text{relu}(W_1 x + b_1) + b_2 $$

Read it inside out. $W_1 x + b_1$ draws a pile of straight lines, one per hidden unit. $\text{relu}$ bends each one at zero. $W_2$ and $b_2$ mix the bent pieces into the final answer. Every symbol earns its keep, so here is a tiny one you can check by hand, two hidden units, input $x = [1, 2]$:

  1. First the lines: $W_1 x + b_1$ with $W_1 = [[1, -1], [0.5, 0.5]]$ and $b_1 = [0, -0.5]$. Unit one: $1\cdot1 + (-1)\cdot2 + 0 = $ $-1$. Unit two: $0.5\cdot1 + 0.5\cdot2 - 0.5 = $ $1$.
  2. Then the bends: $\text{relu}([-1, 1]) = $ $[0, 1]$. The first hinge went flat, the second stayed a ramp.
  3. Then the mix: $W_2 = [2, -1]$, $b_2 = 0.5$. Output $h = 2\cdot0 + (-1)\cdot1 + 0.5 = $ $-0.5$.

One gotcha before the fun part, and it is the reason the notebook's two-linear-layers model flopped. Stack two linear layers with no ReLU between them and watch what happens:

$$ W_2(W_1 x + b_1) + b_2 = (W_2 W_1) x + (W_2 b_1 + b_2) $$

It collapses into one matrix and one bias. Linear of linear is still linear, a fancier straight line, still useless on the blobs. The bend is not decoration. It is the entire source of power.

The capacity dial

Now the main event. Four models, same four blobs, and the boundaries tell the whole story. I trained all four offline in NumPy, plain Adam, no tricks, and froze each model's boundary so you can sweep through them. One dial, four eras:

How to play

  • The point: One dial grows the model from a straight line to a smooth curve to a crazy overfit boundary, and regularization tames it back.
  • What you'll see: The plot opens with just the labeled points, no boundary drawn yet. Press Play and the dial sweeps 13 stops from linear to tamed; the boundary is re-contoured live at every stop, morphing from a straight cut to a clean four-way split to a wiggly mess and back to calm.
  • Capacity: Draws the model for that dial position, scrubbing through the four trained models with live interpolation between them; try parking at 3 and studying the crazy wiggles, then nudge to 4 and watch L2 iron them out.
  • Reset: Puts the dial back at 2 and clears the model back to the bare points. Try it after a sweep and watch the boundary wash away so you can draw it again.

Drag the dial through the four stops and watch the story unfold. It opens with a straight cut that manages 41% on train: it cannot bend, so it fails. A small MLP, 128 down to 64, draws a smooth four-way split and hits 92.6%: it learned the XOR and ignored the flipped labels. Push capacity further and the overkill net, 512 down to 32, climbs to 95.1%, but look at the boundary: little islands drawn around the mislabeled points. It memorized the noise. That is overfitting you can see. The last stop keeps the same giant net but taxes the weights with L2, and the boundary relaxes back to the clean answer, 92.6% again. Capacity is not the enemy; untaxed capacity is.

Taming the crazy

So the overkill model memorized the blobs and drew a boundary that wiggles around individual noisy points. That wiggle is the whole disease: the weights grew huge so the model could bend sharply around every flipped label. Regularization is a tax on weights, and there are two classic tax codes:

$$ L = L_{\text{data}} + \lambda \sum_i |w_i| \quad \text{(L1)} $$
$$ L = L_{\text{data}} + \lambda \sum_i w_i^2 \quad \text{(L2)} $$

L1 is a flat tax per unit of weight. Small weights cost more than they are worth, so they get fired to exactly zero. That is why L1 gives you sparse models. L2 is a progressive tax: it punishes big weights quadratically, so everything shrinks but nothing quite dies. Check it on $w = [2, -3]$ with $\lambda = 0.1$: L1 adds $0.1 \times (2 + 3) = $ $0.5$, L2 adds $0.1 \times (4 + 9) = $ $1.3$.

The fourth model is the overkill net retrained with L2 ($\lambda = 3\times 10^{-4}$), and that is the "tamed" stop on the dial: same giant architecture, but the weights are no longer allowed to go wild, so the boundary relaxes back toward the small net's clean answer. Dropout is the other classic trick: randomly switch off neurons during training, so the net can never lean on one hero neuron and has to learn redundant paths. Same spirit, different tax.

What if the model asks questions?

You now have the full MLP story: bends from ReLUs, power from stacking, and a dial that runs from too simple to too crazy to just right. But notice what every model here assumed. The boundary was always some smooth function of the inputs, a curve drawn through space.

What if the model is not a line at all, but a pile of yes-or-no questions? Is petal width above some threshold? Is x1 to the left of some cut? Ask enough questions and you can carve up any space, no curves required. That is a decision tree, and a crowd of them voting is a random forest. Next: twenty questions, then a forest.
