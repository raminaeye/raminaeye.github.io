Eigenvalues, Eigenvectors and SVD

The directions a matrix only stretches, and how to keep the big ones.

Before you start Matrix times a vector The multivariate Gaussian

On this page

A matrix turns most arrows it multiplies. A few directions only get longer or shorter. These are its eigenvectors, and the stretch of each is its eigenvalue. For face photos, the eigenvectors of the pixels’ covariance (Section 3) are the directions the pixels vary along most. PCA turns them into face-shaped directions, the eigenfaces. Collapse checks test that a model’s output vectors have not squeezed onto a few directions. They list the eigenvalues, the spectrum. LoRA fine-tunes a model with a low-rank update, one built from a few directions.

1 The few directions that matter

2 Directions a matrix only stretches

PCA’s eigenfaces and the axes of GMM’s ellipses are these directions. The example is \(R\), the correlation table of two face pixels across PCA’s 400 photos: its diagonal holds each pixel’s own correlation, 1, and its off-diagonal says how strongly the two pixels brighten together.

A direction the matrix only stretches is an eigenvector; the stretch is its eigenvalue.
\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{M} \htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{v} = \htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\lambda } \htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{v}\)
\(M\) is any matrix and \(v\) a direction, an arrow from the origin. \(Mv\) is the matrix’s output for it (Vectors and Matrices, section 5). \(\lambda\) (lambda) is the stretch: the output points along \(v\), only \(\lambda\) times as long. In Figure 2 the matrix is \(R\): the both-pixels-brighter direction keeps its line and only stretches, so it is an eigenvector, while a one-pixel direction turns, so it is not.

Section 4 finds every such pair for a covariance at once.

3 Covariance from data

Builds on the outer product (Vectors and Matrices, section 6.3) and covariance (Probability and Bayes Rule, section 5)

PCA and collapse checks build this matrix, the covariance, here written \(C\). Figure 3 shows a few faces at the same two pixels: the covariance measures how their cloud spreads and tilts around its mean.

The covariance averages, over faces, each centred face times itself as an outer product.
\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{C} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{j} \htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{(x_{j} - \bar{x})(x_{j} - \bar{x})^{T}}\)
\(x_{j}\) holds face \(j\)’s two pixel values and \(\bar{x}\) (x bar) their mean over the \(N\) faces, so \(x_{j} - \bar{x}\) is face \(j\) centred, the green arrow in Figure 3. Times its own transpose it makes a \(2 \times 2\) table, the outer product of Vectors and Matrices, section 6.3. \(\sum_{j}\) adds the \(N\) tables and \(\frac{1}{N}\) averages them. The diagonal of \(C\) holds each pixel’s variance, its average squared distance from the mean; the off-diagonal says how the two move together.

4 Every covariance splits into its directions

\(R\) of Section 2 is itself a covariance: the covariance of the two pixels after each is scaled to variance 1. One direction, both pixels brighter together, carries most of its variance; the other, one pixel brighter and one darker, carries the rest; and the eigenvalues add up to the total variance, the sum of the diagonal, because turning the axes onto the eigen-directions moves variance between directions but never creates or loses any.

A covariance splits into its eigenvectors and eigenvalues: turn to the eigen-directions, stretch each, turn back.
\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{C} = \htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{V} \htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\Lambda } \htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{V^{T}}\)
\(V\) holds the eigenvectors \(v_{1}\) and \(v_{2}\) as columns; \(\Lambda\) (capital lambda) holds the eigenvalues \(\lambda_{1} \ge \lambda_{2}\) on its diagonal, 0 elsewhere. Reading right to left, \(V^{T}\) turns a point onto the eigen-directions, \(\Lambda\) stretches each by its \(\lambda\), and \(V\) turns it back. For \(R\), the columns of \(V\) are the both-pixels-brighter direction and the one-brighter-one-darker direction, each of length 1.
\(\displaystyle e^{T} \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{C} e \le \htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\lambda _{1}}\)
\(e\) is any direction of length 1 and \(e^{T} C e\) the variance along it. Any direction is a mix of the eigen-directions, so its variance is a weighted average of the eigenvalues and can never beat the largest. It peaks at \(\lambda _{1}\) when \(e\) is \(v_{1}\), so PCA’s first eigenface is the direction of most variance. A diagonal \(C\) has the axes as eigenvectors; equal eigenvalues make a round cloud, the diag and spherical shapes of GMM.
\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{V^{T}} \htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{V} = I\)
\(I\) is the identity, 1 on the diagonal and 0 elsewhere. So \(V\) is orthogonal: its columns have length 1 and meet at right angles, and it keeps every length, only turning or flipping. RoPE uses such turns to mark word positions in attention without changing vector lengths.

Dividing each point’s coordinate along each eigen-direction by the \(\sqrt{\lambda}\) of that direction gives variance 1 in every direction, which is whitening (PCA variants); ICA starts from whitened data.

5 Every matrix turns, stretches and turns

Builds on the norm (Vectors and Matrices, section 2)

Spectral norm and PCA in practice read a matrix through its singular value decomposition (SVD). The example is an update in LoRA’s shape, one column times one row, as in Vectors and Matrices, section 8: it stretches one direction and flattens every other (Figure 4).

Any matrix is a turn, a stretch along the axes, and another turn.
\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{M} = \htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{U} \htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{S} \htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{V^{T}}\)
\(V^{T}\) turns the input so the directions \(M\) stretches most lie on the axes. \(S\) is diagonal and stretches axis \(i\) by the singular value \(s_{i} \ge 0\), largest first. \(U\) turns the result into place. \(U\) and \(V\) are orthogonal, and a zero \(s_{i}\) flattens its direction, as in panel 3 of Figure 4.
\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\lambda _{i}} = \frac{s_{i}^{2}}{N}\)
Take \(X\), the centred faces, one per row. Its \(V\) holds the eigenvectors of their covariance, the same \(V\) as in Section 4, and each eigenvalue is a squared singular value over \(N\): a singular value measures how far the data reaches along its direction, and squaring it and averaging over the \(N\) faces turns that reach into variance. So PCA in practice gets the eigenfaces from one SVD, without building \(C\).

6 Keep only the biggest stretches

Builds on rank (Vectors and Matrices, section 8)

PCA reconstruction, collapse checks, matrix factorization and LoRA keep, count or learn only a few directions; \(k\) is the number kept.

wherewhat is kept
PCA facesa few eigenfaces, which hold most of the variance
collapse checksthe count of non-zero eigenvalues; a zero means the outputs lie on a flat slice of the space
ratings, a row per user and a column per itemnearly low rank: a few taste directions explain most ratings
LoRA, rank 8a low-rank update, a few columns times a few rows, learned in place of a full weight change
Keep the \(k\) largest stretches and drop the rest.
\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{M} \approx \sum_{i\,=\,1}^{k} \htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{s_{i}} \htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{u_{i}} \htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{v_{i}^{T}}\)
\(u_{i}\) and \(v_{i}\) are column \(i\) of \(U\) and \(V\), so \(u_{i} v_{i}^{T}\) is one outer product, a rank-1 block stored as a column, a row and its weight \(s_{i}\). The sum from \(i = 1\) to \(k\) adds the first \(k\) blocks; \(\approx\) (nearly equal) leaves out the rest, which is the error. The first \(k\) give the best rank-\(k\) copy in squared error (Eckart and Young, 1936). Blocks may hold negative numbers that cancel. NMF (non-negative matrix factorization) keeps every block non-negative, so the blocks add up as parts.
\(\displaystyle \text{kept share} = \frac{\sum_{i\,=\,1}^{k} s_{i}^{2}}{\sum_{i} s_{i}^{2}}\)
The share of \(M\)’s squared size (the sum of its squared entries) that the first \(k\) blocks keep. For centred data each \(s_{i}^{2}\) is \(N\) times an eigenvalue, so this is the eigenvalue share. For PCA’s faces it is the blue line in Figure 5: it climbs fast over the first few eigenfaces and then flattens.

One photo is also a matrix, \(32 \times 32\) brightness values. Minus its average brightness, its own SVD rebuilds it block by block; PCA’s reconstruction tool instead uses eigenfaces shared by all 400 photos.

7 Eigenvectors and SVD in NumPy

The block builds the covariance of a faces array, runs eigh on it and svd on the centred data. Both give the same eigenvalues.

import numpy as np

def eigen_directions(faces):
    # faces: one photo per row
    X = faces - faces.mean(axis=0)
    C = np.cov(X.T, bias=True)
    lam, V = np.linalg.eigh(C)
    U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(
        X, full_matrices=False)
    lam_svd = s**2 / len(X)
    return (lam[::-1], V[:, ::-1],
            lam_svd, Vt)
  1. 1The covariance (Section 3): centre the photos, then average the outer products; bias=True divides by \(N\).
  2. 2The eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the covariance (Section 4), smallest first.
  3. 3The same eigenvalues from the SVD of the centred data (Section 5), without building \(C\); the rows of Vt are the eigenfaces, and the return puts both lists largest first.
