Pruning

Take a ResNet-18 that hits 92.58% accuracy on STL-10. Delete half of its weights. Accuracy collapses to 66.25%. Fine-tune for a few epochs and it climbs back to 92.74%, with 5.5 million weights no longer doing any work.

That is pruning in one paragraph. Neural networks are overparameterized: most weights are just along for the ride. If you can figure out which weights barely matter and remove them, you get a smaller, faster, cheaper-to-deploy model without giving up what the network learned.

Two flavors of pruning

There are two ways to cut. Unstructured pruning removes individual weights wherever they are, leaving a sparse Swiss cheese of a matrix. Structured pruning removes whole filters or channels at a time, which actually shrinks the layer. Drag the slider and flip between the two to feel the difference:

Unstructured pruning just zeros weights. Those zeros are still multiplied at inference time, so the model is not actually faster unless your backend has sparse kernels that skip them. Structured pruning removes whole rows, so the layer genuinely gets smaller and every backend benefits.

How PyTorch actually does it

Here is the part that surprises people: PyTorch does not delete anything at first. When you prune a layer, it keeps the original weights as weight_orig and attaches a binary weight_mask next to them. During the forward pass the effective weight is just weight_orig * weight_mask. The zeros are a fiction maintained by the mask, which is why you can always unprune or finalize later.

Pruning every convolution and linear layer by L1 magnitude, keeping the 50% largest weights, is a few lines: 

import torch.nn.utils.prune as prune

def apply_unstructured_pruning(model, amount=0.5):
    for name, module in model.named_modules():
        if isinstance(module, (nn.Conv2d, nn.Linear)):
            prune.l1_unstructured(module, name='weight', amount=amount)
    return model

After running it, each pruned layer carries the two new attributes, and you can check they are there: 

model_pruned.layer1[0].conv1.weight_orig   # untouched original weights
model_pruned.layer1[0].conv1.weight_mask   # 1s where weights survive, 0s where pruned

If you are happy with the result and want to make it permanent, one call bakes the mask into the weights and deletes the extra bookkeeping: 

prune.remove(module, 'weight')

What happens when you prune half a ResNet

The full experiment, data loading and all, lives in the companion notebook. The setup is a ResNet-18 trained on STL-10. Applying the function above with amount=0.5 gives:

Total params: 11,172,032
Zeroed (pruned) params: 5,586,016 (50.00%)

And the accuracy:

Accuracy on STL10 test set: 92.58% -> 66.25%

Half the weights gone, accuracy fell off a cliff. That is expected: the surviving weights were trained assuming the pruned ones would do their share of the work.

Getting it back with fine-tuning

The fix is to let the survivors adjust. We retrain just the final classifier layer for 15 epochs with plain SGD, so the network learns to compensate for everything that was cut: 

optimizer = torch.optim.SGD(model_pruned.fc.parameters(), lr=1e-3, momentum=0.9)

model.train()
for epoch in range(15):
    for x, y in train_loader:
        optimizer.zero_grad()
        loss = criterion(model_pruned(x), y)
        loss.backward()
        optimizer.step()

The loss falls from 0.40 to 0.23 across the 15 epochs, and when we evaluate again:

Accuracy on STL10 test set: 92.74%

Fully recovered, slightly above where we started, with half the weights pruned. This is the standard pruning workflow: cut aggressively, fine-tune to heal, and optionally quantize afterward to shrink the model even further.

Caveats

  • Do not prune batch norm layers. They keep training stable and cost almost nothing.
  • ResNets need care: residual connections constrain which channels structured pruning is allowed to remove.
  • Sparse does not mean fast. Zeroed weights are still multiplied at inference time unless your backend has sparse kernels. Torch and ONNX compilers can help here.
