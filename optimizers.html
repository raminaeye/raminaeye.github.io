The simplest fit is a line

Fitting a Line with Least Squares

How do you fit a line through a list of data points? Obvious question, and a perfect warm-up for optimizers. You want parameters for the line, and each point misses the line by its residual: how far the point's height sits above or below the line's height at that x. Least squares picks the parameters that minimize the sum of those squared misses.

It squares the error so it penalizes larger errors more. It gives a nice, smooth, convex function, and for linear models, there is a closed-form solution: set the partial derivatives to zero, the normal equations, and out drops a formula. The slope is how x and y move together divided by how much x moves on its own, and the intercept puts the line through the average point. One signature of the fit: the residuals always sum to zero, the line cannot be tilted to reduce the total error any further.

Least squares has a closed-form answer: set the derivatives to zero and read off the formula. Neural nets get no such luxury, so we walk downhill instead. Here is what breaks when you walk it the plain way, and the fix each optimizer adds.

Each optimizer fixes a failure of plain SGD

Gradient Descent and SGD

Gradient descent walks the parameters downhill. Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) does it on a random minibatch instead of the whole dataset: same update idea, noisier gradient, way faster per step.

This makes it noisy but much faster and allows for escaping local minima. It can be sensitive to the learning rate; too large, and the optimization path can zigzag or diverge, and too small would make it very slow. Its learning rate is the same across all parameters; there’s no adaptation. With a constant learning rate, SGD will bounce around near a local minimum. The stochasticity helps escape saddle points and poor local minima.

A saddle point is a point in the parameter space where the gradient is zero, and the optimizer might think it’s a stationary point, but it’s not a good minimum. Along one direction, the surface curves up like a minimum, and along another, it curves down like a maximum, which makes it a saddle. High-dimensional loss surfaces are full of saddle points. SGD can shake itself loose from saddles, while full-batch GD may get stuck because there’s no noise.

SGD with Momentum

Momentum smooths the zigzag path of SGD by accumulating a running average of past gradients, a velocity, so updates keep a more consistent direction.

The momentum coefficient (typically 0.9) controls the smoothing: higher means you carry more velocity from the past, like rolling downhill with less friction. The velocity builds up, and the magnitude of the step can actually be bigger than with plain SGD. It’s slower to react but faster to converge in a consistent direction.

Nesterov Accelerated Gradient (NAG)

With NAG, you look ahead in the direction of the velocity, then you compute the gradient direction, which helps with preventing overshooting. You “pretend” to take the gradient of the parameters already moved in the direction of your current velocity and take the gradient of that.

So the gradient is not at your position but at a future-leaning position where momentum would have taken you. The update itself is momentum's, using that lookahead gradient in place of the plain one.

It basically glances in the direction you’re already moving, then adjusts based on the slope there. It’s anticipating where the momentum is taking you. It’s like an early warning if the slope is flattening or turning so you can correct sooner. With vanilla momentum, you say, “I’m here, what’s the slope?” With Nesterov, you say, “I’m about to be over there, what’s the slope? Let me adjust my push so I don’t overshoot.” This makes for a more stable, less oscillatory, and faster adjustment when the optimum is close.

  1. 1. Momentum measures the slope at your feet, then steps. Nesterov first drifts to where momentum is already taking you.
  2. 2. It measures the slope there instead. The lookahead sees the flattening coming, so it brakes earlier and overshoots less.

Momentum asks "I am here, what is the slope?" Nesterov asks "I am about to be over there, what is the slope?" Same update otherwise.

AdaGrad

The next batch of optimizers gives every parameter its own step size: adaptive learning rates. A rare feature that barely ever fires wants a big step when it finally does; a feature that fires constantly wants small, careful steps. AdaGrad adapts each parameter's learning rate from the history of its gradients. For each parameter, you keep an accumulator: a cumulative sum of squared gradients. The update scales each parameter's step by its accumulated history, and over time the accumulator just keeps growing; it never shrinks.

It basically uses the L2-norm of the gradient history to normalize the gradient update for that parameter. For parameters that consistently see large gradients (steep directions), the denominator grows, and the steps shrink, preventing runaway updates. Parameters that see small gradients will have a larger effective learning rate so they can still learn. However, there’s a downside: the denominator grows monotonically, so the learning rate shrinks forever. AdaGrad remembers everything forever and because of this can suffer from a diminishing learning rate.

RMSProp

Instead of a running sum, RMSProp uses an exponentially decaying average of squared gradients. Older gradients decay away exponentially, so they don’t dominate forever. Now the denominator tracks the more recent scale of the gradients, not the entire history. If the problem shifts and we get bigger or smaller gradients, the optimizer can adapt more quickly. This can help with both exploding and vanishing gradients.

Adam (Adaptive Moment Estimation)

Adam combines momentum from SGD with adaptive learning rates from RMSProp. For each parameter it tracks two running averages: the first moment (the mean of gradients, like momentum) and the second moment (the mean of squared gradients, like RMSProp). There’s a problem with the exponential moving average (EMA): at the very first step, the moments are initialized to zero. Adam corrects this with a bias correction that inflates the moments back to realistic values early on; as the step count grows, the correction goes away.

The update then divides the smoothed gradient by the square root of the smoothed squared gradient, so each parameter gets its own well-scaled step.

AdamW, Nadam, SAM: variations on the themes

AdamW (Adam with Weight Decay)

Adam and most other optimizers implement L2 regularization by adding the gradient of the L2 norm to the loss gradient. This is then used in all the first and second moment updates. However, because it was added inside the gradient calculation, the second moment, which contributes to the scaling factor, also includes the L2 norm, distorting the effect of weight decay because it’s being adaptively rescaled.

AdamW keeps a moving average of only the true gradients (without the L2 term) and subtracts the weight decay directly from the update, separately, so it is never adaptively rescaled. So remember that weight decay is not L2 regularization for adaptive methods; that’s why there’s a separate AdamW optimizer.

Nadam (Nesterov-accelerated Adam)

Nadam combines the lookahead moment from Nesterov with the Adam optimizer: take Adam's update rule, but blend the current gradient back into the momentum term.

This isn’t much like Nesterov, where we actually took a step in a direction and used that slope to update our parameters; it’s just blending the current gradient (again) with the smoothed momentum term. It’s like short-circuiting the dilution in the smoothed mean by adding a stronger dose of the current slope so it can react more quickly.

SAM (Sharpness-Aware Minimization)

SAM is not really an optimizer. It is a wrapper you bolt onto one. Plain optimizers chase the lowest training loss and can land in a sharp minimum that generalizes badly. SAM hunts for flat minima instead: valleys where the loss stays low even if you wiggle the weights a little.

It’s minimizing the worst-case loss in a small neighborhood: look for the worst-case wiggle of the weights within a small radius, then update to reduce that worst-case loss. The optimizer prefers parameters that sit in a flat valley. It’s asking, "Would the loss remain low if I wiggle my weights a little bit?" SAM computes the raw gradient, uses that to form the perturbation, then temporarily shifts the weights and computes the gradient there. SAM is usually used with AdamW. AdamW runs as usual but instead of the original gradient g, it uses the SAM gradient to update its moments.

  1. 1. Sharp: the walls are steep, so a small wiggle of the weights jumps the loss.
  2. 2. Flat: the floor is wide, so the same wiggle barely moves the loss. SAM prefers this one.

SAM hunts the flat valley: jiggle the weights and the loss stays low. Caveat: you can reparameterize a network to make any minimum look sharper or flatter without changing what it computes, so flatness is a useful heuristic, not a guarantee.

Two training extras: schedulers and clipping

Learning Rate Schedulers

One learning rate for the whole run is almost never right. Too high and training blows up; too low and it crawls. Schedulers fix that by moving the learning rate around as training goes.

  • Step Decay: Drops the learning rate by a factor every few epochs. Each drop multiplies the rate by a fixed decay factor, once per block of steps.
  • Exponential Decay: Instead of sudden drops, the learning rate decays smoothly every step/epoch, shrinking by a fixed fraction each step.
  • Cosine Annealing: Instead of decreasing stepwise or exponentially, cosine annealing makes the learning rate follow a cosine curve, gliding from the maximum learning rate down to the minimum over the full schedule. The exponential decay can shrink the learning rate too early and cause the model to settle down too soon, so it gets harder to escape suboptimal minima. With cosine annealing, the learning rate stays higher for longer and encourages exploration.
  • Warm restarts: Cosine annealing can be combined with warm restarts: after it reaches the minimum learning rate, it resets back to the maximum and decays again to explore multiple basins.
  • One-Cycle Policy: Instead of just decreasing the learning rate, it increases it first and then decreases it in a single cycle. This helps the model escape bad minima early and then settle into flat minima later. It’s typically paired with a momentum schedule that does the opposite (decreases momentum while the learning rate increases). When we have a larger learning rate and lower momentum, it encourages the model to take large gradient steps, leading to lots of exploration and rapid movements.

Gradient Clipping

Gradient clipping is the emergency brake for exploding gradients. Pick a threshold c: if the gradient's norm is bigger than c, shrink the whole vector down to c. Same direction, sane size.

The model trains now: it descends without zigzagging off a cliff, and the schedule keeps the step size sane. Given that it trains, how far does scale take you? How much loss the next dollar buys: Scaling Laws.
