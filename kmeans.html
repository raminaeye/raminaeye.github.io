k-Means Clustering

Find the clumps.

k-means automates the clump-finding your eyes do

Imagine I hand you a thousand dots on a blank page. No labels, no legend, no colors. Sort them into two piles, I say. You would not hesitate. Your eyes see which dots belong together before you have finished reading the sentence. You never learned a "pile detector". You just see the clumps.

k-means is the algorithm version of that glance, and it runs on maybe the simplest loop in all of machine learning: guess some centers, let every point join its nearest center, move each center to the middle of its joiners, repeat. When I first built this, I tried it on two crescent moons tangled together. I gave the toy two datasets that teach more than moons: round clumps, where k-means shines, and long streaks, where it falls on its face. The failure is the lesson.

Assign points, move centers, repeat

That is the entire algorithm, and I am not simplifying. Step one: every point picks the nearest center. Step two: every center jumps to the middle of the points that picked it. Nobody trains a network, nobody computes a gradient, nobody tunes a learning rate. The centers just walk, round by round, until they stop moving.

When I built this, I ran it on 5,000 noisy moon points and the centers settled after a handful of rounds. The toy uses 600 points so your browser stays happy, and two honest datasets instead of moons: three round delivery zones, and two long delivery routes.

Assign, move, then check whether anything still moves

Two steps. First, the assignment: every point joins its nearest center, measured by squared distance, not plain distance. The square root is monotonic, so skipping it changes nothing about who is nearest, and it saves a fortune over thousands of points. This is a nearest-neighbor vote, nothing fancier.

Second, the move: every center jumps to the middle of its members, the average position of the points that picked it this round. If a center somehow loses every point, it stays put rather than dividing by zero (my build guards this too).

Then you check whether anything is still moving. My first pass stopped when the centers' mean movement dropped to 0.01 or below. My tool stops when even the most restless center moves 0.01 or less in a round. Either way, the loop ends the moment the centers get bored.

Where k-means shines, and where it fails

When I built this, I made two noisy moons, dropped two random centers on them, and let Lloyd's algorithm walk. The numpy run settles at epoch 5, the PyTorch rerun at epoch 7; same idea, different random starts. On my zones with 3 centers, the walk ends with one center per zone, sitting dead center.

This is k-means' home turf: round, separated, roughly equal clumps. Now switch to the routes with 2 centers. The two centers end up parked on the middle line, each claiming the left or right half of both routes. k-means has no notion of a margin. It only knows nearest center, so it chops every route in half and hands each driver the wrong halves.

One thing worth pocketing: the boundary k-means draws between the clumps looks like a logistic or linear regression decision boundary. Same straight-line energy, arrived at with zero labels.

The toy's arithmetic is checked against my own implementation: every point joins its nearest center, every center lands on its members' average, and squared distance is used throughout.

Here is the loop I built, stripped to the parts that matter:

def k_means(data, num_clusters, epochs, center_thr):
    # start: pick num_clusters random data points as centers
    centers = data[np.random.choice(num_samples, num_clusters, replace=False)]
    for epoch in range(epochs):
        # step 1: every point joins its nearest center
        distances = np.sum((data[:, None] - centers[None, :]) ** 2, axis=2)
        assignments = np.argmin(distances, axis=1)
        previouse_centers = copy.deepcopy(centers)
        # step 2: every center jumps to its members' mean
        for cluster_idx in range(num_clusters):
            cluster_data = data[assignments == cluster_idx]
            if len(cluster_data) > 0:
                centers[cluster_idx] = cluster_data.mean(axis=0)
        # stop when the centers barely move
        if check_center_movement(centers, previouse_centers, center_thr) and epoch > 3:
            break
    new_clusters = assignments
    return new_clusters, centers

Here is that loop, assign points, move centers, repeat, written as the two lines Assign, move, then check whether anything still moves describes.

$$ c_i = \arg\min_j \|x_i - \mu_j\|^2 $$
$$ \mu_j = \mathrm{mean}\{x_i : c_i = j\} $$

Here xi is one data point, μj is a cluster center, and ci is point i's assigned cluster. argmin means the j giving the smallest value, ‖‖ is the norm: length of, and ² is squared: the value times itself, the squared distance the steps above use. mean{} is the average of the points that joined the cluster. The first line assigns every point to its nearest center: pick the cluster whose center is closest. The second line moves each center to the middle of its assigned points: the average of everything that joined it.

Two steps until nobody moves

This is Lloyd's algorithm on two datasets: the zones first, then the routes. Each beat is one half of one round: first the points pick their nearest center and recolor, then the centers jump to their new middles.

And k stays your call. The elbow method plots WCSS (within-cluster sum of squares: the total squared distance from every point to its center, the tightness score the tool shows) against k and looks for the bend where adding another center stops paying off; the silhouette score measures how much closer each point is to its own cluster than to its nearest neighboring cluster. Both are rules of thumb, not answers. k is a judgment call about the problem, not the data alone.

How to play

  • The point: two steps repeated until nobody moves. Every point picks its nearest center, every center jumps to the middle of its members.
  • Round zones / Long routes: three round clumps, where a center lands dead in each zone, versus two long streaks, where the same loop chops each route in half because k-means only knows nearest center. The failure is the lesson.
  • Clusters k (2 to 4): the number of centers. On the zones, 4 centers split the east zone in two while the others hold.

Spread out the starting centers, and accept that k is your call

The starting centers are usually picked at random, and a bad start wastes rounds or traps the loop in a lopsided split. k-means++ (Arthur et al. 2007) changes only the seeding: the first center is picked uniformly at random, and each next center is picked with probability proportional to its squared distance from the nearest center already picked. Far-flung candidates win more often, so the centers start spread out, and the Lloyd loop itself never changes. The payoff is fewer bad starts and fewer wasted rounds.
