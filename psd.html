Power Spectral Density

How a signal’s power spreads over frequency, and how to measure it.

Tape hiss and mains hum are both noise, but hiss spreads its power evenly over every frequency while hum puts nearly all of it at 60 Hz (50 Hz in most of the world) and its multiples. The power spectral density (PSD) tells them apart. It is one curve, the signal’s power at each frequency per hertz, measured once per clip, and it is the right picture for noise, which has no fixed waveform but a steady spread of power. One FFT of the whole recording, the periodogram, gives a very jumpy curve; Welch’s method averages the spectra of many short windowed segments, which steadies the curve at the cost of frequency detail.

1 Power per hertz

The PSD Px(f) says how much of a signal’s power sits near each frequency f. Dividing by the bin width makes it a density, so the same sound gives the same curve whatever N or fs you measure it with.

The area under the PSD is the signal’s power.
∫ Px(f) df = 1N Σ x[n]²
Added up over every frequency from 0 to fs/2 on linear axes, the power per hertz gives back the power, the mean of the N squared samples x[n]. This is Parseval’s theorem, which says power is the same counted in time or in frequency (Fourier Transform and the FFT). The unit of Px(f) is power per hertz, V²/Hz, or dB/Hz on a log axis.

The PSD gives a converter’s noise floor (quantization noise is flat, ADC Fundamentals), the SNR in each band and where hum sits.

2 The periodogram is noisy

The simplest estimate takes one FFT of the whole recording of N samples, squares the magnitude and scales it.

The periodogram is one FFT, squared, per hertz.
P̂x(fk) = 2 |X[k]|²fs N
The hat marks an estimate; Px without it is the true PSD being estimated. |X[k]|² is the power the FFT finds at bin k, whose frequency is fk = k fs/N.
Dividing by fs N, the sampling rate times the number of samples, turns it into power per hertz, so the level does not depend on N.
The 2 makes it one-sided, from 0 to fs/2. A real signal’s negative frequencies mirror its positive ones, so each bin also carries its mirror’s power. Bin 0 and bin N/2 have no mirror and are not doubled.

For noise this estimate is ragged. Each value jumps by about its own size, and a longer recording only gives more bins, each just as jumpy. Welch (1967) shows that for Gaussian noise the variance of each value is about Px², whatever the length.

3 Welch averages short windowed pieces

Welch’s fix cuts the recording into K segments, windows each (Hann here), and averages their periodograms.

One segment’s windowed periodogram; the N samples are cut into K segments of L = N/K samples, so the FFT length is L.
vu[n] = w[n] xu[n]
xu[n] is segment u, samples n = 0 to L − 1, faded by the window w[n], so the cut edges do not spray energy across far bins.
Xu[k] = Σn vu[n] e−j2πkn/L
The FFT of the faded segment vu, with L bins.
P̂u(fk) = 2 |Xu[k]|²fs Σn w[n]²
The bottom replaces fs N, because the window takes energy out; with w[n] = 1 it is fs L, the plain periodogram of the segment. The 2 is the same one-sided fold, again except bins 0 and L/2.
Welch’s average and how much it steadies the estimate.
P̂x(f) = 1K Σu=1K P̂u(f)
The estimate is the average of the K segment periodograms; u counts segments (j stays the imaginary unit). Each segment sees the same true PSD with its own random jitter, so the jitter averages out while the true shape stays.
spread of P̂x ≈ Px√K
K independent copies, each as jumpy as Px itself, average to a curve about 1/√K as jumpy (Welch’s method gives variance Px²/K for segments that do not overlap).
Each segment has only L samples, so its bins are fs/L wide, K times wider, and close peaks blur together (Figure 6). It is the same trade-off as the window length in The STFT and Spectrograms.

4 PSD and autocorrelation

The autocorrelation measures how much a signal looks like itself shifted by a lag τ. A hum that repeats every 1/60 s matches itself at every multiple of that lag. The PSD is the Fourier transform of the autocorrelation (Wiener 1930, Khinchin 1934).

Passing a signal through a filter multiplies its PSD by |H(f)|², the squared magnitude response from How FIR and IIR Filters Shape Magnitude and Phase. That is why the noise in Figure 5 slopes down like the one-pole low-pass that made it.

5 In practice

In practice a PSD reads a noise floor, finds hum, or learns the noise that the denoising pages remove.

Rates, state and cost come from the sources in References.

Welch's estimate and the plain periodogram of the same noise; the last line prints each curve's spread relative to its level.

import numpy as np
from scipy import signal

FS, L = 16000, 512
x = np.random.default_rng(0).standard_normal(2 * FS)
f, P_welch = signal.welch(x, fs=FS, nperseg=L)
f1, P_one = signal.periodogram(x, fs=FS)
print(P_welch.std() / P_welch.mean(), P_one.std() / P_one.mean())
