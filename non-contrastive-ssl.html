Non-Contrastive Self-Supervised Learning

As in Contrastive Learning, two random edits of a photo, its views, become embeddings, and training pulls that positive pair together, but with no negatives (other images pushed away). Every training step, one network learns from the batch while its target comes from a momentum encoder (a copy whose weights follow a moving average of the network’s) or from the network itself with the gradient stopped. The output is a trained encoder; each method here adds one part that stops every image from getting the same output.

1 Pulling pairs together with no negatives should collapse

If the only signal is make the two views match, the easiest way to make every pair match is one vector for every image, called collapse; every pair matches and the embedding says nothing. Negatives forbid that answer; without them, nothing in the loss does.

A subtler dimensional collapse, plotted by the spectrum tool on the Failure Modes page, keeps only a few directions.

2 BYOL predicts the output of a slowly updated copy of itself

BYOL (Bootstrap Your Own Latent; latent means the embedding space) trains one network to predict a momentum encoder’s embedding of the other view, uses no negatives, and still does not collapse. Its trained online network (encoder, projector, predictor) turns view 1 into a prediction p1. The target network (encoder and projector only) turns view 2 into a target z2. It gets no gradients and is an exponential moving average (EMA) of the online weights, moving a small part of the way toward them after every step.

BYOL’s loss is the squared distance between the unit-length prediction and the unit-length target, then the same with the views swapped, summed.
L = ‖p̄1 − z̄2‖2
The bar scales a vector to unit length, so p̄1 and z̄2 are the prediction and the target as directions. The loss L is the squared length (‖ ‖2) of the gap between them. Unit length makes the loss depend only on direction, so the network cannot lower it by shrinking its outputs toward zero, and prediction and target are compared on one scale whatever their raw sizes.
= 2 − 2·⟨p̄1, z̄2⟩
Expanding the square of two unit vectors leaves 2 minus twice their dot product, written with angle brackets ⟨ , ⟩. For unit vectors that dot product is the cosine of the angle between them, so L is 0 when the two agree and 4 when they point opposite ways, and lowering L is the same as raising the cosine. The code computes it in this form.

The target follows MoCo’s momentum rule. Each step it keeps a share m of its old weights, and m rises from 0.996 to 1.

The snippet defines byol_loss in the second line of the equation and adds both directions on toy vectors. cosine_similarity scales both vectors to unit length itself, so the norms in the equation need no separate step.

import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

def byol_loss(p, z):
    cos = F.cosine_similarity(p, z, dim=-1)
    return (2 - 2 * cos).mean()               # batch mean

B, D = 8, 256
p1, p2 = torch.randn(B, D), torch.randn(B, D)   # online predictions
z1, z2 = torch.randn(B, D), torch.randn(B, D)   # targets
L = byol_loss(p1, z2) + byol_loss(p2, z1)
  1. 1The loss in eq line 2, 2 − 2 cos.
  2. 2Toy predictions p and targets z.
  3. 3Both directions added.

With no negatives to collect, BYOL needs no big batch.

3 Three parts hold collapse off

Three parts keep BYOL from collapsing, and removing any one of them shows it. BYOL’s authors removed the predictor and changed how the target updates; SimSiam removed the stop-gradient.

  1. The stop-gradient treats the target’s output as fixed, so the two sides cannot walk together to a constant.
  2. The predictor, on the online side only, learns to guess the target from the online output. A constant output gives it nothing to guess from, so the authors argue training moves away from it (BYOL sec. 3.2).
  3. The EMA target lags the online network, which the authors argue keeps the predictor close to its best guess (BYOL sec. 3.2).

4 SimSiam shows a stop-gradient and a predictor are enough

SimSiam (Simple Siamese networks) uses one network for both views, with no momentum encoder and no negatives. As in BYOL, each view gives an output z and a prediction p, and each prediction is compared with the other view’s output behind a stop-gradient. The stop-gradient is written sg.

SimSiam’s loss asks each view’s prediction to point the same way as the other view’s output, with that output held fixed.
D(p, z) = −cos(p, z)
D is the negative cosine similarity, −1 when two vectors point the same way and 1 when they point opposite ways. Lowering it turns p toward z, and only direction counts, as with BYOL’s unit vectors.
L = 12D(p1, sg(z2))
L takes half of D for view 1’s prediction against view 2’s output. sg treats its contents as a constant, so gradients flow only through the prediction. The network can move its prediction toward the other view’s output but cannot move that output toward the prediction, which removes the easy path where both sides drift together to one constant.
+ 12D(p2, sg(z1))
The second half swaps the views, so each view is once the predictor and once the fixed target.

Removing the predictor collapses it too (0.1%). SimSiam’s authors read it like k-means, which holds the targets fixed, fits to them, then refreshes them; that is why the gradient stops at the target.

5 DINO matches a student to a centered, sharpened teacher

DINO (self-distillation with no labels) trains a student network to copy a teacher, a momentum encoder of the student (MoCo’s momentum rule). Two edits to the teacher’s output stop collapse. Each network has K outputs. A softmax turns them into shares that sum to 1, and dividing by a temperature first sets how peaked the shares are, as in Knowledge Distillation but turned low for the teacher. The student matches the teacher’s shares by cross entropy.

The teacher turns an image into target shares that the student learns to match; it subtracts a running center, then sharpens with a low temperature.
Pt(x) = softmax(gt(x) − cτt)
x is one image. gt(x) is the teacher’s K raw outputs for it. The center c is a running average of the teacher’s outputs over recent batches. τt is the teacher’s small temperature. Pt is the softmax of the result, the teacher’s shares. Subtracting c (centering) removes what every image has in common, so no single output can win for every image; on its own it pushes the shares toward flat. Dividing by the small τt (sharpening) stretches the remaining gaps, so each image’s top output stands out; on its own it lets one output win everywhere. Together they leave each image its own peak, which the student copies. The student uses its own, higher temperature and no centering.

Figure 8. Three toy images, K = 8 outputs; the center is their mean.

A ViT-S/16 (a small vision transformer that reads 16 px patches) trained this way reaches 77.0% linear probe on ImageNet and 74.5% by a 20-neighbor weighted vote (nearest neighbors).

6 BYOL, SimSiam and DINO in practice

One BYOL step, reusing byol_loss. After the optimizer step, the target moves toward the online network by EMA, with m rising from 0.996 to 1 on a cosine schedule over training. PyTorch has no LARS, so SGD stands in. A SimSiam step drops the target and the EMA and feeds online(x) through detach() as the target.

import copy, math
import torch
import torch.nn as nn

def mlp(d_in, d_out=256):
    return nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(d_in, 4096), nn.BatchNorm1d(4096),
                         nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(4096, d_out))

encoder = nn.Sequential(nn.Flatten(), nn.Linear(3 * 32 * 32, 512))
online = nn.Sequential(encoder, mlp(512))       # encoder + projector
predictor = mlp(256)
target = copy.deepcopy(online).requires_grad_(False)
params = [*online.parameters(), *predictor.parameters()]
opt = torch.optim.SGD(params, lr=0.2, momentum=0.9)
k, K_STEPS, M_BASE = 0, 1000, 0.996             # step k

x1, x2 = torch.rand(64, 3, 32, 32), torch.rand(64, 3, 32, 32)
p1, p2 = predictor(online(x1)), predictor(online(x2))
with torch.no_grad():
    z1, z2 = target(x1), target(x2)
L = byol_loss(p1, z2) + byol_loss(p2, z1)
opt.zero_grad()
L.backward()
opt.step()

m = 1 - (1 - M_BASE) * (math.cos(math.pi * k / K_STEPS) + 1) / 2
with torch.no_grad():
    for p_t, p_o in zip(target.parameters(), online.parameters()):
        p_t.mul_(m).add_(p_o, alpha=1 - m)
  1. 1Online network, predictor, target copy and optimizer, as in Figure 3.
  2. 2Predictions, no-gradient targets and the loss, eq lines 1 and 2.
  3. 3Updates the online side only.
  4. 4Cosine schedule for m and the target EMA.

A DINO step, reusing the imports and the views x1, x2 from the BYOL step, has the same shape: the student learns, then the teacher takes an EMA with rate λ, and the center c moves toward the batch mean of the teacher outputs with rate 0.9.

import torch.nn.functional as F

def dino_loss(s_out, t_out, c, tau_s=0.1, tau_t=0.04):
    P_t = F.softmax((t_out - c) / tau_t, dim=-1)     # center, sharpen
    log_P_s = F.log_softmax(s_out / tau_s, dim=-1)
    return -(P_t * log_P_s).sum(dim=-1).mean()

K_OUT, LAM, M_C = 1024, 0.996, 0.9
student = nn.Sequential(nn.Flatten(), nn.Linear(3 * 32 * 32, K_OUT))
teacher = copy.deepcopy(student).requires_grad_(False)
c = torch.zeros(K_OUT)
opt = torch.optim.AdamW(student.parameters(), lr=5e-4)

s1, s2 = student(x1), student(x2)
with torch.no_grad():
    t1, t2 = teacher(x1), teacher(x2)
L = (dino_loss(s1, t2, c) + dino_loss(s2, t1, c)) / 2
opt.zero_grad()
L.backward()
opt.step()
with torch.no_grad():
    for p_t, p_s in zip(teacher.parameters(), student.parameters()):
        p_t.mul_(LAM).add_(p_s, alpha=1 - LAM)
    c = M_C * c + (1 - M_C) * torch.cat([t1, t2]).mean(dim=0)
  1. 1Loss with centered, sharpened teacher shares, the eq line in Section 5.
  2. 2Student, teacher copy, center c and optimizer.
  3. 3Both networks on both views, then the loss.
  4. 4Updates the student only.
  5. 5Teacher EMA with rate λ.
  6. 6Moves c toward the batch mean of teacher outputs.
