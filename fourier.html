Fourier Transform and the FFT

Time and frequency, and what the basis looks like.

The Fourier transform turns a list of samples into a list of frequencies: for each one, how much of it the signal holds and where its wave starts. The fast Fourier transform (FFT) computes it once per clip, or once per block of samples for a long signal, and the inverse turns the frequencies back into the same samples.

1 A signal and its spectrum

The FFT of N samples gives one complex value X[k] per frequency, called a bin. Plotted as 2|X[k]|/N, each tone reads its own amplitude.

The spectrum is one-sided: for a real signal the negative frequencies mirror the positive ones, so np.fft.rfft keeps only 0 to fs/2, N/2 + 1 = 501 bins. Here fs = 1,000 Hz and N = 1,000, so bin k sits at k fs/N = k Hz.

The NumPy code that draws Figure 1.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

fs, N = 1000, 1000                       # 1 s at 1,000 samples per second
t = np.arange(N) / fs
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
tones = 0.7 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * 50 * t) + np.sin(2 * np.pi * 120 * t)
x = tones + 0.2 * rng.standard_normal(N)
X = np.fft.rfft(x)                       # bins 0 to N/2
f_k = np.fft.rfftfreq(N, d=1 / fs)       # k * fs / N, in Hz
fig, (top, bottom) = plt.subplots(2, 1)
top.plot(t[:100], x[:100])
bottom.plot(f_k, 2 * np.abs(X) / N)
bottom.set_xlabel("frequency (Hz)")
plt.show()
  1. 1Makes the signal of Figure 1, marker 1: two sines and a little noise, fs = 1,000 Hz and N = 1,000.
  2. 2Takes the FFT, keeping bins 0 to N/2 because the rest mirror them, and the frequency k fs/N of each bin.
  3. 3Draws the time view and 2|X[k]|/N, which reads each tone’s amplitude, markers 2 to 4.

2 The basis waves

How do you find which tones are inside? Hold up a set of basis waves, one at a time, and test how much of each one the signal contains. For N samples there are N basis waves.

Each basis wave has a cosine part and a sine part, because a tone can start at any point of its cycle. One complex number holds both.

One number that holds a cosine and a sine.
ejθ = cos θ + j sin θ
j is the square root of −1. As θ grows, ejθ walks round the unit circle; its real part, cos θ, is the dot's shadow on the horizontal axis and its j part, sin θ, is its height. One complex number therefore carries a cosine and a sine at once.

This is a change of basis, the same data described along new axes, as in Principal Component Analysis (PCA). PCA learns its axes from the data; the Fourier basis is the same fixed set of waves for every signal.

3 The DFT

The discrete Fourier transform (DFT) measures this.

The DFT and its inverse.
X[k] = Σn=0N−1 x[n] e−j2πkn/N
Basis wave k is e−j2πkn/N, k full turns across the N samples. Multiplying x[n] by it sample by sample and adding up gives a large total only when x turns at the same rate, so X[k] measures how much of wave k is in x, with its cosine and sine parts together.
x[n] = 1N Σk=0N−1 X[k] · ej2πkn/N
The inverse adds the N basis waves back, each weighted by its amount X[k], with the exponent's sign flipped so each wave turns the other way. The 1/N undoes the N-fold total that the forward sum built up.

The DFT as one matrix product. Row k of F is basis wave k, and the last line checks the product against np.fft.fft, which does not scale (np.fft.ifft divides by N).

import numpy as np

N = 8
n = np.arange(N)
k = n[:, None]                          # bins as a column
F = np.exp(-2j * np.pi * k * n / N)     # shape (N, N)
x = np.random.default_rng(0).standard_normal(N)
print(np.allclose(F @ x, np.fft.fft(x)))
  1. 1Row k of F is basis wave k, so F @ x is the DFT (eq card in Section 3, line 1).
  2. 2Checks the matrix product against np.fft.fft on random input.

4 Magnitude and phase

Each X[k] is a complex number, so it has a size and an angle.

The two parts of each DFT value.
|X[k]|
|X[k]|, the magnitude, is the length of the complex number, how much of wave k there is; doubling a tone doubles it.
∠X[k]
∠X[k], the phase, is its angle, where in its cycle wave k starts; delaying the signal turns this angle and leaves the magnitude alone.

Because phase sets timing, How FIR and IIR Filters Shape Magnitude and Phase tracks what a filter does to it, and Spectral Subtraction for Denoising edits the magnitude and keeps the noisy phase.

5 Which bin is which frequency

The frequency of bin k.
fk = k fsN
Bin k makes k turns in N samples, and N samples last N/fs seconds, so its frequency is k fs/N. Bins sit fs/N apart, so more samples give finer spacing, and bin N/2 lands on the Nyquist frequency, fs/2.

For a real signal, bins above N/2 mirror the ones below, the same fold as in Aliasing and Anti-Aliasing Filters.

6 Frequency resolution

How finely a record of N samples separates frequencies.
Δf = fsN
Δf is the bin spacing, the sample rate fs split into N equal steps. At fs = 1,000 Hz, N = 1,000 gives 1 Hz bins and N = 100 gives 10 Hz bins. With no window, two tones split once they are about Δf apart or a little closer: the 4 Hz pair of Figure 6 is one peak at T = 0.15 s, where Δf is 6.7 Hz, and two peaks at 0.20 s, where it is 5 Hz.
Δf = 1T
N samples last T = N/fs seconds, so Δf is one over the record length, the frequency resolution: only a longer recording separates closer tones. Zero-padding appends zeros before the FFT, so bins sit closer than Δf and draw more points on the same curve, but T, the length of real data, does not change, so close peaks do not separate.

7 Back to time

A real signal rebuilt from its rfft bins.
x[n] = Σk Ak cos(ωkn + φk)
Every sample is a sum of cosines, one per bin from 0 to N/2. Bin k adds a cosine of height Ak that turns ωk radians per sample and starts at angle φk. All 501 bins give the samples back exactly; leaving a bin out removes that frequency from the result.
ωk = 2πkN
Bin k makes k full turns in N samples, the basis wave of Section 2. In hertz it is fk = k fs/N.
Ak = 2|X[k]|N
The height of wave k. The forward sum grows a tone N/2 times, and the other half of its size sits in the mirrored negative bin that rfft drops, so 2/N undoes both. Bins 0 and N/2 have no mirror and use |X[k]|/N.
φk = ∠X[k]
Where wave k starts, the angle of the complex bin. A sine starts a quarter turn after a cosine, so the 50 Hz sine comes back with φ = −90°.

Build a wave from basis waves

The round trip in NumPy, with the signal of Figure 1.

import numpy as np

fs, N = 1000, 1000
t = np.arange(N) / fs
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
x = 0.7 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * 50 * t) + np.sin(2 * np.pi * 120 * t) + 0.2 * rng.standard_normal(N)
X = np.fft.rfft(x)                       # 501 bins
x_back = np.fft.irfft(X, n=N)            # n=N keeps the original length
print(np.allclose(x, x_back), np.max(np.abs(x - x_back)))   # True 8.9e-16
  1. 1Makes the signal of Figure 1.
  2. 2Goes to the 501 bins (eq card in Section 3, line 1).
  3. 3Adds the waves back (eq card in Section 7, line 1); n=N keeps the length when N is odd.
  4. 4Prints True and the largest error, near machine precision.

Keep every magnitude and scramble the phases, and the same cosines peak at different moments and add to a different signal, the noise of Figure 5 instead of its click.

8 Why the FFT is fast

Done directly, the DFT costs N multiply-adds for each of N bins. The fast Fourier transform (FFT) gets the same numbers with far less work. Its trick is to split the samples into even and odd, do two half-size DFTs, and combine them. Each half splits again, until only pairs are left.

Each split halves the size, so there are log2 N stages (log2 N is how many times you can halve N before reaching 1), and each stage costs about N operations.

Operations for a direct DFT and for an FFT.
N2 → N log2 N
The direct DFT does N multiply-adds for each of N bins. The FFT halves the size log2 N times and each stage costs about N; Figure 10 plots both counts.

James Cooley and John Tukey published this algorithm in 1965; their version needs fewer than 2N log2 N operations. Every np.fft call uses an FFT.

9 Convolution becomes multiplication

A filter in time is a short list of weights that slides along the signal; each output is the weighted sum under it. That sliding sum is called convolution, written x ∗ h; a convolution layer computes the same sum (Convolutional Neural Networks). In frequency the same operation is much simpler, a bin-by-bin product of the two spectra.

Filtering in time and in frequency.
x ∗ h ↔ X · H
The ∗ is convolution, the weighted sum that slides along the signal, and ↔ links a signal to its spectrum. A filter only scales and shifts each basis wave, so in frequency the whole slide becomes one multiply per bin, X times H.

The DFT’s waves repeat every N samples, so the product gives a circular convolution, where the end of the output wraps onto the start. Zero-pad both signals to the full output length first.

10.1 Gotchas

  • Magnitudes grow with N; divide by N/2 to read a tone’s amplitude (Section 7).
  • rfft drops the mirror half, so power sums double bins 1 to N/2 − 1.
