1 When part of the data is hidden

When part of the data is hidden, the EM (expectation-maximization) algorithm fits the same score in two alternating steps. Expectation means taking the expected counts, the average over the soft guesses; maximization means refitting the numbers to maximize the score. EM runs once per fit, in rounds, over the whole training set each round; the current \(\theta\) is the state carried across rounds.

In the field this is Gaussian mixture soft clustering, where each point is shared between bell curves instead of handed to one cluster, and unigram piece probabilities, where EM sets the probabilities of pieces from every split of every sentence.

For background on the score this card fits, see Maximum Likelihood Estimation.

When some labels are hidden, plain counting stops working. Take two coins, A and B, with unknown biases (an example from Do and Batzoglou 2008). Five times, someone picks one and flips it 10 times. Each coin is picked equally often. With the labels you would just count: each coin’s bias is its share of heads over the flips it made.

Hide the labels and each step needs the other’s answer: counting needs the labels, and guessing the labels needs the biases.

In this card, \(P\) is a chance, \(x_i\) is one observation, and \(\theta\) is the model’s numbers.

With a hidden label, a sum lands inside the log, and the counting formula breaks.
\(\displaystyle \log L(\htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{\theta }) =\)
\(\displaystyle \sum_{i} \log \sum_{\htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{z}} P(x_{i}, \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{z} \mid \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{\theta })\)
P is the chance of one set x sub i with label z, under theta. Outer sum over sets, inner over labels z. A log of a sum does not split, so counting breaks. The peak has no closed formula, no direct write-down.

2 EM guesses, then alternates two easy steps

EM (Dempster, Laird and Rubin 1977) replaces each hidden label with a soft guess, refits \(\theta\) as if those guesses were data, and repeats. The E step uses Bayes rule to give each set a share for each coin. The M step counts heads with each set weighted by its share, the expected counts, and refits both biases.

Figure 2. From two close starting guesses the biases move apart and settle.

Each EM round credits every set to the two coins in proportion to how well each explains it, then refits each coin’s bias from the flips credited to it.
\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{a_{iA}} = w_{A} P(x_{i} \mid \htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{\theta _{A}})\)
E step. Each set scores under each coin: the coin weight times how well it explains the set flips. a sub iA is set i score under coin A; w sub A is how often A is picked. Theta sub A is coin A bias.
\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{r_{iA}} = \frac{\htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{a_{iA}}}{\htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{a_{iA}} + \htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{a_{iB}}}\)
E step, by Bayes rule, the rule that flips a conditional. r sub iA, the responsibility, is coin A's share of the two scores. Dividing by the total makes the shares add to 1. Near 0 or 1 it is close to a hard label.
\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{\theta _{A}} = \frac{\sum_{i} \htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{r_{iA}} h_{i}}{\sum_{i} \htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{r_{iA}} (h_{i} + t_{i})}\)
M step. The counting formula from the peak refits coin A: top sum adds credited heads, bottom sum credited flips. r sub iA is set i's share for A; h sub i heads, t sub i tails. Coin B uses 1 minus r sub iA.

Each round the log-likelihood rises; when one more round no longer moves the biases, EM has reached a peak.

3 What EM promises, and what it does not

No EM round lowers the likelihood; the 1977 paper proves it. EM does not promise the highest peak, only the nearest one, so run it from several starts and keep the best.

4 EM in practice

Builds on logs turning products into sums

Libraries run EM inside fit. This snippet is the EM loop for the five coin sets of Figure 1; it stops when one round gains less than tol in log-likelihood, or after max_iter rounds.

import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import binom
heads = np.array([5, 9, 8, 4, 7])
flips, tol, max_iter = 10, 1e-6, 100
theta = np.array([0.6, 0.5])
old_ll = -np.inf
for it in range(max_iter):
    a = 0.5 * binom.pmf(heads[:, None], flips, theta)
    ll = np.log(a.sum(axis=1)).sum()
    if ll - old_ll < tol:
        break
    old_ll = ll
    r = a / a.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)
    theta = r.T @ heads / (r.sum(axis=0) * flips)

  3. 3Sums the log-likelihood, eq card in Section 1, line 2, and stops when it gains less than tol.
  4. 4Computes the E-step shares \(r_{iA}\) and \(r_{iB}\), eq card in Section 2, line 2.
  5. 5Computes the M-step update of \(\theta\), eq card in Section 2, line 3.

5 Common traps

  • Equal starts never separate: both coins get half of every set, so they stay equal forever.
  • Labels can switch; swapping the names A and B leaves the likelihood unchanged. With \(K\) hidden groups there are \(K!\) (\(K\) factorial) equally good answers, one for each way of naming the groups.
  • The likelihood can run to infinity. In a Gaussian mixture a bell can shrink onto one point and grow without bound; keep a floor on its variance.
  • A bad start settles on a poor local peak; use several starts.
  • With too little data per hidden group, the fit chases noise; use more data per group or fewer groups.

6 What if the hidden labels are clusters in a cloud of points?

Swap coins for bell curves and flips for points, and EM fits a Gaussian mixture model, what the coin paper calls the soft variant of k-means. For a chain of hidden states it becomes Baum-Welch, which trains hidden Markov models.
