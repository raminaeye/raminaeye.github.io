Generative Models: What They Learn and How They Differ

How models turn noise into images, from generative adversarial networks (GANs) to diffusion.

Before you start Random variables Classifiers Sampling tokens Log-likelihood

A generative model learns to produce new samples that look like its training data. After seeing the Modified National Institute of Standards and Technology (MNIST) digit set (LeCun et al. 1998) it draws new handwritten digits. After training on a large set of captioned images, as Stable Diffusion does, it draws new images that match a caption. The families differ in three ways: how they turn random numbers into a sample, how many network calls one sample costs, and whether the model can say how likely an image is.

1 Families of generative models

Builds on sampling one token at a time (Softmax, Temperature and Decoding, section 4) and the log-likelihood (Maximum Likelihood and EM, section 4)

Four families cover nearly every image generator in use. This series follows the two in blue, generative adversarial networks (GANs) first and then diffusion.

The three answers in the scoring row follow the taxonomy of Goodfellow (2016): some models write down how likely an image is, and a standard GAN does not.

2 What a generative model learns

Builds on random variables and sampling (Probability and Bayes Rule, section 2) and a classifier (Logistic Regression, section 2)

A classifier and a generator run in opposite directions. A generator takes a handful of random numbers, called the noise, and returns an image. The GAN and diffusion pages draw these numbers from a Gaussian, a bell of random numbers, most near 0.

What does it mean for the generator to be good? Real digits are a tiny corner of all possible images. Most grids of 784 pixel values look like television static, and almost none look like a 7.

Training moves the model’s samples from static toward digits. That is the learning goal of every family on this page.

Make the model’s images as common, and as rare, as real ones.
pθ(x) ≈ pdata(x)
x is one image, 784 pixel values, so the line is about all images at once. pdata(x) is a density: high where real digits are common, near 0 on static, as in rows 1 and 2 of Figure 3. pθ(x) is the model’s density, and θ (theta) are its weights; the GAN pages write pg for it. Training changes θ until the model puts its density where the data puts its own. ≈ reads “close to”, and each family measures closeness its own way: a judge for GANs, whose score measures how far apart the two densities are (the Jensen-Shannon (JS) divergence, GANs, section 4), and a likelihood bound for diffusion (Diffusion Training, section 4).
pθ(x | y) ≈ pdata(x | y)
y is what we ask for, a digit label such as 7 or a text prompt. The bar | reads “given”, so this is line 1 restricted to the images that fit y, row 5 of Figure 3. A model that meets line 2 for every y draws what it is asked for, such as “a 7”.

Line 1 is the goal of the GAN value (GANs, section 2) and of the noise-prediction loss (Diffusion Training, section 1). Line 2 is the goal of the conditional value (GANs, section 8) and of guided diffusion (Guided Diffusion, section 1).

Figure 4. Noise goes in, the data’s shape comes out; training bends it.

A GAN or diffusion model does this for all 784 pixels of a digit at once.

3 Quality, diversity and speed

Three words come back on every page of this series.

  • Quality means each digit looks real.
  • Diversity means all ten classes appear, in many styles.
  • Speed means network calls per image, the calls row of Figure 1.

Guidance (Guided Diffusion, section 3) gives up some diversity for quality in trained networks (Ho and Salimans 2022).

The placements follow the trilemma figure of Xiao et al. (2022).

Speed and quality trade inside diffusion itself. A diffusion model removes noise a little at a time, and each removal is one network call. With too few calls, each jump lands on the average of every place the sample could still go, a blur between digits instead of one digit.

Figure 6. Few calls blur samples between the blobs; more calls land them on the data.

A GAN makes its sample in one call, which is why it sits on the speed corner of Figure 5.

4 The order of this series

GANs led image quality until diffusion passed them (Dhariwal and Nichol 2021). Diffusion began as a separate line of work, and it became popular partly because it avoids the GAN training problems that Training GANs, section 8 sets out.

How can a network learn to draw digits when no loss says what a good digit is? Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) answers it.
