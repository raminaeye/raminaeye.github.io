An optimizer turns each step's gradient into a weight update. It runs once per training step on one minibatch, a small random batch of training examples, and keeps a little state, such as momentum's velocity, between steps.

1 Gradient descent and SGD

Every figure in this series uses one loss, L = 5x² + 0.2y², started at (−3.5, 5). I call its shape, a long narrow valley, the ravine; it stands for the ill-conditioned loss of a real network, where one direction is far steeper than another. Its numbers are illustrative, computed by script. On the first step the steep direction strides a long way downhill while the flat direction barely moves.

Step downhill by an amount proportional to the slope (Gradient Descent, section 1.1).

The gradient descent step
θ ← θ − α·∇L(θ)
θ the weights; ∇L the uphill direction and its steepness; the minus turns it downhill; α converts slope into distance.

Steep places get big steps, flat places tiny ones. SGD computes ∇L on a minibatch instead of the whole data set, so each step is cheaper and a little noisy. Below, SGD fits the mean squared error of a linear model (Linear Regression, section 2).

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
X = rng.normal(size=(256, 3))
y = X @ rng.normal(size=3)
w = np.zeros(3)
lr, bs = 0.1, 32  # α, batch
for i in range(0, 256, bs):
    xb = X[i:i + bs]
    yb = y[i:i + bs]
    err = xb @ w - yb
    grad = 2 * xb.T @ err / bs
    w = w - lr * grad
  1. 1Data, zero weights, α and the batch size.
  2. 2Takes the next minibatch of rows.
  3. 3Slope of the batch's mean squared error.
  4. 4Steps every weight by the same α.

1.1 The steep direction sets the learning rate

Along one axis of curvature h (how fast the slope grows), each step multiplies the distance to the minimum by one number.

One axis at a time
xt+1 = xt − α·h·xt
This is the gradient descent step on one axis. xt is the distance from the minimum after t steps, and the slope there is h·xt.
xt+1 = (1 − α·h)·xt
So each step multiplies the distance by 1 − α·h: it flips the steep direction to the other side and barely shrinks the flat one. It must stay between −1 and 1, so α must stay below 2h, and the steepest axis sets that limit.

Even with the learning rate pushed right up against that limit, the flat direction still takes many, many steps to settle. One α cannot suit both axes; momentum below and per-axis step sizes (Adaptive Optimizers) are the two fixes.

2 Momentum

Momentum keeps a velocity v, a running sum of past gradients in which each older gradient is shrunk by the momentum factor μ every step.

Velocity
v ← μ·v + ∇L(θ)
μ·v is the memory of past pushes, shrunk by μ each step; ∇L is today's push.
vt = ∇Lt + μ·∇Lt−1 + μ²·∇Lt−2 + …
Written out, a push from k steps ago still counts μk. A steady gradient adds up to 11 − μ times itself, many times one push.

Sutskever et al. 2013 train deep networks with momentum 0.9 and higher, so the velocity can reach 10 times one gradient. On the ravine, that build-up plays out step by step: along the flat direction every step points the same way, so each step is bigger than the last. Along the steep direction the steps keep pushing the same way even after the weights cross the minimum, so they carry the weights past it before the velocity turns around.

The momentum step
θ ← θ − α·v
The gradient descent step with the velocity in place of today's gradient.

Directions that agree add up; directions that flip cancel. The same mechanism speeds y and makes x overshoot.

import numpy as np

theta = np.array([-3.5, 5.0])
h = np.array([10.0, 0.4])    # curvature of x and y
v = np.zeros(2)
lr, mu = 0.05, 0.9           # α, μ
for t in range(60):
    grad = h * theta
    v = mu * v + grad
    theta = theta - lr * v
  1. 1The ravine, its start, zero velocity, α and μ.
  2. 2Reads today's slope.
  3. 3Shrinks v by μ and adds the slope.
  4. 4Steps by α times the velocity.
With momentum near 1, the built-up step is many times α times the gradient.

2.1 Momentum keeps moving after the slope vanishes

The last pushed step sets the size of the glide. Once the slope is gone, each step is μ times the one before, so the steps shrink away and the glide fades.

How far momentum coasts
total coast = α·v·μ1 − μ
α·v is the last step; the steps after it are μ, μ², … times it, which sum to μ1 − μ times that last step, several more steps' worth of glide in all.

In multitask training a gradient balancer edits each task's gradient and can remove a direction; the same glide carries it back into the step (Gradient Balancing Inside the Training Loop, section 8.1).

3 Nesterov momentum

Nesterov reads the slope where the velocity is about to carry the weights, not where they are now.

Nesterov momentum
v ← μ·v + ∇L(θ − α·μ·v)
θ − α·μ·v is where the remembered velocity alone would take you; the gradient there is a preview.
θ ← θ − α·v
The step itself is momentum's, unchanged.

The brake starts one step earlier, because Nesterov reads a much gentler slope at the lookahead than momentum reads at θ. At a large learning rate Nesterov can diverge on this ravine while momentum does not, because its earlier reading of the steep slope makes it more sensitive to a large α.

4 Saddle points and minibatch noise

A saddle point
L(x, y) = x² − y²
Uphill along x, downhill along y.
∇L(0, 0) = (0, 0)
At the centre the gradient descent step is zero.

Exact gradient descent started on the x axis stops at the centre. A minibatch gradient is never exactly zero, so its noise nudges the weights along y and they escape. The DQN agent learns from batches of 64 earlier moves drawn at random from its memory, so every gradient it follows is this kind of noisy minibatch gradient (Deep Q-Networks, section 5).

5 SGD and momentum in PyTorch

Both run once per step; momentum keeps one buffer the size of the weights.

opt = torch.optim.SGD(model.parameters(), lr=0.05, momentum=0.9)
opt_nag = torch.optim.SGD(model.parameters(), lr=0.05, momentum=0.9, nesterov=True)

  2. 2The same with Nesterov momentum.
