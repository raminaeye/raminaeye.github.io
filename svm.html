Support Vector Machines (SVM)

The maximum-margin classifier, with interactive toys.

Support vector machines find the boundary with the most room on both sides.

Training repeats one gradient step over all the points, one pass over the data each (1,000 steps in the svm-pytorch notebook); the curved SVC later on the page solves once instead.

1 The maximum-margin classifier

An SVM picks, among the boundaries that separate the classes, the one farthest from the closest points. Two boundaries can both get every training point right; which do you trust on new data? The one with room to spare.

That boundary has the maximum margin, the most room on both sides before it touches a point, so a new point near the edge still lands on the right side.

Those three points are the support vectors: moving any other point leaves the boundary in place.

The decision line is where the score w·x + b is zero, and the margin lines are where it hits +1 and −1:

The margin's width depends only on the length of the weights.
margin width = 2‖w‖
‖w‖ is the length of the weight vector w. Bigger weights make the score change faster, so the ±1 lines come sooner and the margin narrows; halve the weights and the margin doubles. Widening the margin is exactly shrinking the weights.

With no violations allowed, the widest margin is this problem:

The widest separator: shrink the weights, keep every point on its own side.
min ½‖w‖2
min makes what follows as small as it can go: half the squared length of the weights. A smaller w means a wider margin, since the width is 2/‖w‖.
s.t. yi(w·xi + b) ≥ 1
s.t. reads "such that": every point's label yi (+1 or −1) times its score must reach at least 1, so each point sits on its own side, at least one unit past the line. xi is point i's features, the · is the dot product (multiply matching features, add them up), and b is the offset. The points that land exactly on 1 are the support vectors.

2 The hinge loss and C

Real data overlaps, so training allows violations and adds a cost for each. The SVM is a linear model trained with the hinge loss, which asks is the model right with room to spare? The label times the score is positive on the correct side, and past 1 the loss is zero.

The soft margin swaps the hard rule for that cost on each violation:

The hinge loss, and the objective that trades it against margin width.
fi = w·xi + b
fi is point i's raw score: the dot product of w and xi, plus the offset b.
hingei = max(0, 1 − yi·fi)
The shortfall 1 − yi·fi, floored at zero by max: zero once a point is safely past its margin line, and growing the deeper it sits inside the margin or past the line.
J = ½‖w‖2 + C·mean(hinge)
J adds the margin cost to C times the average hinge, the cost for every point that is wrong or too close. The two parts pull against each other: the first wants small weights and a wide margin, the second wants no point inside it. Raise C and violations cost more than width, so the margin narrows; lower it and the model widens the margin and tolerates violations. Near C = 0 only width counts and every point sits inside the margin; a huge C approaches the hard margin above. scikit-learn's SVC defaults to C = 1.0.

C is not a learning rate: a large C is weak regularization, a small C strong. Swap the hinge for the log loss and this is logistic regression.

The svm-pytorch notebook in Machine-Learning-Concepts runs gradient descent on J for 1,000 full passes with PyTorch's SGD. The hinge has a kink, so the step uses the subgradient: a point short of the margin nudges the weights toward its label, and every other point is left alone. The notebook also applies a GELU to the inputs first; the tool does not.

The hinge objective trained with SGD in PyTorch; labels y are −1 or +1.

import torch
lr, C, epochs = 0.01, 1.0, 1000
w = torch.zeros(2)
b = torch.zeros(1)
w.requires_grad_()
b.requires_grad_()
SGD = torch.optim.SGD
opt = SGD([w, b], lr=lr)
for epoch in range(epochs):
    f = X @ w + b
    gap = 1 - y * f
    hinge = gap.clamp(min=0)
    width = 0.5 * (w ** 2).sum()
    J = width + C * hinge.mean()
    opt.zero_grad()
    J.backward()
    opt.step()
  1. 1Starts w and b at 0 and sets up SGD.
  2. 2Scores f and hinge losses, lines 1 and 2 of the card in Section 2.
  3. 3Width term plus C times mean hinge, line 3.
  4. 4One gradient step.

5 Kernels and SVM variants

A kernel lets the same margin bend around curved data, and two variants reuse it for regression and anomalies.

5.1 The kernel trick

Nobody lifts by hand: a kernel scores each pair of points as if they had been lifted.

The kernel: a similarity score that stands in for the lift.
K(x, z) = φ(x)·φ(z)
φ (phi) is the lift, and K(x, z) is the dot product of two points x and z after it. The kernel computes that number straight from the original points, so the flat cut in the lifted space never needs the lifted coordinates.
The RBF kernel, and the polynomial kernel beside it.
K(x, z) = exp(−γ ‖x − z‖2)
K is a similarity score for a pair of points, x and z: e raised to minus γ times the squared distance between the pair, so nearby pairs score near 1 and far pairs fade toward 0. γ (gamma) sets how far each point's influence reaches: raise it and influence shrinks until the boundary bends around each single point; lower it and the boundary smooths toward a straight cut. scikit-learn's default, gamma = "scale", is 1 / (number of features × variance of the data).
K(x, z) = (x · z + c)d
The polynomial kernel scores a pair by their dot product plus a constant c, raised to the power d. Raising to the power d counts every product of up to d features, so d = 2 can draw ellipses and parabolas; c weighs the simpler terms against the highest power. A higher d bends more and overfits sooner; scikit-learn defaults to d = 3 and c = 0.

RBF (radial basis function) scores similarity to nearby points, like distance in k-nearest neighbors. A new point is classified by a weighted sum over the support vectors.

Every support vector contributes; its weight fades with distance.
f(x) = Σi αi yi K(xi, x) + b
The sum runs over the support vectors xi. Each pushes toward its own class yi (+1 or −1) with a weight αi (alpha) learned in training, and the kernel K fades that push with distance at the rate gamma sets. The new point x takes the side whose pushes add up stronger; the boundary is where f(x) = 0 and the pushes exactly cancel.

5.2 Choosing a kernel

RBF is the default for low-dimensional curved data like the rings. The polynomial kernel bends the boundary into smooth curves, not RBF's tight local curves. When features outnumber points, as in text, linear usually wins: a straight cut in a huge space is already flexible.

GotchaWhat goes wrongFix
Unscaled featuresRBF distance is dominated by the feature with the biggest unitsStandardize every feature first
C and gamma tuned one at a timeThe best C depends on gamma, and the reverseGrid search over pairs with cross-validation
Large data setsThe kernel matrix grows with the square of the number of pointsLinear SVM, or sketch the kernel

Tuning C and γ together by 5-fold cross-validation (train on four folds, test on the fifth).

import numpy as np
from sklearn.model_selection import GridSearchCV
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.svm import SVC
sc = StandardScaler()
svc = SVC(kernel="rbf")
model = make_pipeline(sc, svc)
Cs = np.logspace(-2, 3, 6)
gammas = np.logspace(-2, 2, 5)
grid = {}
grid["svc__C"] = Cs
grid["svc__gamma"] = gammas
gs = GridSearchCV(model, grid)
gs.fit(X, y)  # 5 folds by default
best = gs.best_params_
  1. 1Scales, then an RBF SVM.
  2. 2Candidate C and γ values, log spaced.
  3. 3Cross-validates every pair and keeps the best.

sklearn's C multiplies the summed hinge, while the J card's C multiplies the mean, so a value in this grid equals the page's C divided by n.

Past tens of thousands of points a kernel SVM gets slow: the kernel matrix, K for every pair, grows with the square of the data. Try a linear model or a tree ensemble first, or approximate the kernel with a cheaper sketch.

Nystroem builds features whose dot products approximate the RBF kernel; RBFSampler is the random alternative.

from sklearn.kernel_approximation import Nystroem
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.svm import LinearSVC
ny = Nystroem(n_components=300)
lin = LinearSVC(C=1.0)
clf = make_pipeline(ny, lin)
clf.fit(X, y)
  1. 1Approximates the RBF lift with 300 features, then a linear SVM.
  2. 2Fits in time linear in the number of points.

5.3 SVR and one-class SVM

The parameter C sets the soft margin, introduced by Cortes and Vapnik (1995): it sets the cost of violations. Support vector regression draws a tube around the predictions and only adds a cost for points outside it. The one-class SVM draws the boundary around normal examples only, and anything outside is an anomaly.

In scikit-learn, SVR's tube half-width epsilon (default 0.1) sets how big a miss is free: wider ignores more, 0 counts every miss. OneClassSVM's nu (default 0.5) caps the share of training points it may call anomalies; it marks new points +1 or −1.

from sklearn.svm import SVC, SVR, OneClassSVM
clf = SVC(kernel="linear", C=1.0)
clf.fit(X, y)
reg = SVR(epsilon=0.1)  # rbf
reg.fit(X, y_reg)
det = OneClassSVM(nu=0.05)  # rbf
det.fit(X_normal)
flags = det.predict(X_new)  # ±1
  1. 1Classifier with a straight boundary.
  2. 2Regression that ignores errors under ε.
  3. 3Novelty detector trained on normal points only.

6 SVMs in practice

The standard recipe in scikit-learn.

from sklearn.datasets import make_moons
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.svm import SVC

X, y = make_moons(1000, noise=0.2)
parts = train_test_split(X, y)
Xtr, Xte, ytr, yte = parts
svc = SVC(C=1.0, gamma="scale")
sc = StandardScaler()
model = make_pipeline(sc, svc)
model.fit(Xtr, ytr)
acc = model.score(Xte, yte)
n_sv = model[-1].n_support_
  1. 1Two noisy half-moons with a test split.
  2. 2Scaling, then an RBF SVM.
  3. 3Fits, scores and counts support vectors.

SVC, built on libsvm, caches kernel values in 200 MB. Its documentation says fit time grows at least with the square of the samples and suggests LinearSVC or SGDClassifier past tens of thousands, possibly after Nystroem. Scale inside the pipeline, since unscaled features break the RBF distance. SVC handles many classes one pair at a time, and scikit-learn 1.9 deprecates probability=True in favor of CalibratedClassifierCV.
