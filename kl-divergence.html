KL Divergence

KL (Kullback-Leibler) divergence measures the extra loss from predicting with the wrong chances when the target is itself a set of chances. Cross-entropy is the target's own spread plus KL, and which side sits in the first slot decides what a distilled student or an RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback) policy gives up.

1 Surprise and entropy

Builds on expected value (Probability and Bayes Rule, section 2)

The teacher on the Knowledge Distillation page looks at a cat photo and spreads its chances over cat, dog, car and bird: most on cat, least on bird, from that page's toy, not a trained model. These are its soft targets at temperature 2, which flattens its chances. If the label turned out to be bird, the teacher would be the most surprised.

Call the teacher's chances \(y\), with \(y_{k}\) its chance for class \(k\). Logs are natural logs, so the unit is the nat (log base 2 would give bits).

Entropy is the average surprise you feel if you believe the true chances.
\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{s_{k}}} = {-\log \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{y_{k}}}}\)
\(s_{k}\) is the surprise of class \(k\): 0 for a sure class, large for an unlikely one.
\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-entropy)}{H(y)}} = {-\sum_{k} \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{y_{k}}} \log \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{y_{k}}}}\)
\(H(y)\) is the entropy of \(y\). \(\sum_{k}\) adds over the classes and weights each surprise by its chance, so \(H\) is an expected value. It measures how spread out the chances are: 0 for a one-hot target, and largest, \(\log 4\), for an even split over the four classes.

The entropy bonus in policy-based reinforcement learning is this same \(H\).

2 Cross-entropy is entropy plus KL

Builds on categorical cross-entropy (Cross-Entropy Loss, section 2) and logs turning products into sums (Maximum Likelihood and EM, section 4)

The student comes from the Knowledge Distillation page's training tool, two of its ten steps in; its chances \(p_{k}\) still put too little on cat and too much on the other three. Graded against the teacher, each class costs minus the log of the student's chance, weighted by the teacher's.

Cross-entropy is your average surprise when you believe the wrong chances. It splits into the surprise nobody can avoid and the extra caused by believing \(p\) instead of \(y\); that extra is KL divergence.
\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#c0392b}{H(y, p)}} = {-\sum_{k} \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{y_{k}}} \log \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-prob)}{p_{k}}}}\)
\(H(y, p)\) is the cross-entropy: the entropy line with the student's surprise in place of the teacher's, still weighted by \(y_{k}\), since the teacher sets the grading.
\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#c0392b}{H(y, p)}} - \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-entropy)}{H(y)}} = {\sum_{k} \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{y_{k}}} \log \frac{\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{y_{k}}}}{\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-prob)}{p_{k}}}}}\)
Subtract the entropy class by class and the two logs combine into the log of a ratio: positive where the student gives too little chance (cat), negative where it gives too much (dog, car, bird).
\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-kl)}{\operatorname{KL}(y \parallel p)}} = {\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#c0392b}{H(y, p)}} - \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-entropy)}{H(y)}}}\)
The gap is named \(\operatorname{KL}(y \parallel p)\), read "KL of \(y\) from \(p\)". The double bar separates two slots, and the first slot holds the chances that do the weighting, here the teacher's.
\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#c0392b}{H(y, p)}} = {\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-entropy)}{H(y)}} + \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-kl)}{\operatorname{KL}(y \parallel p)}}}\)
Rearranged, cross-entropy is the entropy plus the KL. \(H(y)\) does not depend on the student, so lowering cross-entropy lowers KL by the same amount, and soft-target cross-entropy (Hinton et al. 2015) and a KL term train the same student.

A one-hot target has no spread, so its entropy is 0 and the KL keeps only the surprise at the correct class, exactly the categorical loss. Maximum likelihood, and with it a language model's next-token loss, minimizes this KL with the data in the first slot (Maximum Likelihood and EM, section 6).

The NumPy snippet checks the split.

import numpy as np
# cat, dog, car, bird:
# teacher at T = 2, then student
y = np.array([0.645, 0.194,
    0.096, 0.065])
p = np.array([0.451, 0.209,
    0.175, 0.165])
H_y = -np.sum(y * np.log(y))
H_yp = -np.sum(y * np.log(p))
kl = np.sum(y * np.log(y / p))
print(H_yp, H_y + kl)  # equal
  1. 1The teacher's and the student's chances for cat, dog, car and bird.
  2. 2The entropy.
  3. 3The cross-entropy and the KL.
  4. 4Prints the split; the two values are equal.

3 KL is not symmetric

Builds on cross-entropy is entropy plus KL, Section 2 of this page

Take a student trained on the labels alone, with no teacher term: it ends nearly sure of cat, with almost nothing left for dog, car and bird. Score it against the teacher both ways.

\(\operatorname{KL}(y \parallel p)\), target first, is called forward KL; \(\operatorname{KL}(p \parallel y)\), model first, is reverse KL. Each averages the same kind of log ratio under a different set of chances.
\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-kl)}{\operatorname{KL}(y \parallel p)}} = {\sum_{k} \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{y_{k}}} \log \frac{\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{y_{k}}}}{\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-prob)}{p_{k}}}}}\)
Forward KL is the KL line of the cross-entropy card. The teacher's \(y_{k}\) is the weight, so it charges where the teacher expects something the student does not: dog, which the teacher gives real chance and the student almost none, is the largest term.
\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-kl-reverse)}{\operatorname{KL}(p \parallel y)}} = {\sum_{k} \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-prob)}{p_{k}}} \log \frac{\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-prob)}{p_{k}}}}{\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{y_{k}}}}}\)
Reverse KL swaps the slots, so the student's \(p_{k}\) is the weight and it charges where the student bets: cat, which the student is far surer of than the teacher, is the largest term. For this label-only student the forward score is the larger of the two, so KL is a directed score, not a distance.
  • KL is never negative, and zero only when the two lists match, which is what distillation aims for (Cover and Thomas 2006, Theorem 2.6.3). In Figure 3 both scores reach 0 when the student's bars sit on the teacher's outlines.

4 Forward and reverse KL

Builds on expected value (Probability and Bayes Rule, section 2) and standard deviation and the bell curve (Common Probability Distributions, section 5)

Take distilling a large language model (LLM) into a small one. For one prompt the teacher has two equally good kinds of answer, and the small student can produce only one. Picture all answers on one line, as an illustrative sketch: the teacher is two equal, narrow bumps with an empty valley between them, and the student is one bell curve.

Section 3's two directions carry over with area in place of a sum: each charges the log ratio wherever its first slot puts weight.
\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-kl)}{\operatorname{KL}(y \parallel p)}} = {\int \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{y(x)}} \log \frac{\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{y(x)}}}{\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-prob)}{p(x)}}}\, dx}\)
\(y(x)\) and \(p(x)\) are the two curves' heights at \(x\); chance is area under a curve, and \(\int \dots \, dx\) adds up area. The weight \(y(x)\) is large on both bumps, where a low \(p(x)\) makes the log ratio large, so the forward fit stays high over both bumps and, being one curve, over the valley between.
\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-kl-reverse)}{\operatorname{KL}(p \parallel y)}} = {\int \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-prob)}{p(x)}} \log \frac{\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-prob)}{p(x)}}}{\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{y(x)}}}\, dx}\)
Now the weight is the student's \(p(x)\). Where the student puts chance and \(y(x)\) is near 0 the log ratio is huge, so the reverse fit avoids the valley and sits on one bump; the missed bump costs almost nothing, since \(p(x)\) is near 0 there.
  • In this sketch the forward fit puts a real share of its chance in the valley, where the teacher has almost none: a student that generates answers the teacher finds unlikely.

The picture follows Bishop (2006, chapter 10). A mode is a peak of a distribution, one bump here, so forward KL is called mode-covering and reverse KL mode-seeking. MiniLLM (Gu et al. 2024) switches LLM distillation to reverse KL for the reason in the forward panel ①.

5 KL in distillation, RLHF and t-SNE

Builds on temperature (Sampling the Next Token, the temperature section), the distillation loss (Knowledge Distillation, section 2) and the reward model (Aligning Language Models with RL, section 2.3)

The running student's KL from Section 2 is the KL inside the soft part of the Knowledge Distillation page's loss, which that page scales by the temperature squared and by the soft weight.

In RLHF the policy \(\pi\) is the language model being tuned, \(\pi_{\text{SFT}}\) is the frozen supervised fine-tuned (SFT) model it starts from, called the reference model, and the reward model scores answers the way people would. t-SNE (t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding) draws high-dimensional data as a 2D map.

All three are the KL of Section 2. Distillation scales it by a weight, RLHF scales its per-answer form by a coefficient, and t-SNE uses it as is.
\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-loss)}{L}} = {(1 - \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#196f3d}{\alpha}}) \cdot \operatorname{CE}(z, \text{label})} + {\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#196f3d}{\alpha}} \cdot \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-temp)}{T}}^{2} \cdot \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-kl)}{\operatorname{KL}(y \parallel p)}}}\)
\(y\) and \(p\) are the teacher's and student's chances at temperature \(T\) (the Knowledge Distillation page calls them \(p_{t}\) and \(p_{s}\)), and \(\operatorname{CE}\), the cross-entropy, scores the student's logits \(z\) (its raw scores before the softmax), at temperature 1, against the true label. \(\alpha\) weighs the soft part: raise it and the student copies the teacher more and the labels less. \(T^{2}\) undoes the shrink that temperature puts on the soft term's gradient (Knowledge Distillation, section 2). With the teacher in the first slot, the student must cover every class the teacher expects.
\(\displaystyle \text{reward} = {\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-reward)}{r}} - \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\beta}} \cdot \log \frac{\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\pi(a)}}}{\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{\pi_{\text{SFT}}(a)}}}}\)
\(r\) is the reward model's score for one sampled answer \(a\) to one prompt. \(\beta\) is the KL coefficient, 0.02 in InstructGPT (Ouyang et al. 2022, Appendix C.4): raise it and the policy stays closer to the SFT model; lower it and the policy chases the reward model (reward over-optimization). The log ratio is computed per token and summed over the answer. Answers are sampled from \(\pi\), so averaged over them it is \(\operatorname{KL}(\pi \parallel \pi_{\text{SFT}})\), reverse KL: the policy is charged for answers the SFT model finds unlikely. An answer the policy finds much likelier than the SFT model does has a large log ratio, so its reward is pulled down the most.
\(\displaystyle \text{t-SNE cost} = \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-kl)}{\operatorname{KL}(P \parallel Q)}}\)
For each point, \(P\) holds the chance it would pick each other point as its neighbor, high for points close in the original space, and \(Q\) holds the same chances measured on the map; \(P\) fills the first slot (van der Maaten and Hinton 2008). Forward KL charges a map that puts true neighbors far apart.

6 KL in PyTorch

Builds on the softmax (Cross-Entropy Loss, section 3)

  1. F.kl_div takes the student's log-chances first and the teacher's chances second, the opposite order from the math notation, and returns \(\operatorname{KL}(y \parallel p)\), forward KL with the teacher first. Swapped arguments raise no error. nn.KLDivLoss is the same function as a module.
  2. Get the log-chances from F.log_softmax on the logits, not from the log of a softmax, which can hit \(\log 0\).
  3. Use reduction="batchmean": the default "mean" also divides by the number of classes, and the docs warn it is not the true KL.
  4. A zero target chance adds 0; a zero model chance where the target is positive makes KL infinite.

The PyTorch snippet returns the same KL as the NumPy snippet.

import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F
# a batch of one: cat, dog, car,
# bird; the teacher's chances
y = torch.tensor([[0.645, 0.194,
    0.096, 0.065]])
# the student's logits
z = torch.log(torch.tensor(
    [[0.451, 0.209,
      0.175, 0.165]]))
log_p = F.log_softmax(z, dim=-1)
# forward KL, teacher first
kl = F.kl_div(log_p, y,
    reduction="batchmean")
  1. 1The teacher, a batch of one.
  2. 2Student logits, here the log of its chances.
  3. 3Log-chances (point 2).
  4. 4With the student's log-chances first and the teacher's chances second, the call returns \(\operatorname{KL}(y \parallel p)\); batchmean divides by the batch size (points 1 and 3).
