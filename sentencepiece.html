1 SentencePiece runs BPE or Unigram on raw text

SentencePiece (Kudo and Richardson, 2018) is a toolkit, not a new algorithm: it trains and runs BPE or Unigram, Unigram by default (References 1, 4). Earlier pipelines first cut text into words with language-specific rules, which fails for Chinese and Japanese, written without spaces, and loses the exact spacing. SentencePiece takes the raw text as one stream of characters and builds on three ideas: no language-specific pre-tokenizer, every space kept as a symbol so decoding gives the text back, and one .model file that carries the normalization rules and the vocabulary, so encoding is the same everywhere.

Training (spm_train) runs once. The processor normalizes and encodes on every call, and the .model file is what ships with the model.

2 The space is a symbol, so decoding is lossless

SentencePiece replaces every space with ▁ before splitting, and adds one at the start so a sentence’s first word splits like any other (add_dummy_prefix, Reference 4). Pieces can carry it, such as ▁This. Because every space survives as a symbol, decoding is a plain join followed by turning each ▁ back into a space; no language rules are needed to put words back together (how other tokenizers keep spaces).

What decoding gives back.

\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt sym-c}{\htmlStyle{color:#1f6aa5}{s}} = \operatorname{Normalize}(t)\)
\(t\) is the raw text and \(s\) the normalized text. \(\operatorname{Normalize}\) is the whole cleanup stored in the model, space rules included.
\(\displaystyle \operatorname{Decode}\big(\operatorname{Encode}(\htmlClass{eqt sym-c}{\htmlStyle{color:#1f6aa5}{s}})\big) = \htmlClass{eqt sym-c}{\htmlStyle{color:#1f6aa5}{s}}\)
\(\operatorname{Encode}\) and \(\operatorname{Decode}\) are the model’s split and join. Decoding gives back \(s\), spaces included, not always \(t\). The raw text comes back exactly only when \(\operatorname{Normalize}\) changes nothing and no piece is <unk>, the unknown token; a character that became <unk> decodes to an unknown marker, so the equation then holds only up to those characters.

3 Normalization is stored in the model

Before training and on every encode, SentencePiece rewrites look-alike characters into one standard form. The default rule, nmt_nfkc, builds on Unicode NFKC plus a few space rules: a full-width Ａ becomes A and the ligature ﬁ becomes fi (References 1, 4). The model then sees one form of each letter, which keeps encoding consistent, and the round trip changes the text: Ａ comes back as A.

A second default, remove_extra_whitespaces, collapses runs of spaces into one and trims both ends (Reference 4). It cleans up prose, and it also flattens code indentation. Setting normalization_rule_name="identity" turns the character rewriting off; the space rule is a separate switch.

4 Characters the vocabulary does not cover

character_coverage is the share of the corpus’s characters the vocabulary must cover; the rarest characters past that share are left out. The default 0.9995 suits large character sets such as Chinese and Japanese, and 1.0 suits small alphabets such as English (Reference 4). A character left out becomes <unk>, or, with byte_fallback on, its UTF-8 bytes, so nothing is ever unknown (characters and bytes). Llama 2 and Gemma turn byte fallback on (References 2, 3).

One more switch changes how numbers split: split_digits makes every digit its own piece, on in Llama 2 and Gemma (References 2 to 4; how numbers split).

7 Training your own vocabulary

You train a vocabulary once, on text that looks like what the model will read, and every later step depends on it. Training reads the whole corpus into memory by default; for a large corpus, input_sentence_size trains on a random sample of that many sentences instead (Reference 4). Unigram is the pick when you want sampled splits during training; byte-level BPE elsewhere is the pick when no text may ever be unknown. Hugging Face tokenizers is the other common trainer.

Code 1 trains a vocabulary on corpus.txt, one sentence per line, with the Llama 2 and Gemma switches, then encodes and decodes a line.

import sentencepiece as spm

spm.SentencePieceTrainer.train(
    input="corpus.txt",
    model_prefix="tok",
    vocab_size=VOCAB_SIZE,
    model_type="bpe",
    character_coverage=0.9995,
    byte_fallback=True,
    split_digits=True,
)
sp = spm.SentencePieceProcessor(model_file="tok.model")
pieces = sp.encode("Ｇood  morning 🦊", return_type=str)  # out_type= before v0.2.2
ids = sp.encode("Ｇood  morning 🦊")
print(pieces, ids)
print(sp.decode(ids))

  2. 2The Llama 2 and Gemma switches: bytes instead of <unk>, one piece per digit.
  3. 3Load the trained model, then encode to pieces and to ids.

8 What goes wrong

The model’s last layer gives one score for each of the \(V\) entries at every step. How does it turn those scores into the next id? Decoding answers that.
