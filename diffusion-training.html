Training a Diffusion Model: Predicting the Noise

Teaching a network to guess noise.

Before you start Forward process, closed form Mean squared error Bayes rule Kullback-Leibler divergence

Show the network a noisy image and its step number, and ask it to guess the noise that was added. Score the guess with mean squared error (MSE). A noise guess is also a clean-image guess, and it tells each reverse step how much to remove. This page covers training, the diffusion branch of the map on Generative Models. This one regression loss is how denoising diffusion probabilistic models (DDPM; Ho et al. 2020) and Stable Diffusion v1 are trained (Rombach et al. 2022 train the same noise-guess loss in a compressed latent), and DiffWave, a speech synthesizer, trains audio with a variant of the same bound (Kong et al. 2021).

1 Guess the noise; the loss is plain squared error

Builds on mean squared error (Linear Regression, section 2), the average over many draws (Probability and Bayes Rule, section 2) and the jump to any step (Diffusion Models: Adding Noise on a Schedule, section 3)

Follow the same pixel as on the noising page, clean value \(x_{0} = 0.8\), at step 500; the symbols \(\beta\), \(\alpha\) and \(\bar\alpha\) are that page’s schedule.

Step 1 of 6

Figure 2. Every step is a noise-guessing quiz with a known answer.

Pick a clean training image \(x_{0}\).
Make a noisy image whose noise you know, and grade the network’s guess of that noise.
\(\displaystyle L_{\text{simple}} = E_{t, x_{0}, \varepsilon }\)
\(\displaystyle \|\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=real}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{\varepsilon}}} - \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=fake}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\varepsilon _{\theta }(x_{t}, t)}}}\|^{2}\)
\(\displaystyle x_{t} = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t}}\cdot x_{0} + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_{t}}\cdot \varepsilon\)
\(L_{\text{simple}}\) is DDPM’s name for this unweighted loss. E over \(t\), \(x_{0}\) and \(\varepsilon\) is the average over many random draws of all three, so one batch estimates it. \(\varepsilon\) is the training noise, one draw from N(0, \(I\)). The network’s input \(x_{t}\), on the second row, is the jump’s recipe. \(\varepsilon _{\theta }\) is the network with weights \(\theta\), given the step \(t\) as well. \(\|\cdot\|^{2}\) is the squared error summed over pixels.
The input is the jump’s recipe. Unlike the game of generative adversarial networks, there is no second network, so this loss is a plain regression target that goes down. DDPM reports that predicting \(\varepsilon\), not the reverse mean, with this unweighted loss gave the best samples.

2 From a noise guess to a clean-image guess

Builds on the jump to any step (Diffusion Models: Adding Noise on a Schedule, section 3)

Subtract the guessed noise, then undo the shrink.
\(\displaystyle \hat{x}_{0} = \frac{x_{t} - \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=fake}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_{t}}\cdot \hat{\varepsilon}}}}}{\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=real}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t}}}}}}\)
\(\hat{\varepsilon}\) is the network’s noise guess \(\varepsilon_{\theta}(x_{t}, t)\), and \(\hat{x}_{0}\) the clean-image guess. The numerator removes the noise part of the jump’s recipe. Dividing by \(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t}}\) undoes the shrink, which is why small \(\bar\alpha\) amplifies errors.
It is the jump’s recipe solved for \(x_{0}\); the plain squared-error loss trains \(\hat{\varepsilon}\).

3 The reverse step given the clean image

Builds on Bayes rule (Probability and Bayes Rule, section 4) and the Gaussian (Common Probability Distributions, section 5)

The step back from \(x_{t}\) alone, \(q(x_{t-1} \mid x_{t})\), depends on the whole unknown data distribution, but once \(x_{0}\) is also known it is a plain Gaussian, so training can compare the network’s step with it.

Knowing where the pixel started and where it is now, the step before lies in between, a little toward the start.
\(\displaystyle q(x_{t-1} | x_{t}, x_{0}) =\)
\(\displaystyle N(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=real}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{\tilde{\mu}_{t}}}}, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=fake}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\tilde{\beta}_{t}\cdot I}}})\)
\(\tilde{\mu}_{t}\) is the mean of the true step back and \(\tilde{\beta}_{t}\) its variance.
\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=real}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{\tilde{\mu}_{t}}}} = \frac{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\cdot \beta _{t}}{1 - \bar\alpha_{t}}\cdot x_{0}\)
\(\displaystyle + \frac{\sqrt{\alpha _{t}}\cdot (1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1})}{1 - \bar\alpha_{t}}\cdot x_{t}\)
The \(x_{0}\) weight is large when \(\beta _{t}\) is a big share of the noise so far (early steps) and tiny mid-way. The \(x_{t}\) weight is close to 1 mid-way, because the step back is almost where the pixel already is. Both weights come from the schedule only, with no network. \(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\) is how much of the clean pixel survives to step \(t - 1\), and \(\sqrt{\alpha _{t}}\) is how much of \(x_{t-1}\) this step keeps in \(x_{t}\).
\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=fake}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\tilde{\beta}_{t}}}} = \frac{1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1}}{1 - \bar\alpha_{t}}\cdot \beta _{t}\)
The stacked fraction is the noise so far before this step divided by the noise after it. It starts near nothing and rises close to 1 (Figure 4), so \(\tilde{\beta}_{t}\) sits below \(\beta _{t}\) and nearly equals it after the first few steps: knowing \(x_{0}\) removes some uncertainty. DDPM fixes \(\sigma _{t}^{2}\), the variance of the network’s reverse step, to \(\beta _{t}\) or \(\tilde{\beta}_{t}\) with similar results; Improved DDPM (Nichol and Dhariwal 2021) learns it, which helps when sampling skips steps.
\(\displaystyle q(x_{t-1} | x_{t}, x_{0}) \propto\)
\(\displaystyle q(x_{t} | x_{t-1})\cdot q(x_{t-1} | x_{0})\)
Line 4 is where lines 1 to 3 come from, in three plain steps. It is Bayes rule, where \(\propto\) reads “is proportional to”. Both factors are Gaussians, the noising step and the jump. A product of Gaussians in \(x_{t-1}\) is a Gaussian, whose mean and variance are lines 2 and 3.
It is Bayes rule applied to the noising step and the jump.
Subtract this step’s share of the guessed noise, then undo this step’s shrink.
\(\displaystyle \mu _{\theta } = \frac{x_{t} - \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=fake}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\frac{\beta _{t}}{\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_{t}}}\cdot \varepsilon _{\theta }}}}}{\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=real}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{\sqrt{\alpha _{t}}}}}}\)
\(\mu _{\theta }\) is the mean of the network’s reverse step. \(\frac{\beta_{t}}{\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_{t}}}\) is this step’s share of all the noise so far, a small share, so one step removes only a small share of the guessed noise. Dividing by \(\sqrt{\alpha _{t}}\) undoes one forward shrink, not all of them.
It is the true step-back mean with the clean-image guess put in; ancestral sampling (Faster Diffusion Sampling, section 1) steps with it.

4 The likelihood bound behind the loss

Builds on the log-likelihood (Maximum Likelihood and EM, section 4) and the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence (KL Divergence, section 2)

The loss of Section 1 is not the bound itself; it is the bound’s terms with their weights dropped. The likelihood can never fall below the bound.

The bound is a sum of KL divergences between Gaussians with equal spreads, shown here at the same pixel.

With equal spreads the only way to differ is the mean, and the cost is the squared mean gap in units of the spread.
\(\displaystyle \operatorname{KL}(N(\mu _{1}, \sigma ^{2}) \| N(\mu _{2}, \sigma ^{2}))\)
\(\displaystyle = \frac{\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=real}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{(\mu _{1} - \mu _{2})^{2}}}}}{\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=fake}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{2\sigma ^{2}}}}}\)
The KL here is the extra nats (natural-log units) paid for using the second Gaussian when the first is true. \(\mu _{1}\) and \(\mu _{2}\) are the two means, the true step and the network’s. \(\sigma ^{2}\) is the shared variance; the card holds only because both Gaussians use the same variance, which is true when the network’s \(\sigma _{t}^{2}\) is set to \(\tilde{\beta}_{t}\).
It compares the true step back with the network’s step.

Written in terms of the noise guess, each step’s KL is the squared noise error times a weight \(\lambda _{t}\) that only the noise schedule sets. Figure 8 plots that weight against the flat loss, which weights every step the same.

Dropping the weight spends training on the noisier steps.

With the same small noise error at every step, the first steps carry the largest share of the total because their weight is largest. Summing the per-step KLs over all \(t\) gives the bound’s middle; the last term, \(L_{T}\), needs no training because, by the jump of Diffusion Models, section 3, \(x_{T}\) is about N(0, \(I\)) already.

The model’s log-chance of a real image is at least minus the sum of per-step mistakes, so shrinking every step’s mistake raises the floor.
\(\displaystyle \log p_{\theta }(x_{0}) \ge\)
\(\displaystyle -(L_{T} + \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=real}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{L_{T-1} + \dots}}}\)
\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=real}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{+ L_{1}}}} + L_{0})\)
This is the evidence lower bound (ELBO). \(p_{\theta}(x_{0})\) is the probability the model gives a clean image through its whole reverse chain from noise; the bound exists because this cannot be computed directly. \(L_{T}\) compares the final noisy image with pure noise and has no weights. Each middle \(L_{t-1}\) is the Gaussian KL between the true step and the network’s step. \(L_{0}\) scores the last step and depends on the weights. Drawing one uniform \(t\) per image trains one random middle term at a time. On average over draws that term equals the full sum divided by the number of steps \(T\), so the shortcut trains the same target up to that constant.
\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=real}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{L_{t-1}}}} = \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=fake}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\lambda _{t}}}}\cdot E\|\varepsilon - \varepsilon _{\theta }(x_{t}, t)\|^{2}\)
\(\displaystyle + \text{const}\)
\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=fake}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\lambda _{t}}}} = \frac{\beta _{t}^{2}}{2\sigma _{t}^{2}\cdot \alpha _{t}\cdot (1 - \bar\alpha_{t})}\)
Line 2 is each middle term as the loss’s error times a schedule weight \(\lambda _{t}\), which Figure 8 plots scaled to average 1. \(\lambda _{t}\) is the squared step size \(\beta _{t}^{2}\) over twice the reverse variance \(\sigma _{t}^{2}\) times \(\alpha _{t}\) times the noise so far 1 − \(\bar\alpha_{t}\), so it is largest at the first steps, where 1 − \(\bar\alpha_{t}\) is tiny. E is the average of Section 1, and const holds terms with no weights in them.
DDPM drops that weight, which gives the plain squared-error loss: better samples, slightly worse likelihood.

5 A U-shaped network that knows the step

Builds on downsampling (CNN Basics: Convolution, Stride and Pooling, section 3), skip connections (Classic CNN Architectures: AlexNet to ResNet, section 5), attention (Attention, section 3) and sinusoidal positions (Positional Encoding and Masking, section 2)

The output must be one noise guess per pixel, the same size as the input. DDPM uses the U-Net of Ronneberger et al. (2015), a network named for its U shape and built for biomedical image segmentation, and adds the step embedding and attention. The decoder doubles the map size at each level; DDPM resizes by nearest neighbour and then convolves (the original U-Net used transposed convolutions, Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), section 9). Every block uses group normalization (Normalization Layers, section 2.3).

6 What the best denoiser predicts at each noise level

Builds on Bayes rule (Probability and Bayes Rule, section 4) and the best guess under squared error (The Wiener Filter, section 3)

Training with the loss of Section 1 pulls \(\varepsilon _{\theta }\) toward the best guess of the noise, and so \(\hat{x}_{0}\) toward the best guess of the clean image. Under squared error that guess is the posterior mean, the average of every clean image that could have made this \(x_{t}\), weighted by its chance. The 10 bars show each training digit’s chance, from Bayes rule on the jump’s Gaussian. This exact denoiser can only redraw these 10 digits.

Figure 11. At high noise the best guess blurs every digit; later it commits.

At step 700 with the 7 noised, the guess spreads across digits and commits to none.

DDPM shows the same coarse-to-fine guess on a benchmark of 60,000 photos of 32x32 pixels in 10 classes (CIFAR-10).

7 A training step in PyTorch

Builds on backpropagation (Backpropagation and Gradient Checking, section 5) and Adam and the moving average (Adaptive Optimizers: AdaGrad, RMSProp and Adam, sections 4 and 3)

One training step on a random stand-in batch, with a tiny network standing in for the U-Net.

import copy, torch, torch.nn as nn, torch.nn.functional as F
T, B = 1000, 8
alphas_cumprod = torch.cumprod(1 - torch.linspace(1e-4, 0.02, T), dim=0)
class TinyNet(nn.Module):  # stand-in for the U-Net; t enters as an extra channel
    def __init__(self): super().__init__(); self.conv = nn.Conv2d(2, 1, 3, padding=1)
    def forward(self, x, t):
        step = (t.float() / T).view(-1, 1, 1, 1).expand_as(x)
        return self.conv(torch.cat([x, step], dim=1))
model = TinyNet(); ema = copy.deepcopy(model)
optimizer = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=2e-4)
x0 = torch.rand(B, 1, 28, 28) * 2 - 1

t, noise = torch.randint(0, T, (B,)), torch.randn_like(x0)
a = alphas_cumprod[t].view(-1, 1, 1, 1)
x_t = a.sqrt() * x0 + (1 - a).sqrt() * noise
loss = F.mse_loss(model(x_t, t), noise)
optimizer.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); optimizer.step()
with torch.no_grad():
    for p_ema, p in zip(ema.parameters(), model.parameters()):
        p_ema.lerp_(p, 1 - 0.9999)

  • The loss flattens early while samples keep improving, so judge by samples.
