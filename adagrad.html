1 What AdaGrad fixes

One learning rate has to suit every weight (Adaptive Optimizers, section 1). In a language model’s embedding table, a common word’s row gets a gradient almost every batch and a rare word’s row almost never. A rate small enough for the busy rows leaves the rare rows barely trained, yet rare features are often the most informative ones; Duchi, Hazan and Singer, who introduced AdaGrad in 2011, call them “needles in haystacks”.

AdaGrad gives every weight its own step size: weights that have been pushed a lot take short steps, and weights that have rarely been pushed keep long ones. GloVe word vectors were trained with it for exactly this reason (Pennington, Socher and Manning 2014), and sparse click and ranking features in recommenders (Recommender Systems) have the same shape.

2 How AdaGrad sizes each step

AdaGrad keeps one running total per weight and divides that weight’s step by the total’s square root.

AdaGrad: a running total of squared gradients, and the step divided by its root
\({\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{G_t} = \htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{G_{t-1}} + \htmlStyle{color:#c0392b}{g_t^{2}}}\)
\(G_t\) is a running total of how hard this one weight has been pushed: each step adds today’s gradient \(g_t\) squared. Squaring drops the sign, so a push in either direction counts, and nothing is ever subtracted, so the total never forgets. A common word’s row collects a large total quickly; a rare word’s row collects a small one.
\({\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\theta_t} = \htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\theta_{t-1}} - \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{\alpha}\,\frac{\htmlStyle{color:#c0392b}{g_t}}{\sqrt{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{G_t}} + \epsilon}}\)
Today’s push is measured against all past pushes. \(\sqrt{G_t}\) is the total past size in the gradient’s own units, so \(g_t/\sqrt{G_t}\) compares today with the history, and the learning rate \(\alpha\) turns that comparison into a distance. \(\epsilon\) (epsilon) is a tiny constant that keeps the divide away from zero. So every weight’s first step is about \(\alpha\) long, whatever its gradient’s size, and every later step is shorter, shrinking faster for weights that are pushed often.
\({\displaystyle \frac{\lvert \htmlStyle{color:#c0392b}{g}\rvert}{\sqrt{t\,\htmlStyle{color:#c0392b}{g}^{2}}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}}\)
Joins the two lines for a weight whose gradient \(g\) stays the same size: after \(t\) pushes the total is \(t\) times \(g^2\), so its root grows like \(\sqrt{t}\) and the step falls like \(\alpha/\sqrt{t}\). The bars \(\lvert\,\rvert\) mean size without sign. This slow, endless shrinking is the curve in Figure 1, and it is what makes AdaGrad stall on long runs.

5 When to use it

AdaGrad suits sparse features: embedding tables such as GloVe’s, recommender features, and linear models on sparse inputs, where most weights are pushed rarely and each push matters. It suits long training of deep networks poorly: there every weight is pushed on every step, the totals grow without end and all steps die out. For those, RMSProp, which lets old gradients fade, or Adam is the usual choice.

6 When it runs and what it carries

AdaGrad runs once per training step, right after backward(). It carries one tensor of totals \(G\) per weight tensor, the same size as the weights, and never resets it during training. The totals live in the optimizer’s state, so a checkpoint must save opt.state_dict() as well as the model; saving the whole loop is covered in Optimizers in Practice: what the loop carries.

7 AdaGrad in PyTorch

The snippet trains an embedding table with sparse gradients, the GloVe-like case, and reads back the running totals. Lines 2 and 3 carry the idea.

import torch
emb = torch.nn.Embedding(50_000, 300, sparse=True)   # 50k words, 300-d rows
opt = torch.optim.Adagrad(emb.parameters(), lr=0.05, eps=1e-10)
loss = emb(torch.tensor([3, 41_217])).pow(2).sum()   # a common and a rare word
loss.backward()
opt.step()
G = opt.state[emb.weight]["sum"]   # the running total, one entry per weight
  1. 1An embedding table whose gradients touch only the rows used in a batch (sparse gradients).
  2. 2AdaGrad with \(\alpha\) and \(\epsilon\) of the step line; 0.05 is GloVe’s reported initial rate.
  3. 3One step for two rows, a common word and a rare one.
  4. 4The running total \(G\) of the first line, stored under the key "sum".

8 Failure modes and tips

Most AdaGrad trouble comes from a total that only grows.
symptomcausefix
the loss stalls while gradients are still largeevery total keeps growing on long dense training, so every step shrinks toward zerolet old gradients fade with RMSProp, or use Adam
one layer stops moving after an early blow-upone huge early gradient enters the total foreverclip gradients before the step (Gradient Clipping)
weights with small gradients barely move\(\epsilon\) set too large, so it swamps \(\sqrt{G}\)keep eps tiny
