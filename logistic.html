Logistic Regression

A line that says yes or no.

Last page we fit a line that predicts numbers. But what if the answer is not a number at all? A line predicts 13.7. Useless when the question is "is this flower a versicolor, yes or no?" Same line, new job: we are going to squash it into a probability.

A raw line predicts nonsense probabilities, so squash it

You could try. Fit a line to 0-or-1 labels and call everything above 0.5 a yes. People did. It is awful: the line happily predicts 2.4 or -0.7, which are not probabilities, they are nonsense wearing a trench coat. One faraway outlier drags the whole line sideways.

So we keep the line (it is still doing the real work: turning features into a raw score) and we pass its output through a squashing function that pins everything between 0 and 1. That squashed number reads as a probability, and 0.5 becomes the decision boundary. Same line you already know, now allowed to vote yes or no.

The sigmoid: scores become probabilities

Take the line's raw score. It can be anything from minus infinity to plus infinity. Now feed it through the sigmoid: big positive scores come out near 1, big negative scores near 0, and a score of 0 lands at exactly 50/50. The curve is steepest in the middle, where the decision lives, and flattens at the extremes where the model has made up its mind.

The sigmoid pins every score between 0 and 1, always. No more 2.4, no more -0.7. The curve is symmetric around zero: equal scores on opposite sides sit the same distance from uncertainty.

How it learns: punish surprise, not distance

Squared error asks "how far off was the number?" For probabilities the better question is "how surprised were you?" If the truth is yes and you said 90% yes, no surprise. If you said 10% yes, huge surprise. That is the binary cross-entropy cost: for each sample, you pay for how surprised you were by the truth. A confident right answer costs almost nothing; a confident wrong answer costs a lot. Each sample pays only for its own surprise, yes or no.

The gradient has a shape you already know: it is exactly the linear-regression gradient, except the prediction is wrapped in the sigmoid. Same downhill walk, one squeeze of difference. Beautiful.

0.5 draws the line

Here is the money fact: the sigmoid hits one half exactly when the raw score is zero. So "probability at least one half" is exactly "raw score at least zero", and the decision boundary is the line where the raw score is zero. The same line from regression, now working as a fence between yes and no.

The same trick, in new shapes

More than two classes at once: one-vs-rest is three separate yes-or-no votes. The softmax is one vote over all classes at once, the sigmoid's big sibling, whose probabilities sum to 1.

Keeping the fence honest: on nearly separable data, training inflates the weights to push probabilities all the way to 1 and 0, and the fence tilts steeper and steeper. Regularization penalizes big weights, keeping the fence honest. This is step 4's tilt demo, tamed.

When yes is rare: count each yes sample more, with class weights, instead of moving the threshold by hand.

The fence is still straight. Nothing here bends it.

A straight boundary cannot bend around the moons

A straight fence cannot bend. To be honest: the Iris notebook this page is built on has no moons. One line, zero chance, against the two-moons crescents. The answer is to take the same sigmoid idea, stack it into a network, and bend the boundary around the two interleaved crescents. When a line is not enough, you stack lines with bends between them.
