Reinforcement Learning (RL)

No answers, just a score. The agent figures out the rest.

On this page

What if you could not show the model the answer? No labels, no examples of correct behavior, no dataset at all. Just a world, a few buttons, and a scoreboard. Would anything learn?

Yes, and it is a little unsettling how well. That is reinforcement learning: the agent tries things, the world keeps score, and the strategy slowly bends toward whatever scores. It is how my own code learned to collect bananas, swing a robot arm, and rally a tennis ball, and I barely told it anything beyond what counts as winning.

The same loop shows up everywhere the answer cannot be written down in advance: game-playing agents that invent strategies no human taught them, robot hands that learn to grasp by dropping things ten thousand times, systems that tune themselves against a live scoreboard. Whenever the right move is unknown but the score is clear, RL is the tool that earns its keep. This page is the front door to a three-part series where I built all three of those: a banana collector, a robot arm, and a tennis rally.

The loop

The whole field fits in five nouns. The state is what the agent sees right now. The action is what it can do about it. The reward is the score for what just happened. The next state is the world after the action lands. And the policy is the agent's strategy, the thing being learned, usually a neural net.

Slow it down and watch one step happen. The 37 numbers say the agent is drifting forward, and a few of them just shifted the way they do when something is ahead. That is the state. The policy scores its four moves and picks forward. That is the action. The body steps, the something gets closer, no banana yet, reward zero. That is the reward and the next state, which is just a fresh set of 37 numbers. The policy updates by a hair. Then it all happens again, up to about 1.6 million steps, until "forward when something is ahead" is carved into the weights. Nothing in that loop knows what a banana is. The loop does not need to.

Walk it with the banana collector, a Unity ML-Agents environment from my Value-based-methods repo. The agent looks at the field (state), picks a direction (action), steps onto a yellow banana (+1 reward, happy) or a blue one (-1, sad), and the world updates. Then it does it again. And again. Up to about 1.6 million steps: sixteen hundred episodes, up to a thousand steps each, each step nudging the policy a hair toward whatever produced the +1s.

The plumbing is simpler than it sounds. Each step, the Python side sends one of the four moves to Unity. Unity steps the physics, moves the little collector, checks for banana collisions, and sends back 37 floats and a reward number. That round trip is the entire interface between the brain and the world: four integers out, 37 floats and one score back. ML-Agents is just the mailman. Everything interesting happens in what the Python side does with the mail.

The banana collector, up close

Let me show you the actual gym before we talk about learning. Forget the toy grid below for a minute; here is the real one.

Picture a square arena seen from above. The floor is scattered with bananas: yellow ones worth +1, blue ones worth -1. A little collector agent roams it, trying to run up the score. That is the whole world. From the camera it is almost cheerful: a bright floor, yellow and blue orbs scattered like candy, one small collector trundling between them, and a clock quietly running down.

Now the part that bends people's brains. The network never sees the pretty arena. It sees 37 numbers. Thirty-seven plain floats: some are the agent's own velocity, how fast it is moving and in which direction; the rest come from a fan of ray-cast sensors, like a cheap lidar, each ray reporting how far away something is and which tagged object it hit. That is the full description of reality the network gets. No camera, no picture of a banana. Just 37 numbers and a job.

The job comes as a menu, not a dial. Four moves: forward, back, left, right. No "a little to the left", no steering wheel. The network picks one of the four, the body executes it, and the world rolls on.

Training happens in episodes. One episode is a fixed stretch of time in the arena; the agent roams, bananas get eaten, the score adds up, and then the world resets. Score is everything: yellow +1, blue -1, summed up at the end. There is no demonstration of the correct path, because nobody knows what the correct path is.

What an episode feels like from inside

Now stand where the agent stands. Except there is no standing, and there is no seeing. An episode opens and the agent gets a stream of 37 numbers, refreshed every step, each one meaning absolutely nothing yet. A few of them jiggle when it moves, so they must have something to do with velocity. A few others shift when something drifts past, but what? There is no picture, no banana-shaped anything. The agent does not know what a banana is. It never will. It just gets 37 floats in, picks one of four moves out, and some steps later a number arrives: +1, -1, or nothing. That number is the only opinion the universe ever offers.

Imagine learning to cook blindfolded, where the only feedback is someone saying "good", "bad", or nothing at all, minutes after you touched the stove. That is the job. Every other tutorial on this site starts with a dataset, rows of examples with the answers attached. This one starts with a dark room and a scoreboard. Keep that image, because it explains everything that follows: why learning takes over a million steps, why exploration is a real dilemma and not a footnote, and why the reward had better say exactly what you mean.

How it learns: one line

The learning rule fits in one line, and the tool below walks it one update at a time:

$$ Q \leftarrow Q + \alpha \, (r + \gamma \max Q' - Q) $$

In plain words: move the old guess a little toward (what just happened plus what the future looks like). That is it. That is the engine. The DQN page teaches this rule term by term, with concrete numbers you can follow, including the wild part: the agent learns from its own guesses about the future, and the little anchors of real +1s and -1s are what keep those guesses honest. This page just needs the shape: one nudge per step, aimed at reality, and a strategy falls out the other end.

Roll the dice, then stop

Here is the dilemma the agent cannot escape. The Q-values say forward is worth 0.82. Should it always step forward? If it does, it never learns that right might be better. If it rolls the dice every turn, it never uses what it knows. Pure exploitation freezes early. Pure exploration never cashes in.

The fix is a coin flip with a knob, and the knob is called epsilon. With probability epsilon the agent rolls the dice and picks a random move; otherwise it takes the best Q. The knob turns itself down over training: full dice at the start, near-pure greed by the end, with a one-percent floor of randomness that never quite leaves. Decay the explorer into a hunter. The full schedule, the exact numbers from my run, and the tool that shows why the decay is load-bearing all live on the DQN page.

Why the policy has to be a neural net

Quick detour, because the one-line update hides a big decision. The update talks about $Q$ like it is a number you can look up. Look it up where? In a table, one row per state, one column per move. That works for tic-tac-toe. It does not work here, because the state is 37 continuous numbers, which means there are infinitely many states and the table would need infinitely many rows. The agent will never visit the same exact state twice.

So the policy, and the Q-values with it, live inside a neural net. Thirty-seven numbers in, four numbers out, and the net generalizes: states it has never seen get answers borrowed from states it has. That generalization is the whole reason the hunter can handle a banana spawning somewhere awkward. The price is that neural nets forget. Teach it the left side of the arena for a thousand episodes and it may unlearn the right side, which is exactly the wound the DQN page's two tricks are designed to close. For now, just hold the picture: the "brain" is a function approximator doing its best, and the loop is its teacher.

Why this is hard

If the loop is that simple, why did it take the field decades? Because three things about it are quietly brutal, and every algorithm on the next three pages exists to survive one of them.

First, credit assignment. The +1 arrives steps after the move that earned it. The agent turned left, then forward, then forward again, then ate the banana. Which move gets the credit? All of them, a little, fading with distance: that is what the gamma in the update is for. It smears the reward backward across the moves that led to it. Get the smearing wrong and the agent learns superstitions, like a pigeon that thinks flapping caused the food pellet.

Make it concrete. The agent turns left at step 40, drifts forward for fifteen steps, and eats the banana at step 57. The +1 lands at 57, but the decision that mattered was the turn at 40. The update smears the reward backward: step 56 gets most of the credit, step 40 gets a whisper of it, scaled by gamma raised to the distance. That whisper is doing all the real work. And when two bananas get eaten in one episode, the smears overlap, and the agent has to untangle which moves caused which +1. It does, slowly, over a million steps. But now you see why sparse rewards are so painful: stretch the distance between the move and the payoff far enough, and the whisper fades to nothing.

Second, exploration. You cannot learn what you never try, and trying costs blue bananas. Play it safe and you freeze on the first thing that ever scored. Flail forever and you never cash in. Epsilon is the compromise, and tuning it is half the craft. Every RL practitioner has a story about an agent that was either too timid or too drunk.

Third, hunger. The loop needs over a million steps. My banana run topped out near 1.6 million steps across sixteen hundred episodes; the arm needed twenty copies of itself training in parallel. This is why nobody trains RL on the real robot first. You train in simulation, where crashing is free, and then you hold your breath and transfer. Sample inefficiency is the tax RL pays for learning from scratch, and the whole field is, in some sense, a long argument about how to lower it.

Why not just show it the answer?

Fair question, and it has a real answer. The obvious alternative is behavior cloning: record an expert playing, train the network to copy the moves, done. No dice rolls, no million-plus steps, no blue-banana hobby phase. It works, right up until it doesn't.

The problem is compounding mistakes. The clone only ever saw the expert's perfect play, so the first time it drifts somewhere the expert never went, it has no idea what to do, drifts further, and the episode falls apart. It never learned to recover because it never saw failure. RL is slower precisely because it lives in failure for a while: the chaos era is the agent building a map of all the ways to be wrong, which is exactly what lets the hunter recover when a banana spawns somewhere awkward.

So the tradeoff is honest. Copying is fast and fragile. Reinforcement is slow and robust. When the world is predictable and the expert is available, clone. When the world is messy and nobody knows the right moves, and that was exactly my banana situation, you pay the million-plus steps and let the loop figure it out.

The agent is a clever lawyer

Here is the gotcha that bites everyone exactly once: the agent does not learn what you meant. It learns what you rewarded. If the reward has a loophole, the agent will find it and exploit it with zero shame. You just watched one vibrate in a corner. I learned this the embarrassing way: I once watched one of my agents do exactly that before I fixed the scoreboard.

So design the reward like the agent is a clever lawyer, because it is. Ask not "does this reward sound right" but "what is the dumbest way to score without doing the job". If you can find it in ten seconds, the optimizer will find it in ten thousand.

There is a second-order version of this trap, and it gets the careful people. A reward that only arrives at the very end gives the agent almost no signal, so people reach for shaping: small rewards for getting closer to the goal. Shaping works, right up until the lawyer reads the contract. Pay for getting closer to the banana and the agent learns to approach the banana and back away, approach and back away, farming approach rewards forever without ever eating. The field has the scars to prove it. Every shaped reward is a new sentence for the lawyer to misread, so shape as little as you can get away with, and run the ten-second dumb-test on the shaped version too.

The workflow that survives contact with reality looks like this: write the dumbest reward you can, run the ten-second lawyer test on paper, train a little, and watch what the agent actually does instead of what you hoped. Then fix the reward, not the agent. Nine times out of ten the "broken agent" is a working optimizer for a broken scoreboard. My corner-vibrator was not a bug in the network. It was the network telling me exactly what I had asked for.

One loop, three brains

This page was the loop every RL method shares: state, action, reward, next state, policy, the one-line update, the epsilon compromise. Now the three brains I actually built on it. Each one exists because the previous one hit a wall, so read them as one story, not three topics.

DQN, the menu judge. The banana brain works because the choice is a menu: four moves, one score each, pick the biggest number. Deep Q-Networks is that idea with a neural net doing the scoring: 37 numbers in, four Q-values out, argmax, done. Simple, honest, and hard-capped: the menu has to exist, and it has to be short. Four moves, fine. Four thousand moves, and the scoreboard becomes a joke. The moment the action is not a short discrete list, the judge has nothing to score.

DDPG, the actor and the critic. My robot arm does not have four moves. It has joints, and each joint takes a continuous torque. Not "push left or right" but "push exactly this hard". You cannot argmax over infinity, so DDPG splits the brain in two. The actor network looks at the state and outputs the move directly, a vector of torques. The critic network looks at the state and the move together and says how good that pair was. Same loop, no menu: the actor proposes, the critic grades, the actor climbs the critic's grades. If DQN is a judge picking from a menu, DDPG is a chef inventing the dish while a critic tastes.

Tennis, the moving target. The tennis page runs the same actor-critic machinery, but the setup is weirder: one shared brain drives both rackets, training against itself. Every rally feeds both sides' experience into a single shared buffer, so one policy learns to serve and to return. But the world refuses to sit still, because the opponent is the policy itself, improving every episode. That breaks the quiet assumption under everything so far: that the world behaves the same way twice. Yesterday's winning shot fails today because the other racket adapted overnight. Standing still is falling behind, because your practice partner gets better every episode.

So why three pages instead of one long one? Because each brain is the answer to the previous brain's wall. DQN hits the wall of discrete menus. DDPG climbs over it with an actor and a critic, then hits the wall of a world that fights back. Tennis climbs over that one with self-play: one shared brain, both rackets, every rally training both sides of the net. Same loop all the way down, stranger brain each time. That is the series: one loop, three brains, each earning its page.
