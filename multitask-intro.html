Introduction to Multitask Learning

One model, several tasks, and the fight over the shared trunk: the diseases, the geometry, and the field guide.

One model learning several tasks should be a bargain: shared features, less compute, each task teaching the others. The obvious way to train it breaks. The breakage has names: three diseases, one shared symptom, and a geometry that tells you which disease you have.

One model, many tasks

What if the best way to learn one thing is to learn several things at once?

Most machine learning does one thing at a time: one dataset, one loss, one skill. Multitask learning does the opposite on purpose. One model, several tasks, and the bet is that each task helps the others learn.

The bet is old by deep learning standards. Back in 1997, Rich Caruana noticed something striking: a network taught to recognize doors got better at doors when it was also taught to recognize doorknobs. The second task pushed the network toward the features that really matter, shapes and edges and handles, and away from the noise. Thirty years of vision, language, and robotics work has kept rediscovering the same lesson.

Hard parameter sharing

The workhorse design has a name: hard parameter sharing. One shared trunk learns to make sense of the input, and a small task-specific head per task turns that understanding into an answer.

Say it with symbols: The trunk's parameters are θsh, shared by everyone. Each head gets its own private parameters θi. On one training batch, each task reports its own loss Li, and the optimizer chases their sum.

L is the one number training tries to push down. Li is one task's loss on the batch. K is the number of tasks; on this page, three.

One number is all the optimizer sees. And that single number's gradient pushes on every head plus the one shared trunk. That shared push is the whole idea. It is also, as you are about to see, the whole problem.

Here is the design we just named. The solid arrows are the forward pass: one input through the trunk, each head produces its own loss. The dashed wires are the way back: every task's gradient flows into the same shared trunk.

Cheaper, better features, tasks teach each other

  1. It is cheaper. One trunk instead of K separate models means less memory, less compute, and one model to deploy. A self-driving car's vision system cannot afford ten networks per frame.
  2. Tasks teach each other. Labels for one task quietly act as training signal for the others. Knowing the depth of a scene helps with segmentation; knowing the segmentation helps with surface orientation.
  3. The model learns better features. A trunk that must please many tasks is pushed toward features that are useful in general. It cannot overfit the quirks of any single dataset. The extra tasks act like a built-in safety net.

Once you know what to look for, this design is everywhere: scene understanding (one network predicts segmentation, depth, and surface normals), robot control (one policy handles 10 to 50 tasks), multilingual language models (dozens of languages), and recommendation systems that must care about clicks, watch time, and satisfaction all at once.

Tasks fight over the shared trunk

The catch. Train the tasks together in the obvious way, just add the losses and go, and you will often find that every task does worse than it would have on its own. The shared trunk is not a free lunch. It is a shared resource, and the tasks fight over it.

So here is the question the next section answers. Train the obvious way and every task does worse. Why?

Add the weighted losses

Every task gets a volume knob: setting it to 2 makes that task count double. The total loss is the volume-weighted sum of the task losses. Each training step nudges the model's knobs downhill on that total by a small step size, and the arrow means the nudged knobs replace the old ones.

$$L(\theta) = \sum_i w_i L_i(\theta) \qquad \theta \leftarrow \theta - \alpha \nabla L(\theta)$$

if one task's loss towers over the other's, the sum listens to the loud task almost exclusively, and the quiet one is a rounding error.

That is not a bug in the arithmetic; it is the naive recipe working exactly as written. And the fix is not obvious: to give task 2 an equal vote you must crank its weight into the thousands, and the right number changes as training proceeds, because the losses themselves move.

Three different failures, one shared symptom

With equal weights, "train on the sum" is what most codebases do first. Sometimes it works. Often it quietly fails: one task stalls far behind what it could do alone, or all of them get worse. When the multitask model loses to separate single-task models, researchers call it negative transfer. The failure is usually diagnosed from a loss curve, but the loss curve hides which of three different diseases you actually have. They look alike from the outside and demand different fixes.

Scale: the loud task wins every vote

A depth loss near 10³ and a classification loss near 1 are just numbers to gradient descent. The task with big numbers produces the big gradient, which swamps the others. Hand-tuning weights means searching K knobs, and doing it again every time the task set changes.

The scale panel above starts level: at 1x the two bars match. Sweep the slider up: by 300x task 2 owns 99.7% of every shared update, and task 1's bar is a sliver you can barely see. Nothing about task 2 is more important here. It is just louder.

Tasks learn at different speeds

One task may be nearly solved in the first 10% of training while another improves until the end. The fast task's gradients shrink as it finishes, so the weighting that was fair at step 100 is unfair at step 10,000. The balance must move with training.

The speed panel starts at step 2 with both tasks near full strength. Sweep the marker across training: passing step 30, the fast task's gradient has shrunk to 19% of its start while the slow task still has 72%. No fixed pair of weights is right at both step 2 and step 100, which is why a weighting you tuned early in a run can be wrong by the end of it.

Direction: the arrows fight

Two gradients can point against each other, a negative cosine, so the average update contains a piece that actively raises one task's loss. Task gradients fight on the large majority of training steps, and a whisper turned up is still pointing the wrong way.

The direction panel starts at 0 degrees with the arrows in full agreement. Rotate task 2's gradient: near 100 degrees the cosine turns negative and the badge turns amber, the arrows are fighting. Keep rotating to 180 degrees: the two gradients point opposite ways, the cosine reads -0.99, and the amber sum arrow pushes task 2 uphill, which is exactly what its negative score means. This is the disease no volume knob can fix. Scaling a gradient changes its length, never its direction.

The math kit: norms, dot products, gradients

All of this was geometry: arrows with sizes and directions, added together, sometimes fighting. The conflict is geometry, and the fixes are geometry too. Every fix does one of three things to gradient arrows: it measures how loud they are, checks how much they agree, or cuts out the part that fights. So the whole math kit is five ideas, and each one is simple once seen the right way.

A vector is just an arrow on a map: (3, 1) means walk 3 east, 1 north. A model's parameters are a vector too, one with millions of entries; I cannot draw that map, but every fact about flat 2-D arrows stays true in it, which is why every picture here gets away with flat arrows. The norm is the arrow's length

The name: the norm, written ‖v‖. it is the arrow's length. The recipe: square every entry, add them up, take the square root. That is Pythagoras wearing a hat.

Whenever I call a task's gradient "loud", I mean exactly this number is large. Loudness is a norm. Drag v in panel (a) to a short stub and a long spear and watch the number track the length.

The dot product is an agreement meter

The name: the dot product, written ⟨u, v⟩. an agreement meter. The recipe: multiply matching entries and add.

The meter also equals the two lengths times the cosine of the angle between the arrows, so its sign is the verdict: positive means roughly the same direction, zero means exactly perpendicular, negative means they partly oppose each other.

Dividing by the lengths gives the cosine similarity, a scale-free agreement score between -1 and 1. Scale-free because a loud arrow and a quiet arrow pointing the same way score the same. In panel (b), both pulls are draggable: this sign check is the standard conflict test, and a negative result means the two pulls partly oppose each other.

Projection is one arrow's shadow on another

The name: the projection. the shadow one arrow casts on another's line. The recipe: take the agreement score, divide by the second arrow's squared length, and scale the second arrow itself by that.

When two gradients point against each other, PCGrad removes the conflicting part: it subtracts each gradient's projection onto the other. What remains points in a direction that helps both tasks.

A gradient is a pull

The name: the gradient, written ∇L(θ). the arrow pointing straight uphill from where I stand on the loss landscape, with length equal to the steepness. Panel (d) draws a hill as contours; the pull always points across the contours, straight uphill.

I treat each task's gradient as a pull on the shared parameters. Training follows the opposite pull, straight downhill. Each task has its own landscape and therefore its own pull, and those pulls need not point the same way. That sentence is the seed of every problem in this series.

A convex blend is a way to split 100%

The name: a convex combination. a blend where every weight is at least zero and the weights add to one, so each task gets a percentage of the mix. The set of all legal weight lists is the simplex, written Δ.

Two more bits of vocabulary I will use everywhere: the minimum is the lowest value a function can reach; the argmin is the setting that reaches it: the best knob position, not the best score.

Why blends matter so much: blending is the cheapest way for tasks to compromise, and one fact pays off twice later: a blend can never do worse than its worst ingredient. If every task's pull likes a step, every blend likes it too. So "search over all blends" often turns out to be the same problem as "protect the worst task", only much cheaper to solve. In the MGDA section below I search over these blends for the shortest arrow I am legally allowed to make.

All of this was geometry: arrows with sizes and directions, added together, sometimes fighting. The conflict is geometry, and now the moving parts are named: loudness, agreement, and shadows. The next section asks what to do when the arrows fight. The answer has a name: the Pareto front.

The dot-product test: does this step help or hurt each task

Picture two tasks sharing one set of knobs. You are about to take one small step in some direction, and you want to know, task by task, whether the step helps or hurts. The answer is one dot product.

Why a dot product? The actual move steps opposite to the chosen direction, scaled by the step size, which is positive. Each task's loss changes by roughly the step size times the dot product of that task's gradient with the direction: slope times move, knob by knob. Since the step size is positive, the sign of the dot product is the whole verdict. A positive dot product means the direction points along the task's uphill arrow, so the actual move goes downhill for that task. A negative dot product means the move goes uphill: the task gets worse.

So the betrayal test is one line: whenever some task's dot product with the usual update comes out negative, the usual update makes that task worse. The majority outvotes a minority of one. Keep your eye on this dot product. Every method in this series is really just a different way of picking a direction so that no task's score comes out too negative.

The three failures, measured

Two tasks share two knobs, so every gradient is an arrow with two entries, small enough to draw. These numbers are invented, chosen so we can draw everything. The first arrow points east with length 2. The second points west and slightly north, length about 1.1.

Plain training follows the average of the two arrows. Notice it leans the first task's way: the first arrow is longer, so it pulls the average toward itself.

Now the referee scores the step. Task 1's score is positive, so the step helps task 1. Task 2's score is negative, so the very same step pushes task 2's loss up.

That is the entire conflict problem: the loud task outvotes the quiet one. The next section asks what "optimal" even means when two models trade wins across tasks.

MGDA: step along the shortest blend

The Pareto set: a whole curve of trade-offs

With one loss, comparing two models is easy: lower wins. With K losses, model A can beat model B on task 1 while losing on task 2. Neither is "better," full stop. So "optimal" needs a new meaning.

  1. Pareto-dominated: some other point is at least as good on every task and strictly better on one. There is a free upgrade available, so why stay?
  2. Pareto-optimal: nothing dominates it. You cannot improve any task without hurting another. All the free upgrades are used up. The set of all such points is the Pareto set.
  3. Pareto-stationary: some blend of the task gradients cancels out to zero there. Every Pareto-optimal point is Pareto-stationary, just as every single-task minimum has zero gradient.

Stationary is the target: at a Pareto-stationary point, some legal blend of the task gradients has length exactly zero. The pulls cancel.

Here is the catch: the Pareto set is not one point. It is a whole curve of trade-offs.

So how do you actually land on the frontier? The classical answer is MGDA (Sener and Koltun, 2018): blend the task gradients into the shortest possible combination and step along it. It is proven to reach some Pareto-stationary point.

The authors' starting point: multi-task learning is inherently a multi-objective problem, because tasks conflict and force a trade-off. The standard compromise, a weighted linear combination of per-task losses, is only valid when the tasks do not compete, which is rarely true. Their move was to aim for Pareto-optimal solutions directly.

The textbook MGDA (Désidéri, 2012) provably reaches a Pareto-stationary point, but it needs explicit per-task gradients and does not scale to deep nets. Their contribution is a Frank-Wolfe optimizer with an upper bound computable in a single backward pass, so the overhead is negligible, and they prove it still yields a Pareto-optimal solution under realistic assumptions.

MGDA picks a Pareto point, but not which one

MGDA is proven to reach some Pareto-stationary point. But "some" hides a problem: which point you reach is decided by where you started, not by anything you chose. The average loss, the thing you actually wanted to minimize, is left to luck.

Think of the Pareto set as a menu of all the fair compromises. MGDA only promises to seat you somewhere on the menu; the kitchen picks the dish. Minimizing the average loss is ordering a specific dish. You want the dish you ordered, plus a guarantee that the kitchen is actually cooking it.

Closing that gap, ordering the dish instead of letting the kitchen pick, is the job of the method pages.

One honest caveat before you go: Pareto-stationary is a weaker promise than Pareto-optimal. A blend can cancel to zero at a point that is only a standoff, not a true optimum, the same way a zero gradient holds at a single-task saddle. MGDA promises stationarity, not the dish you ordered.

So the frontier is real, and there is a principled way to land on it. But none of this turns the loud task down: task 1's arrow is still twice as long as task 2's, and it still outvotes the quiet one in every average. Knowing the frontier exists is only half the job. Here is the question the next page answers: how do we fix the loudness first?

Diagnose your failure mode first

Three lines of logging tell you which of the three diseases you actually have. Only then do you pick the fix.

  1. Each task's loss, compared with training that task alone. A gap here is negative transfer, by definition. The other two measurements explain why the gap is there.
  2. Each task's gradient norm at a shared layer (the size of that task's gradient on the shared trunk). If one is orders of magnitude bigger than the others, you have a scale problem, and the fix is to rebalance how loudly the tasks speak.
  3. The cosine between each pair of task gradients. Often negative means a direction problem, and the fix is to remove the fighting component from the update. Once plain gradient descent is trapped in a ravine, the two gradients can keep fighting for the rest of the run. The conflict never resolves itself.

Both measurements are one line of arithmetic, but the intuition comes first. A gradient norm is just the length of a task's arrow on the shared trunk: how loudly that task speaks in the update. If one task is far louder than the rest, its arrow decides every step. Measure the norms at the shared layer: if one task's arrow is far longer than the others, it shouts far louder, and in the plain sum the quiet gradients are a rounding error. That is the scale disease, and the fix is rebalancing the tasks' loudness.

For direction, I want one number that says whether two arrows pull together or fight. That number is the cosine of the angle between them: 1 means the same direction, 0 means perpendicular, -1 means exactly opposed. It is the two arrows' agreement (their dot product) divided by their lengths, so it is scale-free.

A cosine near -1 means nearly exactly opposed: their average is a coin flip decided by whoever is louder. That is the direction disease, and the fix is removing the fighting component from the update.

The same three diseases show up as symptoms first. This is the table I keep taped to the wall:

Five multitask mistakes to avoid

Five mistakes I have watched teams make, including mine:

  1. Watching only the average loss. A great average can hide a task that was quietly sacrificed. I always report per-task numbers too, ideally the average per-task drop against single-task baselines.
  2. Cutting gradients in the task-specific heads. Heads share no parameters, so there is nothing to fight over. Combine gradients on the shared trunk only.
  3. Forgetting to renormalize after reweighting. Without it, the weights slowly change the overall learning speed, and the comparison against baselines stops meaning anything.
  4. Running full K-task surgery on a 50-task problem. K backward passes per step is the real price of per-pair direction surgery. Use pair subsampling or a cheaper approximation.
  5. Treating every conflict as poison. The tragic triad says conflict mostly hurts when it comes with a loudness gap and high curvature. Mild conflict between equals is often harmless.

Multitask deployment: what actually matters

"Our tasks have disjoint datasets. Does this still apply?" Yes, and this is the most common misconception I run into. Every task can have its own dataset and its own head; the gradients still meet in one place, the shared backbone. Conflict is a property of gradients on shared parameters, not of shared data.

The per-task dataset, per-task head, shared-trunk setup is the standard case these methods were built on. They would only become irrelevant if the tasks shared nothing at all, and then it would not be multitask learning. To convince a skeptic, log the cosine similarity between pairs of task gradients on the shared trunk for a few hundred steps. If the cosines go negative, and they almost always do, the conflict is real.

"What if one task's batch is much smaller?" A small batch makes a task's gradient jittery: averaged over 8 examples instead of 128, the direction swings step to step, even though it is honest on average. Plain averaging just absorbs the noise. Loudness rebalancing occasionally turns a knob on a bad reading but self-corrects. Direction surgery sometimes cuts against a fight that was not real, which is wasteful, not dangerous.

Steering toward the worst-off task is the most sensitive: its whole decision is "who is worst off right now," and a jittery signal sometimes makes the small-batch task look worst-off when it simply had an unlucky batch. Keeping each step near the plain average caps how far one noisy step can pull the update. The fixes, in rough order of importance:

  1. Oversample the small task. Going from 8 to 32 samples per step already helps a lot. No algorithmic patch beats a steadier signal.
  2. Smooth the decision signal. Keep an exponential moving average of each task's gradient (decay 0.9 to 0.99), decide on the smoothed gradients, step with the real ones. This composes cleanly with worst-off-task steering: it keeps the protection and the guarantee but stops reacting to one unlucky batch.
  3. Tighten the leash. If you cannot oversample, keep every step closer to the plain average instead of chasing the worst-off task aggressively.
  4. Log the variance, not just the average. If the small task's gradient norm swings wildly over a few dozen steps, you have confirmed a shaky signal before it confuses the algorithm.

Batch jointly every step

Three layouts, and only one of them lets the methods do their job:

  1. Joint batch: all tasks every step. This is the standard, and my recommendation. Each task produces a loss and a gradient on the shared trunk at the same weights at the same moment, which is exactly what "do these gradients fight?" and "which task is worst off?" require.
  2. Round robin: one task per step. The methods become no-ops: no pair to de-conflict, no worst-off task to find. Worse, round robin does not remove the conflict. It spreads it across time, as the backbone zigzags between tasks, the same pacing that traps plain gradient descent. It removes the diagnostic, not the disease.
  3. Random task per step. Same problem as round robin, shuffled. Incompatible with gradient-combining methods.

Within a joint batch, I prefer equal shares per task, oversampling small datasets so every gradient is measured with equal reliability. The whole point is to let the gradients decide the weighting, which only works if every gradient is trustworthy.

If repeating a small dataset worries you, cap the repetition (for example, no task repeats its data more than three times per epoch of the largest) rather than shrinking its share. And if a joint batch does not fit in memory, accumulate: run each task's sub-batch, store its gradient, and combine once all tasks have reported. The direction-fixing methods need all task gradients before combining anyway, so accumulation changes where you pay the memory cost, not whether you pay it.

Only bring losses that must shape the trunk

A common situation: each team arrives with its own mature single-task model, trained with half a dozen losses. Should all of them come into multitask training? No. The backbone is the only shared, one-shot resource in the whole system. After multitask training, a team takes the backbone, freezes it, and fine-tunes its own head privately, where it can use whatever losses it wants. So each loss faces one question: does this loss need to shape the shared features, or can it do its job later, on the frozen backbone, inside my own head?

Extra losses are expensive: every added loss is another gradient pulling the trunk; the cost is paid by every team, not just the one who brought it; and the loss weights a team tuned for its single-task model no longer mean anything once loudness rebalancing or worst-off-task steering is involved. The practical filter is the frozen-head test:

  1. Baseline. Multitask-train with each task contributing exactly one loss, its main one. Measure every task's metric.
  2. Frozen-head check. Freeze the backbone; each team fine-tunes its head with its full private stack. Measure again.
  3. Gap analysis. If frozen-head fine-tuning reaches the target, the extra losses were head-level and stay private. If the metric is stuck no matter what the head does, the backbone never learned something the task needs, and that loss belongs in multitask training.
  4. Admit one loss at a time, and re-run. Confirm it closes the gap, and check the other tasks' metrics did not degrade.

Tips that save real time: start with one loss per task, no exceptions. The minimal baseline is the control group that makes every later decision measurable. Freeze the backbone before you argue. Keep the receipt when a loss joins: the other tasks' metrics and the conflict rate, before and after. Pre-combine a task's main and auxiliary losses with fixed weights from its single-task tuning history, so the multitask trainer sees one voice per task. And let the conflict measurements referee team debates. Re-run the frozen-head check after big changes. The verdict on a loss is not permanent.

When the trunk is too small, no optimizer can save it

This is the part I consider just as important as the methods. Gradient-level fixes redistribute a shortage; they do not remove it. If the shared trunk is simply too small to hold all the tasks, or the tasks genuinely work against each other, no optimizer can fix that. The limit is then set by the architecture (wider trunks, softer sharing, task-specific modules) and by task grouping (which tasks should share a trunk at all).

Optimization methods decide how well I use the sharing I chose; they cannot choose it for me. When a multitask setup is failing, my first question is no longer which optimizer to try. It is whether the tasks belong together and whether the trunk has room for them. The optimizer is the second question, not the first.

Open problems

The open problems I take seriously are the ones this series does not solve. First, objectives beyond the plain average: letting users set priorities instead of pretending every task matters equally. Second, guarantees that hold with noisy gradients and many tasks, not just the clean two-task toy setups. Third, principled ways to combine weighting, surgery, and architecture choices instead of treating them as three separate literatures that never talk to each other.

That is where I would put my effort if I were starting a project in this space today. The series ends here; the field does not.

The series in one paragraph: three diseases, three kinds of fixes

The one-paragraph summary. Multitask training fails quietly, in three ways: one task can be so loud it drowns the rest (scale), tasks can learn at different speeds so a fixed balance is always wrong for someone (speed), and tasks can pull in opposite directions (direction).

The first two are fixed by rebalancing how loudly each task speaks as training runs. The third is fixed by reshaping the update itself: remove the part of each gradient that fights the others, or steer toward the trade-off point you actually want instead of letting the starting point pick it. Diagnose with gradient norms and cosines, then pick your fix.

The first method page starts with the simplest of the three diseases: one task shouting over the rest. How do you keep every task equally loud without hand-tuning a weight per task? That is the next page's question.
