Recommender Systems

1 The sparse rating matrix

Each user rated a handful out of thousands of movies, so almost every cell is empty. Recommending means predicting those cells.

2 Matrix factorization

Matrix factorization (Koren et al., 2009) gives every user a vector \(p_{u}\) and every movie a vector \(q_{i}\) of \(K\) latent factors, learned numbers with no preset meaning (a larger \(K\) fits finer preferences and overfits sooner; the notebook uses a fixed size), plus a user bias and a movie bias.

MovieLens ratings run from 0.5 to 5 stars. A guess for an empty cell comes from the user's vector, the movie's vector and two biases:

A rating is guessed from how well a user’s taste lines up with a movie’s traits, plus two habits: how generously the user rates and how widely loved the movie is.
\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=rhat}{\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\hat{r}_{ui}}}} = \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=pu}{\htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{p_{u}}}}\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=dot}{\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\cdot }}}\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=qi}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{q_{i}}}} + \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=bu}{\htmlStyle{color:#c0392b}{b_{u}}}} + \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=bi}{\htmlStyle{color:#196f3d}{b_{i}}}}\)
The predicted rating \(\hat{r}_{ui}\) of user \(u\) for movie \(i\) is the dot product of the user vector \(p_{u}\) and the movie vector \(q_{i}\) (high when they line up), plus \(b_{u}\), because some people rate everything high, plus \(b_{i}\), because some movies are just widely loved. The notebook uses this line, so the two biases also carry the overall average.
\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=rhat}{\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\hat{r}_{ui}}}} = \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=mu}{\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\mu }}} + \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=bu}{\htmlStyle{color:#c0392b}{b_{u}}}} + \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=bi}{\htmlStyle{color:#196f3d}{b_{i}}}} + \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=pu}{\htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{p_{u}}}}\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=dot}{\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\cdot }}}\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=qi}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{q_{i}}}}\)
Koren et al. add \(\mu\), the average of all known ratings, so each bias is only an offset from it: positive for a user who rates generously, negative for a harsh one. \(\mu\) is one fixed number, not learned and never penalised.
So a predicted rating is the dot of two learned vectors plus two biases: the dot says how well this user's taste fits this movie, and the biases say how generous the user is and how loved the movie is. The next eq card says how the vectors and biases are learned from the ratings people gave.

Nothing is hand-picked: training fits all the vectors and biases to the ratings you have by lowering one cost: the squared misses on the known ratings, plus a small penalty, set by \(\lambda\), on the size of every vector and bias.

Learning means making the guesses match the ratings people actually gave, while keeping every vector and bias small so users with few ratings do not get wild guesses.
\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-objective)}{J} = \sum_{(u,i)\ \text{rated}} [ (r_{ui} - \htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\hat{r}_{ui}})^{2}\)
It compares the guess \(\hat{r}_{ui}\) of the eq card above with the real rating. \(\sum\) adds one term for every pair \((u, i)\) where user \(u\) actually rated movie \(i\); the empty cells never enter, so the model fits on a table that is almost all blanks. \(r_{ui}\) is the real rating and \(\hat{r}_{ui}\) the prediction from the card above; squaring makes a big miss cost far more than a small one, so training works hardest on its worst guesses.
\(\displaystyle + \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=lam}{\htmlStyle{color:#7d6608}{\lambda }}}\lVert \htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{p_{u}}\rVert ^{2} + \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=lam}{\htmlStyle{color:#7d6608}{\lambda }}}\lVert \htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{q_{i}}\rVert ^{2}\)
Each rated pair also pays \(\lambda\) (lambda) times the squared lengths \(\lVert \cdot \rVert^{2}\) of its two vectors, so long vectors cost more.
\(\displaystyle + \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=lam}{\htmlStyle{color:#7d6608}{\lambda }}}\htmlStyle{color:#c0392b}{b_{u}^{\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{2}}} + \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=lam}{\htmlStyle{color:#7d6608}{\lambda }}}\htmlStyle{color:#196f3d}{b_{i}^{\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{2}}} ]\)
It pays \(\lambda\) times the squares of its two biases too. With \(\mu\) in the prediction, raising \(\lambda\) keeps vectors short and predictions near \(\mu\) plus the biases, which steadies users with few ratings but underfits, missing even the training ratings; lowering it fits the training ratings more closely and overfits sooner; \(\lambda = 0\) removes the penalty.
So \(J\) is the squared misses on known ratings plus a small size tax; the eq card in Section 3 steps through one prediction that this cost then corrects.

predict computes the prediction card's first line for a batch of user and movie pairs; Section 7 trains the tables with the cost \(J\).

def predict(p_u, q_i, b_u, b_i):
    # p_u, q_i: (B, K)
    dot = (p_u * q_i).sum(dim=1)
    return dot + b_u + b_i

3 Predicting one rating

Figure 2. One prediction for one user and Toy Story from toy factor vectors.

One prediction is built in two moves: match the user’s taste to the movie’s traits, then adjust for the user’s habit and the movie’s appeal.
\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=rhat}{\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\hat{r}_{ui}}}} = \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=pu}{\htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{p_{u}}}} \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=dot}{\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\cdot }}} \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=qi}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{q_{i}}}} + \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=bu}{\htmlStyle{color:#c0392b}{b_{u}}}} + \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=bi}{\htmlStyle{color:#196f3d}{b_{i}}}}\)
This is the first line of the prediction card in Section 2, and its miss is what the cost there corrects. The predicted rating dots the user vector with the movie vector, then adjusts for how generous the user is and how popular the movie is.
So one rating is a taste dot plus two habits: here Toy Story’s appeal lifts the guess far more than the user’s habit lowers it, and the guess lands on the real rating. The eq card in Section 4 compares two movies by their vectors instead of a user and a movie.
\(p_{u}\) the user vector\(q_{i}\) the movie vector\(\cdot\) the dot product, their affinity\(b_{u}\) the user's rating habit\(b_{i}\) the movie's general appeal\(\hat{r}_{ui}\) the predicted rating

4 Movie similarity measures

Similarity answers "more like this movie" and covers a new movie with no ratings yet (cold start, Section 8). Rating similarity compares two movie columns of the rating matrix \(R\) (zeros where nobody rated). Genre similarity compares two genre vectors from the genre matrix \(G\) (1 if the movie has the genre, 0 if not). Both use cosine similarity. The two can disagree: two movies with exactly the same genres score the top value by genres even when different people liked them.

Two movies are similar when their vectors point the same way, whatever their lengths; cosine reads only that direction.
\(\displaystyle \cos (\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=a}{\htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{a}}}, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=b}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{b}}}) = \frac{\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=a}{\htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{a}}} \cdot \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=b}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{b}}}}{\lVert \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=a}{\htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{a}}}\rVert \cdot \lVert \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=b}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{b}}}\rVert }\)
The eq card in Section 3 compared a user with a movie; this one compares two movies. \(a\) and \(b\) are two movies’ vectors, their columns of ratings or their genre bits. The top is their dot product: multiply matching entries and add. The bottom divides by both lengths, so the score reads only direction, never size: 1 means they point the same way and 0 means nothing shared.
So cosine says how alike two movies point, by ratings or by genre; the eq card in Section 6 turns the genre cosine into a bonus on the learned rating.
import numpy as np

def cosine(a, b):
    na = np.linalg.norm(a)
    nb = np.linalg.norm(b)
    return a @ b / (na * nb)

r_ts = R[:, ts_in_R]
r_ts2 = R[:, ts2_in_R]
rating_sim = cosine(r_ts, r_ts2)
g_ts = G[ts_in_G]
g_ts2 = G[ts2_in_G]
genre_sim = cosine(g_ts, g_ts2)

6 The hybrid recommender

The notebook's hybrid adds one genre signal to the learned score: each movie gets a bonus for how closely its genres match its nearest genre neighbor.

The hybrid adds a genre bonus to the learned rating, so a movie that resembles another movie by genre gets a lift even before many people rate it.
\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\hat{r}_{ui}} = \htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{p_{u}}\cdot \htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{q_{i}} + \htmlStyle{color:#c0392b}{b_{u}} + \htmlStyle{color:#196f3d}{b_{i}} + \htmlStyle{color:#7d6608}{s_{i}}\)
It is the prediction of the first eq card in Section 2 plus \(s_{i}\), a bonus for movie \(i\) built from the genre cosine of the eq card in Section 4. It is the same for every user and close to 1 for most movies.
\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#7d6608}{s_{i}} = \max _{j\ne i} \cos _{\text{genre}}(i, j)\)
Movie \(i\)'s strongest genre-cosine with any other movie \(j\); its match with itself is dropped.
So the hybrid is the learned rating plus the strongest genre match. Once the hybrid is refit, the item bias can absorb that bonus, so it wires genres in without making a better model. End of the chain.

genre_cos holds the genre cosine of every movie pair, with each movie's match to itself set to 0.

def hybrid(mf, genre_cos, ids):
    # genre_cos: (movies, movies)
    best = genre_cos[ids].max(1)
    return mf + best.values

The notebook fits the hybrid's model the same way, for a few full-batch steps.

7 Matrix factorization in practice

Builds on SGD (SGD, Momentum and Nesterov, section 1) and Adam (Adaptive Optimizers: AdaGrad, RMSProp and Adam, section 4)

The full training loop in PyTorch lowers the cost \(J\) from Section 2. The inputs users, items and ratings hold one entry per known rating, with user and movie ids already mapped to rows 0 to \(n - 1\).

from torch import nn
import torch.nn.functional as F
from torch.optim import Adam
from torch.utils.data import DataLoader
from torch.utils.data import TensorDataset

# users, items: LongTensor (N,)
# ratings: FloatTensor (N,)
# N_ITEMS = movies in movies.csv
n_users, n_items = N_USERS, N_ITEMS
K, B, lam = K_FACTORS, BATCH_SIZE, LAMBDA  # no penalty, as in the notebook
P = nn.Embedding(n_users, K)
Q = nn.Embedding(n_items, K)
b_u = nn.Embedding(n_users, 1)
b_i = nn.Embedding(n_items, 1)
parts = [P, Q, b_u, b_i]
mods = nn.ModuleList(parts)
params = mods.parameters()
opt = Adam(params, lr=LEARNING_RATE)
TD = TensorDataset
data = TD(users, items, ratings)
dl = DataLoader(data, B, True)

def sq(t):
    return t.pow(2).sum()

for epoch in range(N_EPOCHS):
    for u, i, r in dl:
        bu = b_u(u).squeeze(1)
        bi = b_i(i).squeeze(1)
        dot = (P(u) * Q(i)).sum(1)
        pred = dot + bu + bi
        mse = F.mse_loss(pred, r)
        pen = sq(bu) + sq(bi)
        pen = pen + sq(P(u))
        pen = pen + sq(Q(i))
        n = len(r)
        loss = mse + lam * pen / n
        opt.zero_grad()
        loss.backward()
        opt.step()
  1. 1Vector and bias tables with \(K\) factors, and Adam over all four.
  2. 2Shuffled batches of known ratings.
  3. 3Prediction card in Section 2, then the mean squared error, cost line 1 divided by the batch size.
  4. 4Penalty \(\lambda\), cost lines 2 and 3, divided by the batch size to match the mean; Surprise uses \(\lambda = 0.02\).
  5. 5One Adam step per batch.

The notebook takes one Adam step (gradient descent with a per-weight step size) per batch of ratings, for many epochs. A step costs about twice a prediction (scores, then gradients) for each rating in the batch; once trained, one prediction is one dot product of length \(K\).

Recommending scores every movie for one user with the prediction card, hides the ones already rated, and returns the \(k\) highest. It reuses the trained tables above; R is the rating table with rows and columns in the same order, 0 where unrated.

import numpy as np

def arr(emb):
    w = emb.weight.detach()
    return w.numpy()

Pn, Qn = arr(P), arr(Q)
bu = arr(b_u)[:, 0]
bi = arr(b_i)[:, 0]
u, k = USER_IDX, TOP_K  # userId 608
s = Qn @ Pn[u] + bu[u] + bi
seen = R[u] > 0
s = np.where(seen, -np.inf, s)
top_k = np.argsort(-s)[:k]

  2. 2Predicted rating for every movie, prediction card in Section 2.
  3. 3Hides movies the user already rated.
  4. 4Top \(k\) predictions.

Adam keeps two running averages per weight, three copies of every vector in all. Surprise, a Python library for recommenders, ships the same model under the name SVD (a historical name; it is this factorization, not the SVD of the PCA page) and trains it by plain SGD:

SettingWhere it entersRaise itLower itDefault
n_factorsLength of \(p_{u}\) and \(q_{i}\)Finer patterns, more overfittingCoarser; 1 is one shared axis100
reg_all, \(\lambda\)Adds \(\lambda\) times every squared vector and bias to the errorVectors shrink toward 00 means no penalty; rare users overfit0.02
lr_allStep size of each SGD updateToo high divergesToo low fits slowly0.005
n_epochsPasses over the ratingsFits training ratings closerUnderfits20

Hide some known ratings from \(J\), fit on the rest, and score the hidden ones: they stand in for the empty cells you will actually recommend. A model that fits the rated cells but misses the hidden ones has overfit, memorizing its training ratings instead of learning tastes that carry over. Split ratings by user or time, not random rows only, or the score flatters the model. If you use AdamW instead, its weight decay (default 0.01) shrinks every row each step, even rows of users absent from the batch; the penalty in the loop above touches only the batch's rows.

Take the real ratings of Section 5, hide a few scattered across users and movies, the easy kind of split, and fit the rest by plain gradient descent on \(J\), all of them in every step, with \(\mu\) in the prediction and a pair of factors, so each vector is an arrow in a plane. The miss on a set of ratings is their typical gap in stars: square the gaps between predicted and real, average them, and take the square root.

8 Strengths and limits of matrix factorization

Learned vectors need plenty of ratings per user and per movie; three gaps have known fixes.

CauseSymptomFix
Cold starta new movie or user has no ratings, so no vectorgenre similarity until ratings arrive
Sparse usersa few ratings give a noisy user vectorregularize the vectors or fall back to popular picks
Popularity biaswidely loved movies get recommended to everyonere-rank the list to favor surprising picks

Every row of the predicted table is a mix of just \(K\) movie-vector patterns, the same idea as the eigenfaces in PCA.
