Self-Supervised Learning Failure Modes and Checks

This page follows Masked Image Modeling.

Every method in this series shares one setup. I take one image and make two views of it with random augmentations (crops, colour changes, blur). A shared encoder, the network that turns an input into a vector, maps each view to an embedding, that vector, and the two views of one image form a positive pair that the loss pulls together.

Training fails when the network finds an easier answer than learning what the image shows. The worst one is collapse, every input mapped to the same vector, which makes every positive pair match perfectly. I check the result with a linear probe, a linear classifier trained on the frozen embeddings with a few labels. Each section below gives the symptom, why it happens, the fix and the source.

Overlapping receptive fields let the network match low-level signal instead of content

An output's receptive field is the part of the input it is computed from. In tasks that predict one patch or time step from another, if the two receptive fields overlap or touch, the network can solve the task from shared low-level signal (a continuing edge, a smooth waveform, a lens colour fringe) and learn no content.

Doersch's patch task needed a gap, jitter and a colour fix

Doersch et al. (2015) trained on the 1.3 million ImageNet photos with the labels thrown away. From each photo they cut a 96 px patch and one of its eight neighbours, and the network had to say which of the eight positions the second patch came from. Each patch goes through its own copy of the network (the copies share weights) until a late layer, so each copy's receptive field is exactly its patch. The paper found three shortcuts, each with a fix:

  • Edges or textures that continue from one patch into the other give the position away. The fix is a gap of about half a patch, 48 px, between the two patches.
  • Even across the gap, a long straight line through both patches lines up, and that still gives the position away. The fix is jitter: each patch is moved at random by up to 7 px, so the line no longer meets the second patch at a predictable point.
  • Even then the network found chromatic aberration, the colour fringe a lens makes because it focuses colours slightly differently. One colour channel, commonly green, is shrunk toward the image centre, so the fringe tells where on the lens a patch sat. The fixes remove the green and magenta colour direction from each patch, or drop two of the three colour channels and put noise in their place.

The toy below is the same patch task. Move the second patch from overlapping to a wide gap and switch jitter on and off; the word at the top says whether a low-level clue still gives the position away, and the coloured mark shows which clue.

Only a wide gap plus jitter leaves content as the clue (toy, after Doersch 2015).

CPC audio predicts far enough ahead to leave the overlap behind

CPC (Contrastive Predictive Coding, van den Oord et al. 2018) predicts the future of a signal in a learned vector space instead of predicting raw samples. The paper notes that predicting only the next step exploits the local smoothness of the signal; predicting further ahead leaves less shared information, so the model has to infer more global structure.

The audio setup: 100 hours of LibriSpeech read speech. A stack of five strided convolutions runs on the raw waveform (the audio samples themselves, 16,000 per second) and gives one vector every 10 ms. A recurrent network summarises the past vectors into a context, and the model predicts 12 steps ahead. The test is a linear classifier on the learned features that names the phone, the speech sound being spoken, from 41 classes.

My arithmetic from the paper's filter sizes and strides: each vector is computed from 465 samples, about 29 ms of audio, and neighbouring vectors start 160 samples apart. So the vectors 1 and 2 steps ahead share input samples with the current one, and from 3 steps on they share none. The measured result follows that line:

CPC images: only the next row overlaps

For images, CPC cuts a 256 by 256 photo into a 7 by 7 grid of 64 by 64 crops with 32 pixels of overlap, takes a random 60 by 60 piece of each crop, and predicts up to five rows below. My arithmetic from those numbers: crops start 32 pixels apart, so only the next row overlaps the context row, and rows two or more below share no pixels with it. The paper does not say whether this was the reason for predicting several rows ahead, so I claim nothing more.

Crop-based contrastive methods overlap on purpose

SimCLR, MoCo and BYOL (each matches two random crops of one photo to each other) let their crops overlap on purpose; SimCLR's Figure 3 shows global-to-local and adjacent crop pairs. Their shortcut was colour instead: two crops of one photo share its colour distribution, and colour histograms alone are enough to tell photos apart, so SimCLR adds colour distortion. That story is on Contrastive Learning.

Masked image modeling has a neighbour cue with no overlap

In masked image modeling the hidden patches never overlap the visible ones, but if only a few are hidden, a missing patch can be recovered from its neighbours with little high-level understanding (MAE §1). That is why MAE hides 75% of the patches; see Masked Image Modeling.

Collapse squeezes every embedding to one point or a thin slice

Complete collapse puts every embedding on one point. The quieter version is dimensional collapse: the embeddings do not meet at one point, but they spread along only a few directions and leave the rest of the space unused (Jing et al. 2022, using the term from Hua et al. 2021). The loss can look fine while the probe score suffers.

Negatives do not prevent it. Jing et al. trained SimCLR on ImageNet for 100 epochs with 128-number embeddings, and a number of directions of the embeddings carried no spread at all (their Figure 2). They give two causes; the one I find easiest to picture is augmentation that varies a direction more than the real images do, so the loss learns to flatten that direction.

To see this I look at the covariance matrix C of a batch of embeddings, the table of how much every pair of embedding numbers varies together. Read the line below as: C is the average, over all N embeddings, of each embedding's offset from the mean multiplied by itself as a column times a row. Its job is to summarise the shape of the cloud. Its singular values are the amount of spread along each of the cloud's main directions, largest first. It matters because a direction whose singular value is near zero is a direction the encoder has stopped using, and the loss never shows it. In the code below, svdvals gets the same directions straight from the centred embeddings.

How the embeddings spread: the covariance of a batch, whose singular values reveal unused directions.
C = 1N Σi=1N (zi − z̄)(zi − z̄)T
zi is the embedding of image i, z̄ (z bar) is the mean embedding of the batch, and N is the number of embeddings. Σ adds up the term for every i from 1 to N, and the fraction in front turns the sum into an average. The superscript T turns the column into a row, so each term is a square table with one row and one column per embedding number. C is that averaged table (Jing et al. 2022, Eq. 1).

Three numbers from one batch catch collapse. The first four lines are the singular value check; the last three turn it into two single numbers I can plot over training.

import torch, torch.nn.functional as F
Z = F.normalize(embeddings, dim=1)             # [n, 128] embeddings of one batch
s = torch.linalg.svdvals(Z - Z.mean(0))        # spread along each direction, largest first
ratios = (s / s[0]).round(decimals=2)          # collapse: a few large values, the rest near 0
spread = Z.std(dim=0).mean()                   # about 1/sqrt(128) = 0.088 healthy, near 0 collapsed
p = s / s.sum()                                # share of the spread in each direction
eff_rank = torch.exp(torch.special.entr(p).sum())  # 1 = one direction used, 128 = all used

s is the spread along each direction. Squaring s and dividing by N gives the singular values of C, so the same directions go to zero. spread is the check SimSiam uses: the standard deviation of each embedding number, averaged, which sits near 1 over the square root of the embedding size when nothing has collapsed (SimSiam §4.1). eff_rank is the effective rank, the exponential of the entropy of the shares (entropy is large when the spread is shared evenly and zero when one direction holds it all), so it counts the directions in use. As an illustration, not a measured number: if 128 directions behave like about 20, something is wrong.

A loss that drops too fast can mean the model found a shortcut

Two examples. SimSiam (two views, one network, no negatives) without its stop-gradient reaches its lowest possible loss, −1, quickly and ends at 0.1% probe accuracy, which is chance on ImageNet (see Non-Contrastive Self-Supervised Learning). The chromatic aberration case in the first section is the other: the task got easy for a reason that had nothing to do with content.

MoCo (contrastive, with a queue of past embeddings as negatives) adds a third. Without its BatchNorm fix, covered below, the pretext task's training accuracy quickly passes 99.9% while the accuracy of a nearest-neighbour check on validation images drops (MoCo, Appendix A.9).

My rule: a loss that falls far and fast while kNN accuracy (label each validation image by a vote of its nearest training images in embedding space) stays flat is a shortcut sign. The converse needs care: SimSiam's authors note that chance-level accuracy alone does not prove collapse, so I check the spread from the previous section as well.

Batch size, temperature and augmentation strength decide success

In SimCLR the negatives (the other images an anchor is pushed away from) come from the same batch, the set of images processed in one step. The temperature τ divides every similarity before the softmax, so it sets how sharply the loss focuses on the closest negatives. Both settings move the result by several points:

  • Negatives and batch size. A small batch gives SimCLR fewer negatives per step, and it learns less; the gap shrinks with longer training (SimCLR §5.2). The fixes are a queue of past embeddings as negatives (MoCo) or a method with no negatives at all (BYOL, SimSiam; BYOL's tolerance for small batches is on Non-Contrastive Self-Supervised Learning).
  • Temperature. Too low and the softmax becomes a spike, so gradients vanish except on the hardest negatives; too high and every image looks the same. The best value moves with the setup: 0.1 for SimCLR on ImageNet (the chart above), 0.5 for SimCLR on CIFAR-10 trained past 300 epochs (SimCLR Appendix B.9), 0.07 in MoCo, and 0.2 in MoCo v2 with its two-layer head, where it gave 66.2% against 62.9% at 0.07 (ResNet-50, 200 epochs). In my experience it matters more than most architecture choices, so tune it.
  • Augmentation strength. Too weak lets shortcuts through, as in the first section. With random crops as the only augmentation (ResNet-50, ImageNet, 300 epochs, linear probe), BYOL drops from 72.5% to 59.4% and SimCLR from 67.9% to 40.3% (BYOL §5, Figure 3b). Too strong can flatten directions of the embedding, the dimensional collapse above (Jing et al. 2022). The strength trade-off has its own tool on Contrastive Learning.
  • Sensors and spectrograms. Crop, colour jitter and blur are image recipes. For sensor signals or spectrograms (charts of how a signal's energy spreads across frequencies over time) you have to design augmentations that keep the identity of the sample. In my experience this is where most failed self-supervised projects fail.

BatchNorm can leak information between samples in a batch

BatchNorm normalises each layer's outputs with the mean and variance of the current batch, so every sample's output depends a little on the other samples in the batch. When both views of an image sit on the same GPU (one of the graphics processors the batch is split across), that link is a side channel. MoCo found the model can "cheat" the pretext task this way and find a low-loss solution, and fixed it by shuffling the sample order across GPUs before the key encoder's BatchNorm (MoCo §3.3). SimCLR instead computes the BatchNorm mean and variance over all devices together (global BN, SimCLR §2.2); PyTorch's SyncBatchNorm does the same. LayerNorm never mixes samples at all; see The Transformer Block.

A probe score is not the whole story

Fine-tuning updates the encoder's weights on the labelled task, while a linear probe leaves them frozen, and the two can rank methods differently. On MAE, fine-tuning just the last Transformer block (ViT-L) lifts ImageNet accuracy from the probe's 73.5% to 81.0% (details on Masked Image Modeling).

MoCo v2 shows the same split with detection, finding and boxing objects in a photo (ResNet-50, 200 epochs, then a Faster R-CNN detector fine-tuned on PASCAL VOC). Adding blur alone scored 82.2 on the detection measure against 82.0 for adding the two-layer head alone, while its linear probe was lower, 63.4% against 66.2%. The paper's conclusion: "linear classification accuracy is not monotonically related to transfer performance in detection" (MoCo v2, Table 1).

DINO's authors also found that linear and fine-tuning evaluations are sensitive to settings such as the learning rate, with a large spread between runs, so they also report a weighted vote of the 20 nearest neighbours, which needs no tuning (DINO §3.2). Compare methods only under the same protocol; in my view papers tend to report the most flattering one.

Self-supervised learning pays off when unlabeled data far outnumbers labeled data

These methods shine when you have far more unlabeled data than labeled data. My rule of thumb, not a measured threshold: if you only have about 10k images in total, just label them and train supervised.

Settings that usually work

  • Probe the encoder output, not the projection head's output (Contrastive Learning).
  • With a small batch, use a queue or a method with no negatives (batch size).
  • Start the temperature near the values the papers found for a similar setup, then tune (temperature).
  • Compute BatchNorm statistics across all GPUs, or shuffle samples across GPUs (BatchNorm).
  • For masked image modeling, hide a high share of patches at random and use crops as the only augmentation (Masked Image Modeling).
  • For non-image data, design augmentations that keep the sample's identity (augmentation).
  • Compare methods only under the same evaluation protocol (probe scores).

What I watch during training

None of these needs labels for training, and each fails in its own way. The card shows what a healthy and a broken run look like over training.

  • Per-dimension spread of the normalised embeddings: near 1 over the square root of the embedding size when healthy, near 0 when collapsed (SimSiam §4.1). Code: spread in the collapse check above.
  • Effective rank: should not fall toward 1. Code: eff_rank above.
  • Alignment and uniformity (Wang and Isola 2020). Alignment is how close positive pairs sit; uniformity is how evenly all embeddings spread over the sphere; lower is better for both. When every input maps to the same vector, the uniformity loss climbs to its highest value, 0. Across hundreds of trained encoders, the best ones had low values of both.

The two measures in code, following the authors' code (their Figure 6), with the uniformity function folded into one line. The first averages the squared distance between the two embeddings of each positive pair; the second averages a similarity over every pair of embeddings in the batch, so it is high when they bunch together.

def align_loss(x, y, alpha=2):     # x, y: [N, d] normalised positive pairs
    return (x - y).norm(dim=1).pow(alpha).mean()
def uniform_loss(x, t=2):          # x: [N, d] normalised embeddings
    return torch.pdist(x, p=2).pow(2).mul(-t).exp().mean().log()
  • kNN accuracy on a small labelled split during training: label each validation image by a vote of its 20 nearest training images in embedding space. SimSiam watches this curve during training, and DINO evaluates with a weighted vote of the 20 nearest neighbours.
bank = F.normalize(encoder(train_x), dim=1)     # [M, d] stored features
q = F.normalize(encoder(val_x), dim=1)          # [n, d]
idx = (q @ bank.T).topk(20, dim=1).indices      # 20 nearest neighbours (DINO's k)
pred = torch.mode(train_y[idx], dim=1).values   # plain majority vote (DINO weights votes)
acc = (pred == val_y).float().mean()
  • Loss falling far and fast with flat kNN accuracy: the shortcut sign from earlier.

You now have a feature extractor that learned from nothing but structure. To squeeze it into a smaller model, the SSL encoder becomes the teacher and a small student learns to match its vectors on the sphere, which is knowledge distillation.
