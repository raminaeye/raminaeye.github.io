Mixup: Training on Blended Examples

How blending two training examples and their labels by a random share teaches a model to change its answer gradually between examples instead of jumping, and when that helps.

1 What mixup is and the problem it solves

A classifier trained only on its recorded examples is free to behave any way it likes in the space between them. It often jumps from one answer to the other with full confidence, and it can memorize examples whose labels are wrong. Nothing in ordinary training asks for anything better, because no training input ever lies between two classes.

Mixup (Zhang et al. 2018) trains on blends of pairs of examples, with the target blended by the same share. A blend that is mostly a spoken “yes” and partly a “no” gets the target “mostly yes, partly no”. In the paper’s words, this regularizes the network to favour simple linear behaviour in between training examples. The paper frames it as vicinal risk minimization (Chapelle et al. 2000), the same average over nearby inputs as data augmentation, with blends as the neighbours. It is the one augmentation that edits the label, and the label stays true because it is blended in step with the input.

It runs on every training batch, with a fresh share each batch and each example paired with a shuffled partner from the same batch. It keeps no state, costs one permutation and two blends, and is off at evaluation.

2 The paper’s experiments

Zhang et al. tested mixup on image classification (ImageNet and CIFAR), on the Google Commands keyword task with inputs blended at the spectrogram level, and in tests with corrupted labels and adversarial examples. What they found:

  • On ImageNet, small and moderate mixing settings helped, while large ones made the model underfit.
  • On Google Commands, mixup made the small LeNet model worse and helped only the larger VGG-11; the paper notes that higher-capacity models and longer training gain the most.
  • Mixing within one shuffled batch worked as well as drawing pairs from two loaders.
  • Mixing only pairs with the same label gave no gain.
  • With corrupted labels, mixup together with dropout did best.

3 The blend

Take two keyword spectrograms from a batch, a “yes” and a “no”, each with a one-hot label. The card draws a share, blends the inputs, blends the labels by the same share, and scores the prediction.

Draw a share, blend two inputs and their labels by it, score against both.
\(\displaystyle {\htmlClass{eqt sym-reward}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-reward)}{\lambda}} \sim \operatorname{Beta}(\alpha, \alpha)}\)
Draw a mixing share \(\htmlClass{eqt sym-reward}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-reward)}{\lambda}}\) between zero and one. The Beta distribution is a family of shapes on that interval, and \(\alpha\) (alpha) picks the shape: a small \(\alpha\) puts most draws near the ends, so a blend is mostly one example with a little of the other; \(\alpha\) of one makes every share equally likely (the uniform draw); as \(\alpha\) shrinks toward zero, the blends vanish and mixup turns back into ordinary training.
\(\displaystyle {\htmlClass{eqt sym-x}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-x)}{\tilde{x}}} = \htmlClass{eqt sym-reward}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-reward)}{\lambda}}\, \htmlClass{eqt sym-x}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-x)}{x_i}} + \big(1 - \htmlClass{eqt sym-reward}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-reward)}{\lambda}}\big)\, \htmlClass{eqt sym-x}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-x)}{x_j}}}\)
Blend two inputs by that share. \(\htmlClass{eqt sym-x}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-x)}{x_i}}\) is an example from the batch and \(\htmlClass{eqt sym-x}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-x)}{x_j}}\) its shuffled partner; \(\htmlClass{eqt sym-x}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-x)}{\tilde{x}}}\) (x-tilde) is the blend the model sees.
\(\displaystyle {\htmlClass{eqt sym-target}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-target)}{\tilde{y}}} = \htmlClass{eqt sym-reward}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-reward)}{\lambda}}\, \htmlClass{eqt sym-target}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-target)}{y_i}} + \big(1 - \htmlClass{eqt sym-reward}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-reward)}{\lambda}}\big)\, \htmlClass{eqt sym-target}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-target)}{y_j}}}\)
Builds on line 2: blend the one-hot labels by the same share, so the target says “mostly yes, partly no”. The label moves in step with the input, which is what keeps it true.
\(\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}\htmlClass{eqt sym-loss}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-loss)}{\mathcal{L}}} &= \htmlClass{eqt sym-reward}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-reward)}{\lambda}}\, \htmlClass{eqt sym-loss}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-loss)}{\ell}}\big(f_\theta(\htmlClass{eqt sym-x}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-x)}{\tilde{x}}}),\, \htmlClass{eqt sym-target}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-target)}{y_i}}\big) \\ &\quad + (1 - \htmlClass{eqt sym-reward}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-reward)}{\lambda}}) \\ &\qquad \times \htmlClass{eqt sym-loss}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-loss)}{\ell}}\big(f_\theta(\htmlClass{eqt sym-x}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-x)}{\tilde{x}}}),\, \htmlClass{eqt sym-target}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-target)}{y_j}}\big)\end{aligned}}\)
Builds on line 3. Cross-entropy is linear in its target, so scoring the prediction against the blended target splits into these two terms: the loss against each original label, weighted by the share. This holds for plain cross-entropy; with class weights under mean reduction the two differ, so compute the loss from the blended target directly.
So mixup asks the model to answer a blend with a blended answer, which forces it to change its answer gradually along the way from one example to another.

5 Mixup in PyTorch

The one-batch, two-term form used in the authors’ released CIFAR-10 code. The two-term loss matches cross-entropy against the blended target for plain cross-entropy only; where class weights are used, pass the blended target itself, since PyTorch’s CrossEntropyLoss accepts class probabilities as targets.

lam = np.random.beta(alpha, alpha)
perm = torch.randperm(x.size(0))
x_mix = lam * x + (1 - lam) * x[perm]
out = net(x_mix)
loss = lam * loss_fn(out, y) + (1 - lam) * loss_fn(out, y[perm])

  2. 2Blend the inputs, line 2.
  3. 3Score against both labels, line 4, which carries the label blend of line 3.

6 The tool: gradual answers between examples

A tiny keyword spotter trained in your browser to tell “yes” from “no” (illustrative). The curve is its “yes” confidence along the path from a “yes” recording to a “no” recording; the dashed curve is the same model trained without mixup. Drag the mixing shape and watch training reshape the curve.

Figure 2. Illustrative spotter, trained live. Without mixup the answer jumps; with it, the answer ramps.

Near zero the curve is the dashed jump: the model is certain on both sides and flips at one point. As the shape widens, the blends cover the whole path and the curve settles into a ramp, because each blend asked for an answer in proportion to its share. How wide to set the shape is tuned on validation data: on real data the paper found that large settings make the model underfit (Section 2).

7 Variants

  • CutMix pastes a rectangular patch of one image into another and mixes the labels by the patch’s area (Yun et al. 2019). The pasted region stays a real image, so local features remain natural.
  • Manifold Mixup blends hidden representations inside the network instead of the inputs (Verma et al. 2019). Blending deeper features gives smoother decision boundaries at several levels of the network.

8 In practice

  • Where it runs. In the training step or a collate function, on the whole batch, after loading and any per-example augmentation.
  • Choosing the shape. Tune \(\alpha\) on validation data, starting small.
  • Combining. It composes with the per-example edits of Data Augmentation and with dropout.
  • Evaluation. Always on unblended inputs.
  • Warm-up. For speech the paper first trained for five epochs on unmixed examples before mixing, a setting stated in the paper.
  • Rare classes. Blends do not fix a class that is rare; check per-class accuracy (Class Imbalance).
