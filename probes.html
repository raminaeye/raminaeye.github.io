Activation Probes

Tiny classifiers that read a model's internal activations to flag harmful prompts, before it answers.

The problem

Frontier models keep getting more capable, and capability cuts both ways: the same model that helps a security engineer can help an attacker. The standard defense is a monitor, a second system that checks each prompt. The obvious monitor is another large language model asked whether the prompt is an attack. That works, but it roughly doubles the bill, because every user request now costs two model runs.

An activation probe is the cheaper answer. It does not read the prompt's words. It reads the model's own internal understanding of the prompt, which the model already computed during its normal forward pass, and turns it into one suspiciousness score. The extra cost is tiny: one small classifier running on numbers that already exist.

Two terms, glossed once here and used everywhere below. A token is a word-piece, the chunk the model actually reads ("injection" might be one token, "unbelievable" might be three). An activation is a list of numbers inside the model describing what it has understood so far.

A smoke detector, not a locked door

The probe never changes what the model does. The model processes the prompt exactly as it would without the probe. The probe is tapped to the side: it copies numbers out of one middle layer, computes a score, and stops there. If the score clears a threshold, the surrounding system acts instead of letting the model answer. It can block the request, refuse it, or hand the prompt to a bigger model for a second opinion. If the score is low, nothing happens and the model answers normally.

Timing is the point. The probe finishes scoring while the model is still reading the prompt, before any answer exists. Early warning, not a lock on the door.

What "activation probe" means

In plain words: tap a middle layer of the model, check every part of the prompt, keep the most suspicious part, compare it to a threshold. The precise meaning of "part" arrives in two steps below.

Lists of numbers

Each part of the prompt that the model processes is represented as a list of numbers. "Hidden size" is just the length of that list: how many dials the model gets for describing what it understood. In the toy model on this page the hidden size is 8, so every list has 8 numbers and the arithmetic stays visible. In the paper's setting the lists are thousands of numbers long, and the same arithmetic runs over all of them.

Positions, not words

By the middle of the model, those lists are no longer word lists. Every block lets each slice of the prompt look at the other slices and mix their information in, so by block 8 the list sitting where "injection" was has absorbed the whole sentence around it. Call each slice a position: a slot in the prompt whose list of numbers sees the entire prompt, not just its own word.

The probe scores positions, not words. Here is the proof, in the toy numbers below: the word "SQL" appears in both demo prompts, but its list of numbers is different each time, so its score is different too, 2.84 in the attack prompt and 0.75 in the benign one. Same word, different situation, different score. The probe is not detecting the word "SQL." It is detecting the situation the word sits in, as the model understood it. That is the entire reason for tapping the middle of the model instead of scanning the raw text.

The concrete machine

The toy model on this page is a GPT-style transformer: 12 blocks, hidden size 768 (8 of the 768 numbers are shown, so the arithmetic stays visible), 12 attention heads. The probe taps block 8 and copies every position's list of numbers. In the paper, the same setup watches Gemini 2.5 Flash; the toy stands in for it, and you will meet Flash properly in the cascade section below.

The equation

Honest label first: what follows is a simplified single-head teaching version. It scores like the classic linear probe and combines like MultiMax, and it matches no single paper architecture exactly. The paper's real probes add heads, MLPs, and windows on top of this skeleton.

$$s_i = \max_j (w^T x_{i,j}) \qquad p_i = \sigma(s_i + b)$$
Reading the symbols, one per line.
\(w\): the learned direction. A list of 8 numbers in the toy (768 in the real setting), learned from labeled prompts.
\(x_{i,j}\): the list of numbers at position \(j\) of prompt \(i\). For the winning position below, [1.30, −0.90, 1.60, 0.50, −1.40, 1.10, 2.10, −0.60].
\(w^T x_{i,j}\): the dot product, how much that position's list points along the learned direction. For the winning position this is 10.32 (computed in the tool above).
\(\max_j\): keep the largest position score, not the average. This is the combine step the whole paper turns on.
\(b = -1.5\): the bias, a learned offset. 10.32 − 1.50 = 8.82.
\(\sigma\): squash into 0 to 1. \(\sigma(8.82) \approx 1.00\). Above the 0.5 threshold: flagged.

One skeleton, one knob

Every probe in the paper is the same machine with one knob changed: how position scores get combined into one number. The tap, the scoring, and the threshold are shared. Only the combine step differs, because the combine step is what breaks on long documents.

  • Linear probe (the classic): average all position scores. Simple, but one bad sentence drowns in a million innocent words.
  • EMA probe: smooth the scores as it reads with an exponential moving average, then take the peak.
  • MLP probe: score each position with a small neural net first, then average. Smarter scoring, same dilution problem.
  • Attention probe: learned weighted average, where the weights come from the positions themselves. Better, but the weights still spread thin over huge contexts.
  • MultiMax (new in the paper): drop the averaging. Each head takes its single most suspicious position. A short attack cannot hide in a long document.
  • Max of Rolling Means (new, the paper's selected probe): score every 10-position window with attention, then take the worst window.
  • AlphaEvolve probes (new, machine-found): an AI system searched thousands of combine recipes and kept the winners. It rediscovered max-style combining on its own.

Where the weights come from

The weights are learned from labeled prompts. The attack side is cyber attacks curated by security experts, plus multi-turn conversations that start innocent and escalate. The benign side is real user traffic with personal data removed, plus hard negatives: prompts that sound dangerous but are not, like asking how a famous breach worked or asking for a code security review. The hard negatives teach the probe the difference between talking about attacks and asking for help with one.

The training loss is binary cross-entropy, in plain words: for each training prompt, nudge the weights so attacks score high and benign prompts score low. A confident correct answer gets a small nudge; a confident wrong answer gets a large one.

Binary cross-entropy, one line at a time.
\(L = -[l \log p + (1 - l) \log(1 - p)]\), where \(l\) is the true label (1 for attack, 0 for benign) and \(p\) is the probe's score.
Attack prompt (\(l = 1\)) scored at \(p = 0.9\): \(L = -\log(0.9) \approx 0.11\). Small loss, small nudge.
Attack prompt (\(l = 1\)) scored at \(p = 0.2\): \(L = -\log(0.2) \approx 1.61\). Large loss, large nudge.
The optimizer is AdamW, run for 1000 epochs over the precomputed activations.

One deliberate choice shapes everything that follows: the probes are trained only on short prompts. Training directly on long-context data costs about 22 times more, because million-token activation sets do not fit on accelerator memory and have to be shuttled back and forth. That 22x is why the whole paper is about generalizing to long contexts instead of just training on them. Long contexts are reserved as a pure test the probe never studied for.

The decision threshold is picked on a validation set with false alarms weighted heaviest: overtriggering on benign traffic counts 50 against 5 for a missed attack and 2 for a hard-negative false alarm.

The cascade

The probe is cheap and the big model is not, so the paper pairs them. The probe scores every prompt. Clear cases are decided by the probe alone. The unsure middle, scores too close to call, goes to Gemini 2.5 Flash for a second opinion.

What this showsThe cascade rule: below t0 the probe decides benign, above t1 it decides attack, and the band between them, about 8% of prompts, is deferred to Gemini 2.5 Flash.

The numbers that matter: only about 8% of prompts land in the unsure band and get deferred, and the combined system scores lower error than Flash alone at roughly 1/50th the inference cost. The probe does the bulk work for almost free; the expensive model is spent only where the cheap one is uncertain. That 1/50th is the economic reason probes are worth building at all.

Three more findings round it out. No single probe wins on every distribution shift: the best architecture for long contexts is not the best for multi-turn, so production means choosing per deployment. The automatic search worked: starting from a plain attention probe, AlphaEvolve evaluated about 2,500 architectures and rediscovered max-style combining on its own. And the findings informed real deployment: misuse-mitigation probes built on this work now run in user-facing Gemini.

Caveats

  • Adaptive attackers still get through. Every method tested leaves at least 1% attack success on the jailbreak set, and the authors note some of that may be dataset labeling error. A probe is a speed bump for a determined adversary, not a wall.
  • Inputs only. Everything in the paper watches the prompt going in. Watching the model's answer is listed as future work.
  • Architecture was not enough alone. The new designs fixed the context-length problem, but broad generalization still required training on diverse distributions too.
