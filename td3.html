Twin Delayed DDPG (TD3)

A robot-arm learner that scores its turning forces with two networks, trusts the lower score, and updates the network that sets them less often.

1 Why the scoring network rates moves too high

Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG) trains one network to output a robot arm's turning forces and a second to score them, and the scorer often rates some of them too high, so the first network chases those errors. On a real arm, a torque picked from a score that is too high is a wasted or harmful move.

Twin Delayed DDPG (TD3) is DDPG with three changes that curb this. Each change tackles one way those errors grow.

The agent is the learner that picks the moves. (RL Basics) A state is the agent's situation right now. A move, or action, is one choice the agent can make in a state. A reward is the number the world hands back after each move, good or bad. (RL Basics) On a robot, sensing, moving and scoring repeat many times every second.

The running example is the Reacher task: a robot arm with two joints whose actions are four torques. A torque is a turning force a motor applies at a joint. On the Reacher each torque is a number from -1 to 1. Each joint can turn about two axes, so each joint gets two torques. Every step the arm senses its state, numbers describing the position, rotation and speed of its parts, and earns a small reward whenever its hand is inside a moving goal. (Continuous-Action RL) So the hand must follow the goal as it moves, not just reach it once.

The return is all the reward still to come, with later rewards counted a little less. (RL Basics) A move's value \(Q(s,a)\), also called its score, is the return the agent expects if it makes move \(a\) in state \(s\) and plays on from there. (RL Basics) DDPG, which TD3 changes, learns two networks.

The deterministic actor \(\mu_\theta\) is a network with weights \(\theta\) that outputs one action every time, with no random draw. The critic \(Q_\phi\) is a network with weights \(\phi\) that scores a state and an action together; its score stands for the return the agent expects. (Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG): Actor and Critic) On the Reacher nothing stops the critic scoring some of the arm's torques too high.

1.1 The actor climbs into the critic's mistakes

The DDPG actor update moves the actor's weights so its torques shift the way the critic's score rises. (Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG): Actor and Critic) That works only as well as the critic's scores. Value methods take a max, the highest of the scores their network gives the possible next moves, when they learn.

Overestimation means the values climb above the return the agent really earns, because a max over noisy guesses picks lucky ones. (Double DQN) In DDPG the actor plays the role of the max, since its actor update pushes the torques toward wherever the critic is highest, including where it is wrong. The TD3 paper measured this on simulated robots and found the critic's estimates well above the true values.

1.2 Why the value methods' fix is not enough

The critic learns by being pulled toward a target, the step's reward plus its own score for what comes next, counted a little less. (Continuous-Action RL) That target moves whenever the critic moves. The Deep Q-Network (DQN) scores its moves with one network, its Q-network. DQN had the same problem of a target that moves, and fixed it with a second network. On the arm, a target that moves would let the critic's scores chase their own changes.

The target network is a slow copy of the Q-network, with weights \(\phi'\), that computes the targets. (Deep Q-Networks (DQN)) Slow means the copy, written with a prime on its weights, is updated only a little at a time. In this family the critic plays the Q-network's part. (Training DDPG: Target Networks, Replay and Noise) This holds the target still while the critic learns.

In DDPG both the actor and the critic have a slow copy, marked by a prime on the weights, and the targets are built from those copies. (Training DDPG: Target Networks, Replay and Noise) The live networks are the actor and critic being trained, with weights \(\theta\) and \(\phi\). (Training DDPG: Target Networks, Replay and Noise) This holds the target still while the critic learns. TD3 keeps these slow copies just as DDPG does.

For value methods, overestimation already has a known fix. Double DQN is a fix for DQN that uses two networks inside the target; it has only one live network, the Q-network. Double DQN's target lets the live network pick the next move and the target network score it. (Double DQN) TD3 borrows this check of one network's score against another's, so one lucky high score gets caught.

In DDPG the same trick would let the live actor pick the next torques and a slow critic score them, but the slow copies stay close to the live networks, so pick and score come from nearly the same opinion, and the score stays too high.

1.3 Errors pile up through the target

Bootstrapping means putting your own current guess for the next state inside the target; each target is built from the critic's own next score, so an error at one step is carried into the next. (Actor-Critic: A2C and A3C)

That chain is why a single fix is not enough. A lower score alone would still feed the next target, and a slower actor alone would still climb an inflated critic. TD3 cuts the chain in three places at once.

2 Twin critics, a smoothed target and delayed actor updates

TD3 makes three changes to DDPG's update, one for each way the errors grow:

  1. A policy is the agent's rule for choosing a move in each state; it may always pick the same move or pick by chance. (RL Basics)
  2. Here the policy is the actor.
  3. The first change adds a small random nudge, kept small by a cap, to the next torques inside the target.
  4. Target policy smoothing adds a small clipped random nudge to the next torques inside the target, so one narrow spike in the critic cannot be exploited.
  5. The nudge sits only inside the target, not in the torques the arm plays, so the critic learns that torques close together score about the same and the actor has no lone spike to climb into.
  6. TD3 trains two critics side by side and builds the target from the lower of their two scores.
  7. The paper calls this clipped double Q; clipped there means capped by the lower of the two scores, unlike the nudge's clip, which holds it within a limit.
  8. Both critics learn from the same batches and the same target.
  9. TD3's delayed update moves the actor and the slow copies only once every \(d\) critic updates, so the actor climbs a critic that has settled.
  10. The lower score is the safer side, because a torque the critic underrates is simply avoided by the actor, so that error is not chased and carried forward.

3 TD3's update, rule by rule

3.1 Smoothing the next torques

Replay keeps a memory of past steps, and training draws random batches from it. (Deep Q-Networks (DQN)) The discount \(\gamma\) sets how much a reward one step later still counts. (RL Basics) The DDPG critic's target is the step's reward plus the discounted score the slow critic gives to the slow actor's next torques. (Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG): Actor and Critic)

TD3 changes this target twice: it nudges the next torques and keeps the lower of two scores.

Equation 1. Smoothing the next torques. The slow actor proposes next torques for an arm; each torque gets a small random nudge, so the target scores a little neighbourhood around the pick, not one exact point.
\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{n} = \operatorname{clip}(\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{\tilde\sigma}\,\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{\xi},\ \htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{-c},\ \htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{c})\)
\(\xi\) is a fresh random number drawn from a standard normal bell, one per torque. (Training DDPG: Target Networks, Replay and Noise) Standard means the bell is centred at zero and its width is set to one. \(\tilde\sigma\) is the nudge's size. Clip keeps the nudge \(n\) within plus or minus \(c\), the clip limit.
\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\tilde a} = \htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\mu_{\theta'}}(\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s'}) + \htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{n}\)
\(\mu_{\theta'}(s')\) is the slow actor's next torques; the prime marks the slow copy. Adding the nudge means similar torques score about the same, so scoring a little neighbourhood flattens narrow spikes in the critic that the actor could climb into.
\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\tilde a} \leftarrow \operatorname{clip}(\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\tilde a},\ \htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{-1},\ \htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{1})\)
The clip keeps each torque inside -1 to 1, as the TD3 paper's supplementary material does, so the target never scores an impossible torque. This builds on the DDPG critic's target, which uses the slow actor. It is not exploration noise: this nudge sits inside the target, not in the torques the arm plays. (Training DDPG: Target Networks, Replay and Noise) \(\tilde a\) is what the next equation scores.

3.2 The lower of two critics

The nudged torques from Equation 1 go to both slow critics.

Equation 2. The lower of two slow critics. Two slow critics each score an arm's nudged next torques; if one is optimistic about torques that bring the hand near the goal, the lower score wins.
\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{y} = \htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{r} + \htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{\gamma}\,\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{\min_{i=1,2}}\ \htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{Q_{\phi'_i}}(\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s'},\ \htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\tilde a})\)
\(Q_{\phi'_1}\) and \(Q_{\phi'_2}\) are the two slow critics; the prime marks a slow copy. \(\min\) takes the lower of the two scores. \(\tilde a\) is the smoothed next torques from Equation 1. \(r\) is the reward stored with this step; \(\gamma\) and \(s'\) are as in the DDPG critic's target. (Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG): Actor and Critic) This is the DDPG target with one slow critic replaced by the lower of two.

The TD3 paper writes \(\phi\) for the actor, \(\theta\) for the critics, \(\pi_\phi\) for the actor and \(\epsilon\) for the target nudge; we keep the series letters.

3.3 Training both critics

Each live critic then shrinks its own gap to that shared target.

Equation 3. Training both critics. Both live critics are pulled toward the one shared target for the same stored arm step.
\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{L(\phi_i)} =\)
\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{\mathbb{E}}\big[(\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{y} - \htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{Q_{\phi_i}(s,a)})^2\big]\)
\(i\) is 1 or 2, one loss per critic. The sign \(\mathbb{E}\) marks an expected value, the average over the batch. (Probability and Bayes Rule) \(Q_{\phi_i}(s,a)\) is live critic \(i\); \(y\) is from Equation 2. Sharing \(y\) trains both critics toward the same number. This is the DDPG critic's loss run twice, with one target.

3.4 The delayed actor and slow copies

Until that update comes, the actor keeps its old weights.

Equation 4. The delayed actor and slow copies. The critics update on every Reacher batch; the actor and the slow copies move only every d-th update.
\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{g_1} = \htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{\nabla_a}\ \htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{Q_{\phi_1}(s,a)}\)
\(g_1\) is critic 1's uphill direction for the torques, taken at the torques the actor picks.
\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{\nabla_\theta}\ \htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{J} \approx \htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{\mathbb{E}}\big[\,\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{g_1}\,\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{\nabla_\theta}\ \htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\mu_\theta(s)}\big]\)
The sign \(\approx\) means approximately. This is the DDPG actor update using critic 1 only. \(J\) is the actor's expected return, the quantity the actor's weights are moved to raise. (Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG): Actor and Critic) \(\nabla_\theta \mu_\theta(s)\) is how the actor's torques move when its weights move. The paper uses critic 1 alone for the actor; the lower score is needed only inside the target.
\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{\phi'_i} \leftarrow \htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{\tau}\,\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{\phi_i} + (1-\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{\tau})\,\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{\phi'_i}\)
This blends both slow critics toward the live critics. The soft update blends a small share \(\tau\) of the live weights into each slow copy after every update, instead of overwriting it. (Deep Q-Networks (DQN)) Here it runs every \(d\)-th update. The arrow \(\leftarrow\) means is replaced by.
\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\theta'} \leftarrow \htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{\tau}\,\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\theta} + (1-\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{\tau})\,\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\theta'}\)
This blends the slow actor toward the live actor, on the same slower clock. The actor climbs a critic whose target came from Equations 1 to 3.

5 TD3's update in PyTorch

The four equations above become one update function below. An episode is one run from the start until it ends. (RL Basics) Each episode is also cut off after a fixed number of steps; that cut is not a real end, so the future value is kept.

def td3_update(s, a, r, s2, done, step):
    # smooth the next torques
    noise = (torch.randn_like(a) * target_noise).clamp(-c, c)
    a2 = (actor_t(s2) + noise).clamp(-1, 1)
    # the target from the lower slow score
    with torch.no_grad():
        y = r + gamma * (1 - done) * torch.min(q1_t(s2, a2), q2_t(s2, a2))
    # both critics toward the shared target
    loss = mse(q1(s, a), y) + mse(q2(s, a), y)
    loss.backward()
    critic_opt.step()
    # the actor and the slow copies, every d-th update
    if step % d == 0:
        actor_loss = -q1(s, actor(s)).mean()
        actor_loss.backward()
        actor_opt.step()
        soft_update(actor_t, actor, tau)
        soft_update(q1_t, q1, tau)
        soft_update(q2_t, q2, tau)
  1. 1First, the smoothing of Equation 1: target_noise is the nudge's size and c is the clip limit. The outer clamp keeps each torque in range, as the TD3 paper's supplementary material does.
  2. 2Next, the target of Equation 2: under torch.no_grad() the target is a fixed number, not trained. done is set when the episode truly ended, not when the time limit cut it, so nothing follows.
  3. 3Then, the loss of Equation 3: both live critics move toward the one shared target. mse is the mean squared gap, as Equation 3 shows.
  4. 4Last, the actor and slow copies of Equation 4: every d-th update only, the actor loss is minus the critic's score of the actor's torques, so the code minimises minus the score, and the slow copies blend toward the live networks.

7 How TD3 is trained and how it compares

7.1 One critic update per step, actor updates less often

Every step the arm takes stores one step and runs one critic update; the actor and the slow copies update every \(d\) critic updates. Exploration noise is a small random nudge added to the actor's actions while it learns, so the agent tries moves it is not sure about. (Training DDPG: Target Networks, Replay and Noise) While the arm acts, TD3 draws this noise fresh each step.

Evaluation means testing the trained actor with exploration switched off, so the tests are noise-free. (Training DDPG: Target Networks, Replay and Noise)

Before learning starts, the first stretch of steps uses random actions, a warm-up whose length is in the settings table. The paper found noise drawn fresh each step worked as well as DDPG's Ornstein-Uhlenbeck noise, a nudge with memory taught on Training DDPG. So TD3 keeps the simpler noise.

The authors' code and Stable-Baselines3, a widely used reinforcement learning code library, both run this update. Both run the same TD3 update, with two critics and a delayed actor.

7.3 TD3 against other methods on simulated robot tasks

A seed is one full training run started from its own random start. Each method was trained on every task from several seeds.

Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), another way to train an actor that makes small, careful updates and learns only from fresh steps, is one of the methods compared. TD3 reuses each stored step many times from its replay memory. Soft Actor-Critic (SAC), the method of the next page, is compared too.

The comparison runs seven simulated robot tasks in MuJoCo, a physics simulator used for robot tasks, through Gym, a standard collection of reinforcement learning tasks. Each task is a simulated body learning to move or balance: a running half cheetah, a one-legged hopper, a two-legged walker, a four-legged ant, a small two-joint arm reaching a goal point (Reacher), and a pole or double pole balanced on a cart. The TD3 paper's Reacher is a smaller simulated arm from Gym, not this one.

TD3 scored highest on most tasks. It tied at the maximum on InvertedPendulum and sat just below DDPG on InvertedDoublePendulum. DDPG's critic estimate climbed above the true value, and the TD3 paper reports that taking the lower of two critics greatly reduces this overestimation (Figure 2).

8 When TD3 fits

TD3 fits when the moves are continuous, old steps are worth reusing, and a deterministic actor is wanted, since one actor that writes its torques straight out is cheap to run at deployment. It is a near drop-in fix when DDPG's critic scores too high or training is unstable: the same networks and the same replay memory, with the three changes above.

When exploration is hard, or the settings must work across tasks without tuning, Soft Actor-Critic, the next page, is the better pick.
