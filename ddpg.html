Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG)

DQN picks buttons. DDPG turns dials.

Quick recap from the DQN page: the agent scores every move and picks the best one. That works beautifully when the moves are a short list. Forward, back, left, right. Pick the max. Done.

But here is the question that breaks it: how hard do you twist the shoulder joint? Not which button. How much. A torque is any number between -1 and 1, and there are infinitely many of them. You cannot argmax over infinity. I hit exactly this wall in my RF-DDPG repo, where a double-jointed arm has to hold its hand on a target in a Unity ML-Agents task. So this page is about the fix: an actor that outputs the move directly, and a critic that judges it.

The move DQN cannot make

DQN's whole trick is one line: pick the action with the biggest Q value, $\arg\max_a Q(s, a)$. With four buttons that is trivial. With a 4-dimensional torque vector where each entry is any real number in [-1, 1], the argmax is over an infinite set. There is no biggest one to pick. The trick does not scale to dials.

DDPG splits the job in two. The actor is a policy network: it looks at the state and emits the torques directly, $a = \mu(s)$. No argmax anywhere. The critic is a Q-network: it scores state-action pairs and teaches the actor through the deterministic policy gradient. The actor proposes, the critic judges, and the actor listens.

The critic learns from the usual Bellman backup. Term by term:

$$L = (r + \gamma Q'(s', \mu'(s')) - Q(s,a))^2$$
  1. $r$ is the reward you just got: +0.1 if the hand is on the target, 0 otherwise. That is the repo-real reward from the Reacher task.
  2. $\gamma$ is the discount, 0.99 in my repo. Future rewards count, just slightly less than present ones.
  3. $Q'(s', \mu'(s'))$ is the target networks' guess of future value: the target actor $\mu'$ picks next-step torques, the target critic $Q'$ scores them.
  4. $Q(s,a)$ is what the critic currently believes about the move you just made.
  5. Square the difference and minimize. The actor then climbs the critic's gradient: nudge the torques a little in whichever direction the critic likes more.

Two gotchas before you touch the toys. First, a deterministic actor never tries anything new on its own, so during training I add Ornstein-Uhlenbeck noise to the torques. Without it the arm repeats its first guess forever and learns nothing. Second, the target networks $Q'$ and $\mu'$ are slow copies blended in with $\tau = 0.001$. If the critic chased its own live predictions the training would oscillate; the slow targets are the stabilizer.

Caveats

  • Deterministic policies are brittle without noise. Ornstein-Uhlenbeck noise during training, none at test time. Skip the noise and the arm learns exactly one pose: its first guess.
  • The target networks are load-bearing. With $\tau = 0.001$ the targets creep; hard-copy them every step and the critic chases its own tail.
  • Continuous is not always better. On a discrete problem DQN is simpler, faster, and often wins. Match the algorithm to the action space, not to the hype.
  • Twenty parallel arms do a lot of heavy lifting. One arm alone takes far longer to solve this. Parallelism is a compute cheat code, and this repo uses it.
