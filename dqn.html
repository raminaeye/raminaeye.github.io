Deep Q-Networks (DQN)

How an agent learns to want the yellow bananas.

How do you teach a computer to play a game when you are not allowed to tell it the rules? You cannot hand it a strategy guide. All you can do is let it flail around, keep score, and hope it notices what worked. That sounds stupid. It works embarrassingly well.

A while back I trained an agent to collect bananas in a Unity ML-Agents arena. My Value-based-methods repo has the whole thing. The rules are dead simple: the agent looks at the world, picks one of four moves (forward, back, left, right), and gets +1 for a yellow banana and -1 for a blue one. Yellow good, blue bad. That is the entire game, and the agent starts out knowing none of it.

The scoreboard idea

The trick is a scoreboard. For every situation the agent has ever been in, it keeps four numbers: how good did moving forward turn out, how good was back, left, right. That number is called a Q-value, Q for quality. The whole game plan is then one line: always pick the move with the biggest number.

But where do the numbers come from? From experience, one nudge at a time. Every step, the agent looks at what just happened and corrects its guess:

$$Q(s, a) \leftarrow Q(s, a) + \alpha \big[r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s', a') - Q(s, a)\big]$$
  1. $Q(s, a)$: the current guess for "how good is move $a$ in situation $s$". Starts at zero, total ignorance.
  2. $r$: the reward that just arrived. +1, -1, or 0. The only ground truth the agent ever gets.
  3. $\gamma \max_{a'} Q(s', a')$: the best the agent thinks it can do from the NEXT situation, discounted by $\gamma = 0.99$. The future counts, just a hair less than right now.
  4. The bracket is the surprise: reality minus expectation. $\alpha$, the learning rate, decides how much of the surprise to believe.

That is it. That is the whole algorithm. Nudge every number toward reality, forever, and a strategy falls out the other end.

The entire brain, minus the neural net, fits in six lines:

# epsilon-greedy: mostly smart, sometimes random
if random.random() < epsilon:
    action = random.choice([0, 1, 2, 3])
else:
    action = argmax(Q[state])

# the Q-update: one nudge toward reality
Q[s][a] += lr * (reward + gamma * max(Q[s_next]) - Q[s][a])

Playing dumb on purpose

There is a catch, and it is the cleverest part. If the agent always picks its best-known move, it never tries anything new, and the scoreboard freezes on whatever luck it stumbled into first. So it plays dumb on purpose. Most of the time it picks the best move it knows. But with probability epsilon, it picks a completely random move, just to see what happens.

Epsilon starts at 1.0, total chaos, and decays toward 0.01 as the agent gets confident. Early on it needs the chaos to discover anything. Late in training the chaos would just be vandalism. Hence the classic beginner mistake: setting epsilon to zero from the start. Your agent will confidently march into the first blue banana it finds and never learn any better, because it never looks anywhere else.

How to play
  • The point: Q-learning is a scoreboard that fills itself in. Watch blank cells heat up orange near yellow bananas and cold blue near blue ones, while the agent's behavior flips from chaos to purpose.
  • What you'll see: 200 episodes in 10 stages, 2.2 seconds per stage. Each cell's color is its best Q-value, the score chart climbs as the agent figures it out, and the red dot slides down the epsilon decay curve.
  • Step 1 episode: try stepping a few episodes at the start and watch the agent eat a blue banana while epsilon reads 1.00, then let a full run finish and watch it dodge blue on purpose near epsilon 0.01.
  • Try this: hit Reset mid-run and watch the table go blank. The learning starts over from total ignorance, and the score chart tells the same story a second time.

The honest footnote

One footnote so nobody can accuse me of oversimplifying: the network in my repo is actually a Dueling Double DQN. The Q-network splits into two streams, one guessing how good the situation is and one guessing how much better each move is than average, then adds them back together. Fancy plumbing, same idea: learn how good each move is, pick the biggest number.
