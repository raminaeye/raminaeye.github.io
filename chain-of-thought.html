Chain of Thought: Thinking Before Answering

Showing the steps before the answer lets the model work through a problem instead of guessing.

1 Guessing the answer against working through it

Ask a language model a question that needs two steps and it answers in one breath. Sometimes that works. Often the model guesses, because it has to do all the reasoning inside one leap from question to answer, with nowhere to put the middle.

Chain of thought gives the model somewhere to put the middle. The model writes the steps out before it answers, one sentence at a time, and each sentence can use the ones before it. The answer comes last, built on the working.

It runs at inference, once per question. Nothing is trained and nothing is carried between questions. The prompt goes in, the model writes the chain and the answer, and the next question starts fresh.

Here is the paper’s own illustration, a question about giving flowers away. Jane has 12 flowers. She gives 2 flowers to her mom and 3 flowers to her dad. How many flowers does Jane have now?

The two sides differ in one place only: the chained side writes the steps first. That is the whole method, and the rest of the page is what that change buys and where it breaks.

2 The prompt triple

A prompt is the text the model is given before it writes. Few-shot prompting means the prompt holds a few worked examples of the task before the new question, so the model copies the shape of the examples. An exemplar is one of those worked examples.

Chain of thought prompting changes what the examples contain. Each example is a triple: the input, the chain of thought, and the output, in that order.

The prompt triple. Each example in the prompt has three parts, in this order.
\(\displaystyle \langle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{x}}, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{c}}, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{y}} \rangle\)
Read it as a sentence: the prompt shows the model an input, the chain of thought that leads from it, and the output it ends at. \(x\) is the input, the question as asked. \(c\) is the chain of thought, a series of short sentences that work through the question one step at a time. \(y\) is the output, the final answer, written after the chain. The order is the method: the chain comes before the answer, never after it, so the answer can build on the steps. Standard few-shot prompting shows pairs with no middle, \(\langle x, y \rangle\); chain of thought prompting inserts \(c\) between them.
\(x\) the input, the question \(c\) the chain of thought, the steps \(y\) the output, the final answer

The model is never told to reason. It copies the shape it was shown: question, steps, answer. The new question ends open, and the model writes a chain in the same shape before it answers.

One prompt, built from a worked example and a new question, then passed to the model to continue.

from transformers import AutoModelForCausalLM, AutoTokenizer

tok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(MODEL_NAME)
model = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained(MODEL_NAME)
prompt = """Q: Roger has 5 tennis balls. He buys 2 more cans of tennis balls. Each can has 3 tennis balls. How many tennis balls does he have now?
A: Roger started with 5 balls. 2 cans of 3 tennis balls each is 6 tennis balls. 5 + 6 = 11. The answer is 11.

Q: Jane has 12 flowers. She gives 2 flowers to her mom and 3 flowers to her dad. How many flowers does Jane have now?
A:"""
ids = tok(prompt, return_tensors="pt").input_ids
out = model.generate(ids, max_new_tokens=LIMIT)
print(tok.decode(out[0], skip_special_tokens=True))

  2. 2The new question ends open, so the model writes the chain itself.
  3. 3Generate continues the text token by token, as in Decoding. The capital names are placeholders.

3 What the chain does for the model

The chain is ordinary text, written one token at a time like any other reply, and each token can look back at the steps already written. That is why the trick works through the same machinery Decoding teaches, with the next token drawn as in Sampling the Next Token. What changes is only what the model writes before the answer, and that buys four things.

First, it splits a hard problem into steps. A question that needs two gifts given away becomes two short counts, one after the other, and a problem that needs more steps gets more writing to work with.

Second, it leaves a window into the answer. The steps show how the model might have reached its answer, so when the answer is wrong the faulty step can be found instead of guessed at.

Third, it travels. The same shape works for math word problems, commonsense questions, and symbol puzzles, and in principle for any task a person can solve by talking through it.

Fourth, it needs no training. A ready-made large model picks it up just from chain examples in the prompt, with its weights untouched.

Notice what each sentence does. The first fixes the count after one gift. The second updates it after the next. The third turns the last count into the answer. No sentence does the whole problem, and no sentence repeats one before it.

The toggle shows the whole method in one move. The examples set the shape and the model copies it: answers only in, answers only out; steps in, steps out.

4 Practice

Chain of thought costs nothing beyond the longer generation. The model is prompted, not retrained, so there is no training run, no new weights, and no extra data to collect. Each question costs more tokens out, because the chain is written before the answer.

The headline setting is few-shot prompting with chain-of-thought exemplars, from section 2 of the paper. The paper’s arithmetic runs used eight chain-of-thought exemplars (four for the one multiple-choice benchmark) and greedy decoding, so the model always wrote the most likely next token.

The paper’s own repository holds the exact prompts and predictions behind the results, at github.com/jasonwei20/chain-of-thought-prompting. It holds prompts and results, not training code, because the method needs none: to try it, put a worked example with steps in the prompt and let the model continue the shape.

5 Where chains fail

A chain is only as good as the model writing it. Three failures cover most of what goes wrong.

5.1 The model is too small

Chains only help large models. Below roughly a hundred billion parameters the model writes chains that read smoothly but do not follow, and the score lands below plain direct answering. The symptom is a chain that looks fine at a glance while the logic slips between sentences. The fix is a larger model, or no chain at all: ask a small model for the answer directly.

5.2 One wrong step early

Each sentence builds on the last, so an early slip carries through to the answer. The symptom is a chain that reads carefully while the answer is wrong, with the slip sitting near the top. The fix is to read the steps, not just the answer, and to split the question into smaller steps when the chain goes wrong.

5.3 Right answer, wrong working

The chain is text the model wrote, not a record of how it computed, and the paper found correct answers whose chains were wrong. The symptom is an answer that checks out with a step in the middle that does not. The fix is to verify the steps on their own: a right answer never proves the reasoning was right.

6 What comes next

A chain works through what the model already knows. But what happens when the thinking needs a fact the model does not have, like the date of an event or the population of a city? Guessing from memory is the wrong move, and writing more steps does not fix a missing fact. ReAct: Thinking and Acting in One Loop puts the chain inside a loop with a tool, so the model can look the fact up instead of guessing it.
