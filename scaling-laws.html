Scaling Laws

The exchange rate between compute and loss.

The model trains. Now the question is money. Scaling laws describe how test loss falls as you spend more on data, parameters, or compute. Where does the next dollar go? That is what this page prices.

You want to know if you should invest in more data collection, a bigger model, or if the bottleneck is the optimization error. Scaling laws help you decide where the marginal gain is coming from.

The pattern is a power law, found empirically across many domains. In words: the loss falls toward a floor that no training run ever goes under, and the small exponent is the brutal exchange rate between compute and loss.

Here is the most expensive straight line in machine learning. Every point on it is a training run someone paid for. The loss falls slowly. Painfully slowly. That is what an exponent of 0.05 means: you can 10x your compute and buy two hundredths of a loss point. On linear axes the line bends into a hockey stick. That is why everyone plots this in log-log. The straightness is not the phenomenon. The straightness is the paper you wrap the phenomenon in.

In words: the loss bottoms out at a floor of 1.5 that no budget can beat, 2.0 is the starting size of the gap, and the small 0.05 exponent is the brutal exchange rate between money and loss: each doubling of compute buys only a sliver off what remains.

$$L = 1.5 + 2.0 \cdot C^{-0.05}$$

Illustrative law: loss equals 1.5 plus 2.0 times compute to the negative 0.05, a Kaplan-style compute exponent.

But wait, how do you estimate the floor? You can train your model on multiple dataset sizes and record the test loss. Then plot the gap, loss minus your floor guess, vs. the data size in a log-log space. You adjust the floor guess until the curve is a straight line, then fit the slope, which will be the negative scaling exponent.

How the law is used once fitted: pick a budget, read the predicted loss off the curve, and compare the gain from the next 10x. Each 10x of compute shaves the gap by the same factor, so the absolute gains shrink as you spend more. Doubling barely dents it. A million-x halves it.

Find the floor by straightening the curve

If adding more data improves the test loss, you haven’t hit the floor yet. If the curve starts to flatten, you’re approaching the floor. If you guess wrong (too small), it will flatten out prematurely. If you guess too large, it may go negative at large data sizes or bend the other way. Subtracting the floor is what straightens the curve, because only the gap follows the power law: guess the floor too low and the leftover floor bends the right end up, guess it too high and the math breaks where the gap goes negative.

$$L = 1.5 + \frac{2}{\sqrt{D}}$$

Five illustrative runs generated from a simple curve: loss equals 1.5 plus 2 over the square root of the data size. Here 1.5 is the floor, D is the data size, the horizontal axis of the fitting procedure above, and the square root form is why the data exponent here is 0.5, as the later section states. This is the data form of the same floor plus gap shape as the compute law: you can apply the same equation to model size (number of parameters) or compute.

The straightness measure, defined: the meter's |r| is the correlation between the plotted points and a straight line: 1 means the points sit exactly on a line, and it falls as they bend away.

For fixed compute, grow params and data together

Once you have fit the line correctly, you know three things: the irreducible error, the slope in log-log space (which tells you how fast the error falls when you add more data, parameters, or compute), and a constant factor. If the irreducible error is high, that means your modality has a limit. If the scaling exponent is large (like 0.5), then doubling the data would give you a noticeable gain, and if it’s small, not so much. You can also predict performance as you scale the data.

The gap above the floor, your loss minus the irreducible error, falls as a power law in the data size with the scaling exponent as its slope.

One warning before you compare exponents across this page. The 0.5 above is a data-scaling exponent (the exponent is often written α, "alpha"). Compute exponents are much smaller, around 0.05, which is why doubling your data can matter and doubling your compute barely registers. Do not compare alphas across resources. The visceral pair: with an exponent of 0.5, doubling shaves a real chunk off the gap. With 0.05, doubling barely dents it. Same math, very different wallets.

You can apply the same equation to model size (number of parameters) or compute. So when you plot loss vs. dataset size on a log-log plot, you often get a straight line. That line’s slope tells you how much benefit you would get from scaling the data. For example, with model size, it will tell you if you need a deeper model. And if you’re building a model for a wearable where there is a compute limit, then you might get a sense of how much you might need, because if the scaling ratio is too large and you need a bigger model, it will tell you what you’re capped at.

There are also joint scaling laws that add a params term alongside the data term. This lets you trade off between being data-limited and model-limited: hold one fixed and vary the other, and the pattern to watch is when the data term dominates the params term, you are data-limited, and doubling the model buys you almost nothing until you feed it.

For years the recipe was simple: fix the data, grow the model. Then Hoffmann et al. (2022) trained Chinchilla and called that recipe wasteful. Their finding: for a fixed compute budget, model size and training tokens should scale together, roughly tens of tokens per parameter. (The famous run was 70B params on 1.4T tokens.)

To make that tradeoff concrete you need FLOP accounting. A common rule of thumb: compute is about six times the parameter count times the token count: about 2 FLOPs per parameter per token on the forward pass and about 4 on the backward pass, six in total. Compute is your money. The split slider in For fixed compute, grow params and data together is how you spend it.

$$L = 1.69 + 406.4 \cdot N^{-0.34} + 410.7 \cdot D^{-0.28}$$

The Chinchilla functional form: loss equals 1.69 plus 406.4 over the parameter count to the 0.34 plus 410.7 over the token count to the 0.28, with the paper's fitted coefficients. The paper is Hoffmann, J., et al. (2022), "Training compute-optimal large language models", arXiv:2203.15556, cited in the References below. In those terms: N is the parameter count and D is the token count, the two terms of the joint law; 406.4 and 410.7 and the exponents 0.34 and 0.28 are the paper's fitted coefficients.

$$C = 6 \cdot N \cdot D$$

Compute is about six times the parameter count times the token count: about 2 FLOPs per parameter per token on the forward pass and about 4 on the backward pass. Compute is your money.

The split procedure: hold compute fixed at the rule above, vary the split between params and tokens (hold one fixed and vary the other), and read where the loss bottoms out. And the constant 1.69 plays the role the opening section gives the floor: the loss bottoms out at a floor that no training run ever goes under, the part of the loss that neither term shrinks.

The bottom of the U is not at 1:1. It sits data-heavy, tens of tokens per parameter. Toward param-heavy the loss climbs: you bought parameters you cannot feed. For a fixed budget, scale params and data together.

How to play

  • Param-heavy split: Away from an even split toward param-heavy, the loss climbs.

A final honesty note. Scaling laws describe smooth loss, and smooth loss is what you budget with. But capabilities can jump: a loss curve never told anyone the model would suddenly do arithmetic. The line prices the loss, not the emergence. Use scaling laws to decide where the next dollar goes, not to prophesy what the model will learn.
