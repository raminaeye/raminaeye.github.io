Naive Bayes

Classifying by counting words.

How do you sort 15,000 forum posts without reading them?

You know me, I love a question that sounds impossible and turns out to be bookkeeping. Here is one: how do you sort 15,076 forum posts into 20 newsgroups without reading a single one? You do not read. You count. The post in front of you says "windows" twice and "motif" twice, and you ask: which pile uses those words the most? That is naive Bayes. It is a classifier that treats every document as a bag of words, counts the bag, and bets on the class whose counts look most like it.

Documents are bags of words

The first move is to throw away word order. "I need to port several applications" becomes a bag: port x1, applications x1, need x2. The notebook in this series does exactly this with a custom CountVectorizer over the 20 Newsgroups corpus: 15,076 training posts become rows of 10,000 word counts. Order gone, counts kept. It feels lossy, and it is, but for topic sorting the counts carry almost all the signal. A comp.windows.x post says "windows" a lot. A rec.sport.hockey post never does. The bag knows.

Bayes rule, then the naive part

Here is the whole classifier in one line. Read it slowly, because every symbol earns its place:

$$ P(c \mid d) \propto P(c) \prod_w P(w \mid c) $$

Term by term. $P(c \mid d)$ is the answer we want: how likely class $c$ is, given this document $d$. $P(c)$ is the prior: how common the class is before we read anything. In the notebook, comp.windows.x posts are 0.0513 of the training set, so every document starts with that as its comp.windows.x score before a single word is counted. $\prod_w P(w \mid c)$ is the likelihood: for every word in the document, how often does that word show up in class $c$'s posts? Multiply them all together. That product is the "naive" part: we pretend words appear independently, which is obviously false ("san" and "francisco" are not independent events) and works anyway. The word "naive" is doing a lot of heavy lifting in this algorithm's name.

Now the second equation, the one that does the actual counting:

$$ P(w \mid c) = \frac{\mathrm{count}(w, c) + \alpha}{N_c + \alpha V} $$

Term by term. $\mathrm{count}(w, c)$ is how many times word $w$ appeared in class $c$'s training posts. $N_c$ is the total number of words in class $c$. $V$ is the vocabulary size: 10,000 in the notebook. And $\alpha$ is Laplace smoothing, the add-a-little-to-everything trick. Without it, one word the class never saw gives $P(w \mid c) = 0$, and the whole product dies on the spot. With the notebook's $\alpha = 0.1$, every word gets a tiny baseline probability, as if the model had seen each word a tenth of a time before training started. It is stupid easy and it saves the entire classifier.

One more equation, the one the code actually runs. Multiplying thousands of tiny probabilities underflows to zero, so the notebook computes in log space, and so does the tool below:

$$ \log P(c \mid d) \propto \log P(c) + \sum_w \log P(w \mid c) $$

Same math, additions instead of multiplications. When $\alpha = 0$ makes a word probability exactly zero, $\log(0)$ would be negative infinity, so the tool floors it at a large negative number and marks the class "zeroed out" instead of letting the arithmetic explode. You will see this the moment you drag the alpha slider to zero.

The tool below replays that moment on a corpus small enough to hold in your head: 62 posts across the same 20 newsgroups, with that exact post sitting in the training pile. The math is the notebook's math, including $\alpha = 0.1$ as the default. Let me walk one word through the arithmetic so you trust the bars:

  1. In the toy corpus, the comp.windows.x pile holds 5 posts and 59 words, and "windows" appears twice in it.
  2. The toy vocabulary has 292 words, so \(P(\mathrm{windows} \mid \mathrm{comp.windows.x}) = (2 + 0.1) / (59 + 0.1 \cdot 292) = \mathbf{0.0238}\).
  3. The prior is \(\frac{5}{62} = \mathbf{0.0806}\) for comp.windows.x (5 posts out of 62) versus \(\frac{3}{62} = 0.0484\) for every other pile.
  4. Each word you add contributes its $\log P(w \mid c)$ to all 20 class scores at once. Thirteen words in, comp.windows.x stands alone at probability 1.0000 to four decimals.

Here is the notebook's classifier, stripped to the parts that matter:

class MultinomialNB:
    def __init__(self, alpha=1.0):
        self.alpha = alpha  # Laplace smoothing parameter

    def fit(self, X, y):
        # P(word|class) = (count of word in class + alpha) /
        #                (total words in class + alpha * vocab size)
        for i, cls in enumerate(unique_classes):
            X_cls = X[y == cls]
            total = X_cls.sum() + num_features * self.alpha
            self.feature_log_prob_[i, :] = np.log((X_cls.sum(axis=0) + self.alpha) / total)

    def predict_log_proba(self, X):
        # log P(class|doc) = log prior + doc . log P(word|class)
        return np.log(self.class_prior_) + X @ self.feature_log_prob_.T

How to play

  • The point: Every word you add shifts all 20 class scores at once, because each word contributes its log probability to every class. You can watch "windows" drag the comp.windows.x bar up.
  • What you will see: Play feeds the 13 words of the notebook's comp.windows.x post in one at a time at 2.2-second steps. comp.windows.x takes the lead on the first word and never lets go, while the other 19 bars drain away as evidence piles up. The word just added gets a green ring so you can spot it.
  • Word chips: Toggle words in and out by hand and watch all 20 bars respond live. Try just "port", then add "windows" and watch the comp.windows.x bar jump from about 0.49 to about 0.81.
  • Smoothing α (0 to 1): Sets the Laplace smoothing. Drag it to 0 and every class that never saw one of your words gets marked "zeroed out" (with the full post, that is 19 of the 20 classes: only comp.windows.x saw every word). Drag it back up and the bars soften as unseen words stop being fatal.
  • Reset: Clears the words and puts the smoothing back at 0.10, so all 20 bars sit at their priors again. Try it after a run and watch the evidence drain away.

What is next

Naive Bayes needed labeled piles: 15,076 posts, each already stamped with its newsgroup. But what if nobody labeled anything? What if you just have the points and want to find the clumps? That is k-means: no labels, no counting words, just points picking the nearest center until nobody moves. It is the unsupervised mirror of everything on this page.
