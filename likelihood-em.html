Maximum Likelihood Estimation

Fitting a model to data by picking the numbers that make the data most likely.

How do you set a model’s numbers from data? Pick the numbers that make the data most likely.

1 Fit the numbers that make the data likely

Every model has parameters, numbers set from data, such as a coin’s bias or a network’s weights. \(\theta\) stands for them. Maximum likelihood sets \(\theta\) to the value that makes the observed data most probable. Fitting happens once per fit, on the training data, by scoring candidate values; the fitted numbers are the state carried out of the fit.

2 Same formula, two questions

Flip a coin a few times and count the heads. Probability fixes \(\theta\), here the coin’s chance of heads, and asks which flips are likely. Likelihood fixes the flips you saw and asks which \(\theta\) explains them best. The coin flips are a textbook toy illustration of that scoring, and the same scoring picks a language model’s next-token probabilities.

The likelihood scores each possible \(\theta\) by how probable it makes the flips you actually saw.
\(\displaystyle L(\htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{\theta }) = \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{\theta }^{h}(1 - \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{\theta })^{t}\)
The likelihood scores theta by how probable it makes the flips. Parentheses hold theta as the argument, the caret is a power, each head adds a factor theta, each tail a factor 1 minus theta. Theta is the chance of heads; h heads, t tails.

3 Maximum likelihood picks the peak

Choosing the \(\theta\) at the top of the curve is maximum likelihood estimation (Figure 2).

The peak of the coin likelihood is simply the share of heads.
\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\hat{\theta }} = \frac{h}{h + t}\)
The best theta is the share of heads. Theta hat is the best theta, the top of the curve. h and t are the counts from the likelihood line above. Naive Bayes uses this share for a class chance.

4 Logs turn products into sums

A likelihood multiplies one chance per independent observation, so it shrinks fast: every new observation multiplies in another number below 1, and a long audio file underflows to zero. Logs fix that.

Logs turn a product of many small chances into a sum that never underflows and peaks at the same \(\theta\).
\(\displaystyle \log L(\htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{\theta }) = \sum_{i} \log P(x_{i} \mid \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{\theta })\)
Taking the log turns the product of many small chances into a sum that never underflows. x sub i is observation i; the sum adds over all observations. P of x sub i given theta is that observation's chance; the bar means given.

5 Fitting a Gaussian gives the sample mean and variance

A bell curve (see Common Probability Distributions) has two parameters, its mean \(\mu\) and its standard deviation \(\sigma\), whose square \(\sigma^{2}\) is the variance. Its likelihood is the product of each point’s height on the bell.

For a bell curve, maximum likelihood sets the mean to the average of the points and the variance to their average squared gap from that mean.
\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\hat{\mu }} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i} x_{i}\)
For a bell curve the best mean is the plain average. Mu hat is the best mean; mu is the center of the bell. x sub i is point i; the sum adds all points and 1 over N divides by how many there are.
\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\hat{\sigma }}^{2} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i} (x_{i} - \htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\hat{\mu }})^{2}\)
The variance is the average squared gap. Sigma hat squared is the variance, sigma the bell width. x sub i is point i. Dividing by N, not N minus 1, it comes out (N minus 1) over N of the true spread on average.

This snippet fits a bell with the card’s two formulas and again with scipy.stats.norm.fit.

import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm
rng = np.random.default_rng(SEED)
x = rng.normal(loc=MEAN, scale=SPREAD, size=N)
mu_hat = x.mean()
var_hat = np.mean((x - mu_hat) ** 2)
loc, scale = norm.fit(x)

  2. 2Computes \(\mu\) and \(\sigma^{2}\), eq card in Section 5, lines 1 and 2.

6 Losses you know are likelihoods

Builds on squared error in Linear Regression and cross-entropy in Logistic Regression

If each target is the prediction plus Gaussian noise, maximum likelihood is the same as minimizing squared error. For classes it is the same as minimizing cross-entropy, the loss logistic regression trains on.

Minimizing squared error, or cross-entropy for classes, is the same as maximizing a likelihood.
\(\displaystyle -\log \mathbf{N}(y \mid \htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{f(x)}, \htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\sigma }^{2}) =\)
\(\displaystyle \frac{(y - \htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{f(x)})^{2}}{2\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\sigma }^{2}} + \text{const}\)
Minus log of a bell is the squared gap between the true y and the prediction f of x, over twice sigma squared, plus constant. Bold N is the normal density, not count N. The constant does not move the fit. This is least squares.
\(\displaystyle -\log P(c \mid x)\)
For categories, minus the log of the chance the model gives the true class c is the cross-entropy loss. P of c given x is that chance; the bar means given. It is near 0 when the model is sure and right.

Every training step of regression or logistic regression minimizes the same minus log-likelihood this page derives.

7 When the peak misleads

A peak is only as good as the data behind it. With too little data the curve is flat, and many values score almost alike; Figure 2 steps through the peak search on that curve.

  • Too little data: few observations leave the curve flat, so the peak moves with one more flip. Gather more data, or say the peak is uncertain instead of quoting it as certain.
  • One outlier widens the bell: the variance counts squared gaps, so a single far point inflates the fitted spread. Check the far points before trusting the spread; Figure 5 shows how the bell sits on the points.
  • The variance runs a little small: dividing by N, not N minus 1, leaves the fitted bell narrower than the true spread on average. Know the bias, and use the corrected divisor when the count is small.

Tips: count first, then fit; check the far points before trusting the spread; when the curve is flat, say so instead of quoting a peak as certain; use logs whenever a product of many chances is scored.

What if part of the data is hidden, so counting cannot start? The EM Algorithm fits the same score in two alternating steps, guessing the labels and refitting until the peak stops moving.
