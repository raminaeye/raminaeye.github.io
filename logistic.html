Logistic Regression

A line that says yes or no.

Last page we fit a line that predicts numbers. But what if the answer is not a number at all? A line predicts 13.7. Useless when the question is "is this flower a versicolor, yes or no?" Same line, new job: we are going to squash it into a probability.

Why not just use the line?

You could try. Fit a line to 0-or-1 labels and call everything above 0.5 a yes. People did. It is awful: the line happily predicts 2.4 or -0.7, which are not probabilities, they are nonsense wearing a trench coat. One faraway outlier drags the whole line sideways.

So we keep the line (it is still doing the real work: $w \cdot x + b$) and we pass its output through a squashing function that pins everything between 0 and 1. That squashed number reads as a probability, and 0.5 becomes the decision boundary. Same line you already know, now allowed to vote yes or no.

The sigmoid: scores become probabilities

Take the line's raw score $z = w \cdot x + b$. It can be anything from minus infinity to plus infinity. Now feed it through the sigmoid:

$$ \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}} $$

Term by term:

  • $z$: the raw score, the line's unfiltered opinion. Big positive: the line is shouting yes. Big negative: shouting no.
  • $e^{-z}$: the exponential flips the shouting into a ratio. Positive $z$ makes it tiny, negative $z$ makes it huge.
  • The fraction: pins the result between 0 and 1, always. No more 2.4, no more -0.7.

Three numbers worth memorizing, all checkable on a calculator:

  1. $\sigma(0) = 1/(1+1) =$ 0.5. Dead center. Maximum uncertainty.
  2. $\sigma(2) = 1/(1+e^{-2}) =$ 0.8808. Confident yes.
  3. $\sigma(-2) = 1/(1+e^{2}) =$ 0.1192. Confident no.

Notice the symmetry: $\sigma(-2) = 1 - \sigma(2)$. The curve is a polite S, steep in the middle where the decision lives, flat at the extremes where the model has made up its mind.

How it learns: punish surprise, not distance

Squared error asks "how far off was the number?" For probabilities the better question is "how surprised were you?" If the truth is yes and you said 90% yes, no surprise. If you said 10% yes, huge surprise. That is the binary cross-entropy cost:

$$ J(w, b) = -\frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^{m} \left[ y_i \log h_i \right. $$
$$ \left. + (1 - y_i) \log (1 - h_i) \right] $$

where $h_i = \sigma(w \cdot x_i + b)$. Term by term:

  • $y_i \log h_i$: active only when the truth is yes ($y_i = 1$). You pay $-\log h_i$: predict 0.9, pay 0.1054, barely a slap. Predict 0.1, pay 2.3026, more than twenty times worse.
  • $(1 - y_i) \log (1 - h_i)$: the mirror term, active only when the truth is no. Same deal from the other side.
  • Only one term is ever active. Each sample picks its side and pays for its own surprise.

The gradient has a shape you already know:

$$ \frac{\partial J}{\partial w_j} = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^{m} (\sigma(w \cdot x_i + b) - y_i)\, x_{ij} $$

Compare it with linear regression's gradient from the last page. Identical, except the prediction is wrapped in $\sigma$. Beautiful. Two samples, checked by hand: $x = [1], y = 1$ and $x = [2], y = 0$, starting at $w = 0, b = 0$ so both predictions are $\sigma(0) = 0.5$:

  1. Weight $w$:
    $$ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial J}{\partial w} &= \frac{(0.5-1)(1) + (0.5-0)(2)}{2} \\ &= 0.5/2 = \mathbf{0.25} \end{aligned} $$
    Push $w$ up a little.
  2. Bias $b$:
    $$ \frac{\partial J}{\partial b} = \frac{(0.5-1) + (0.5-0)}{2} = 0/2 = \mathbf{0.0} $$
    The two mistakes cancel exactly. The bias stays put.

Watch it decide: 0.5 draws the line

Here is the money fact: $\sigma(z) = 0.5$ exactly when $z = 0$. So "probability at least one half" is exactly "$w \cdot x + b \ge 0$". The decision boundary is the line $w \cdot x + b = 0$. The same line from regression, now working as a fence between yes and no.

How to play

  • The point: The sigmoid turns the line's raw score into a probability, and the threshold draws the boundary: every point whose probability clears t lands on the yes side. Move t and the boundary slides.
  • What you'll see: The threshold sweeps from 0.1 to 0.9 in 9 steps; the dark boundary line for the selected class slides across the scatter while points flip between filled (yes) and hollow (no), and the marker on the sigmoid curve rides along with it.
  • Threshold t: Sets the yes/no cutoff. Try 0.1 and watch the boundary slide deep into the other classes' territory so nearly everything counts as yes; try 0.9 and watch it retreat until only the surest points stay filled.
  • setosa / versicolor / virginica: Picks which class's boundary you are moving. Try setosa and watch its boundary sit far from everyone else (easy class); try versicolor and watch it squeeze between its two neighbors (the hard one).
  • Reset: Puts the threshold back at 0.5 and the class back at versicolor, where the boundary is exactly w.x + b = 0. Try it after a sweep and watch the fence snap back to the textbook position.

A straight fence cannot bend

A straight fence cannot bend. To be honest: the Iris notebook this page is built on has no moons. One line, zero chance, against the two-moons crescents.
