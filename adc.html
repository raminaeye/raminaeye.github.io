ADC Fundamentals

Your brain speaks analog, continuous voltage that can take any value. Your computer only speaks integers. The ADC is the translator between the two, and if you don't understand how that translation works, you will misread every neural signal you record. Here is how I think about it. Fair warning: this is a topic I am still learning, so treat this page as my working notes, not gospel.

Left to right: the electrode picks up microvolts (millionths of a volt, the tiny scale neural signals live at), the amplifier multiplies them into the ADC's volt range by its gain, a multiplication factor of 250 times, the anti-alias filter removes the high frequencies before sampling so they cannot fold back as fake low frequencies, which is the aliasing the filter is named for stopping, the ADC samples in time and rounds in amplitude, and out come integer codes.

An ADC turns a wave into numbers

An ADC turns continuous voltage into integer codes. Bit depth decides how fine the stairs are (the LSB, the Least Significant Bit, the voltage width of one stair step), and that sets your quantization noise floor (the level of rounding noise sitting under the signal), measured as the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). Sampling rate plus an anti-aliasing filter decide whether you capture the signal or a distorted ghost of it.

What the ADC measures

The brain produces analog signals, continuous voltage that can take any value. The ADC, analog to digital converter, is the component that does this translation.

Every ADC has a reference voltage, V_ref. It defines the top and bottom of what the ADC can measure. The ADC is differential, meaning it measures the difference between its two inputs rather than a single input on its own, so a span of 2.4V means it can measure from -1.2V to 1.2V. The ADC can take voltage within the range -1.2V to 1.2V, and assigns each interval of voltage a digital code. Anything beyond this region gets saturated; the ADC output just flatlines at its maximum digital code.

Quantization rounds the continuous voltage to the nearest available code. If analog input is a ramp, ADC is a staircase. Each stair step corresponds to one LSB (Least Significant bit). The finer the steps, the closer the digital version is to the real signal.

The ADC resolution, number of bits, determines how precisely you can measure small voltage changes. That range is the reference voltage that determines how large a signal can be measured before saturation, clipping. These two together set the quantization granularity, the smallest detectable change.

If the ADC has 10 bits of resolution it can represent 1024 values. Each bin, one voltage interval between two rungs, would correspond to one discrete code, integers from -512 to +511.

The LSB: how wide one stair step is

The LSB represents the voltage difference between two adjacent digital codes. The LSB is the bridge between physical voltage and digital code. Mathematically: the LSB is the full voltage span the ADC can see, divided by the number of codes.

At ±1.2 V and 10 bits that is a few millivolts per code. Measured in volts, this step size is what we call the LSB.

A wider V_ref stretches each step; more bits multiplies the step count and shrinks every step. It is the one number that ties the analog world to the digital one.

Four moves:

  1. Grab the voltage
  2. Snap it to the nearest rung
  3. Write down the bits
  4. Accept the rounding error

Gain: a magnifying glass that shrinks the room

The neural signals produce much smaller voltages, thousands of times smaller, so if you directly connect to the ADC it would read as zero because each step is 2.34mV. That's where the amplifier, the gain, comes in. The amplifier simply multiplies the electrode voltage by a constant factor.

The amplifier brings the neural signals into a range the ADC can digitize accurately. The ADC-side LSB is 2.34mV per code, but at the electrode one code covers far less: the electrode-side LSB is the ADC-side LSB divided by the gain. At 250x gain that is 9.375μV per code, a much finer slice of the original signal.

The gain affects both range and resolution equally. With a smaller LSB you can detect smaller voltages, but the input range that fits without saturating shrinks too, a clipping risk. The amplifier must make sure the largest expected electrode signal stays within what the ADC can see.

The maximum electrode voltage you can measure without saturation is inversely proportional to the gain: what the ADC sees must stay within the reference voltage, between the rails, the plus 1.2 V and minus 1.2 V limits the signal cannot pass, so crank the gain and the measurable voltage at the electrode shrinks in lockstep.

So far: the ADC converts continuous voltage, analog signals, into discrete digital signals. V_ref is the max voltage the ADC can see, quantization is rounding to the nearest integer, and the LSB is how wide each step is.

Changing the LSB directly changes the measured voltage range. At the ADC input the max is about the reference voltage, ±1.2V, no matter the gain; gain only changes what that means at the electrode. A smaller LSB means higher resolution over the same range: adding bits splits the same ±1.2V into finer steps. Only a smaller reference voltage shrinks the range itself.

Rounding error never goes away: it becomes the noise floor

Because ADC converts a continuous analog voltage to a discrete digital code, it must round the input to the nearest quantization level. This rounding introduces a quantization error. The true voltage can sit anywhere inside one LSB step, and the code names that step, so the error is never more than half an LSB either way. Even a noiseless perfect signal carries that half-step of uncertainty because of digitization.

If you plot the quantization error for a slowly increasing signal it would look like a saw-tooth wave, ramps linearly between -½ LSB to +½ LSB, because the ADC snaps to the next step. For a real neural signal the waveform continuously changes at ADC step boundaries, so each crossing is where a sawtooth cycle resets.

If you overlay these sawtooth error cycles but with slightly shifted phases they no longer line up, it blurs together like random noise. Quantization error carries no bias: its average is zero, and any particular error value in the range is roughly as likely as any other. That is a definition of a uniform random variable, hence we treat quantization error as uniform noise, with variance (a measure of how spread out the error values are) equal to the LSB squared over twelve:

Every extra bit buys you 6.02 dB

The signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) compares the strength of your desired signal to the background noise level. In decibels, take ten times the log of that power ratio. Log is short for logarithm, a way of counting ratios in equal steps: multiplying the power by ten always adds the same amount, so very large and very small ratios fit on one scale.

For a full-scale sine wave (a sine wave that fills the whole range the ADC can measure, swinging across that full range) through an ADC, that ratio works out to a famous rule of thumb: about 6 dB per bit, plus 1.76 dB.

Each extra bit buys about 6 dB, which means twice the resolution, or half the quantization noise voltage:

  1. One bit doubles the codes, which halves the noise voltage.
  2. Halving the voltage is 6.02 dB.
  3. The 1.76 is a shape factor: the head start the sine wave's shape gives you over the noise's shape.
Every extra bit buys you 6.02 dB.

Sample too slowly and high frequencies fold back as impostors

So far we discussed the amplitude axis controlled by the LSB. The time axis is controlled by the sampling rate. The sampling rate tells you how many samples per second the ADC can take. The signal bandwidth, the range of frequencies the signal contains from its lowest to its highest, links to this sampling rate through the Nyquist rule: the sampling rate must be at least twice the highest frequency in the signal.

Here the highest frequency component of your analog signal must stay below the Nyquist frequency, which is half the sampling rate. If you sample lower than that, the high frequency part of the signal folds back into lower frequencies and you get aliasing.

Sampling keeps one value every sample period, which is the inverse of the sampling rate. This is similar to multiplying the continuous signal by a train of delta spikes (infinitely narrow pulses, one at each sampling instant) spaced by the sample period. By spectrum I mean the signal re-described by how much of each frequency it contains, and the frequency domain is that re-description, a view of the signal as a mix of frequencies instead of as a voltage changing over time. In the frequency domain that multiplication corresponds to repetition of the signal's spectrum at multiples of the sampling rate: periodicity in one domain is repetition in the other. If those repeats overlap, the frequencies mix.

Multiplying the time domain by this train of spikes convolves the spectrum with the train's own spectrum. Convolution is the combining operation behind that word: slide one shape across the other one step at a time, at each stop multiply the values that line up, add those products into a single number for that stop, and collect those numbers as the result. Here that sliding and adding replicates your original spectrum at each spike location, one copy of the spectrum centered on every spike of the train.

Here is the same idea with actual sines. The gray wave is the real signal going into the ADC. The dots are all the ADC ever sees. The red wave is what the ADC thinks it saw. Past the Nyquist line, a 600 Hz signal folds into a fake 400 Hz one. Nothing about the dots changed. That is why aliasing is scary: the samples are telling the truth and lying at the same time.

Say the ADC sampling rate is 1 kHz, that means we sample every 1 ms. If our analog signal was a sine wave at 600 Hz, each cycle would last 1.67 ms. Because the ADC can only represent up to the Nyquist frequency of 500 Hz, anything above that mirrors back around 500 Hz.

In our example that lands at 400 Hz. Two different analog frequencies produce identical sample sequences when sampled at 1 kHz. Another way to look at aliasing is through phase: a sine wave of frequency f_sig completes one full 360° rotation per period, and the period is one over the signal frequency.

At 1 kHz with a 600 Hz sine wave, we won't land on the same phase each time: the phase advances more than 180° between samples, skipping ahead in cycles, so it shows up as a lower-frequency mirror. Move more than 180° per sample and you skip ahead too far: the samples look like a slower reverse motion, at 400 Hz.

Higher sampling rate means more data, higher power and better temporal precision. The quantization noise is spread over the full Nyquist band, so increasing the sampling rate spreads the noise thinner per Hz, slightly improving SNR per bandwidth.

Kill the high frequencies before they can fold

If you can't increase the sampling rate, you can still avoid aliasing using anti-aliasing filtering. Before the ADC samples your analog signal the system inserts an analog low-pass filter, called anti-aliasing filter, that removes all frequencies above the Nyquist frequency. This filter chops off the high frequency parts of the signal so they can't fold back.

analog signal low-pass filter ADC digital samples

Where the filter sits: between the analog world and the sampler. Kill the high frequencies here and they can never fold.

The anti-aliasing low-pass cutoff is a little below Nyquist. Because filtering is not perfect, we typically oversample slightly, meaning we sample a little faster than the minimum the signal actually needs, so the filtering design constraint is easy for digital downsampling later, where downsampling means keeping only some of the samples afterwards to bring the rate back down.

Filters have a transition band and it doesn't suddenly go from 100% pass to 100% block, it rolls off gradually. A filter's cut off is the point where the output power has fallen to half of its input power. Half power is -3 dB, about 0.7x the amplitude.

The roll-off is the rate at which the filter attenuates the signals beyond the cutoff and it's measured in dB per decade. A decade is 10x frequency.

It's how sharp the transition band is. The filter order adds another stage that makes the roll-off steeper. Higher order means steeper cutoff, stronger rejection of unwanted frequencies but also more complexity, more power and more phase distortion (the signal's frequency components getting delayed by different amounts, so their timing no longer lines up). If the Nyquist is close to the signal bandwidth, you need a filter that attenuates everything beyond Nyquist very strongly, that's a higher-order filter.

Let's take a look at our aliasing example again. At a 500 Hz cutoff the filter is only down -3 dB. Strong 600 Hz components would still leak through at about half power, about 0.7x the amplitude, and alias down to 400 Hz, so you need to set the cutoff lower so the attenuation is great, about -40 dB. The actual cutoff depends on the filter and its order. A first order filter attenuates at -20 dB per decade and each additional order adds another -20 dB/decade.

If you want at least -40 dB of attenuation by 500 Hz, starting the roll-off at 400 Hz, where the response is -3 dB, does not get there by itself: even at order 8, the response at 500 Hz is only about -16 dB down, and -40 dB would take an order near 21. For a Butterworth low-pass filter, a design that keeps its response as flat as possible where the signal should pass and then falls away past the cutoff, higher order means a steeper collapse past the cutoff, which is exactly why you pay for higher order when Nyquist sits close to your signal.

Remember that using higher order filters comes with side effects, phase shift. If your signal contains many frequencies at once then each component might get delayed by a different amount and when you combine them the timing is no longer aligned. The waveform shape is altered even if the spectrum looks fine.

Common IIR filters (infinite impulse response: the output feeds back into itself, so every blip keeps echoing) like Butterworth have non-linear phases outside their transition bands. The higher the order the more non-linearity and more total phase delay. That is why sometimes it is wiser to use two lower-order filters cascaded, placed one after the other so the signal passes through both in turn, than one higher order filter.

We're typically interested in signals up to 250 Hz. For example high gamma signals, the fastest band of brain rhythms, are around 70-200 Hz. At 1 kHz sampling rate, with 500 Hz Nyquist frequency, we can capture this band using a high-order filter. The timing of high gamma activity (HGA) can be slightly skewed because the filter's group delay (how much it delays each frequency) ripples can extend well into 200 Hz.

Three converters, three tradeoffs: speed, precision, power

Flash compares the voltage against every level at once, with a comparator (a circuit that compares two voltages and reports which one is larger) per rung. The fastest thing there is, but the comparators multiply with the bits, so it stays at low bit depths and drinks power. You meet it where speed beats everything.

SAR (successive-approximation register) homes in one bit at a time, like guessing a number with higher-or-lower questions. Three questions for eight rungs. The middle ground, and what most neural headstages use, where a headstage is the small front end electronics package that sits at the electrodes and holds the first amplifiers and converters next to the signal source: fast enough, fine enough, sips power.

Sigma-delta samples way faster than it needs to and averages the noise away. Slow, but extremely fine. Inside it, a feedback loop keeps a running sum of the error so far and feeds back the difference between its coarse output and the input, and that loop is where the name comes from, sigma for the sum and delta for the difference. The feedback pushes the rounding noise up toward high frequencies, where the averaging that follows can remove it, and that is why sampling so far past what the signal needs can still end up so fine. It is the oversampling idea from Sample too slowly and high frequencies fold back as impostors taken to its limit.

Okay, so that is ADC in a nutshell, at least how I understand it so far. Bit depth sets how many codes you get, and together with V_ref that gives you the LSB, the size of each stair step. Gain is what shrinks the electrode LSB down to microvolts so tiny neural signals don't get lost between steps. Quantization error is stuck at half a step no matter how clean your signal is, and each extra bit buys you 6.02 dB of SNR.

Then the time axis: sample faster than twice your highest frequency or the high stuff folds back as aliases, and if you can't sample faster, put an anti-aliasing low-pass filter in front and accept the phase cost. I'm still learning this, so take all of it as working notes.
