This page builds on RL Basics and Aligning Language Models with RL. The terms below are the ones this page keeps using.

  • An agent is the learner that picks the moves. A state is the agent’s situation right now; a move, or action, is one choice the agent can make in a state; a reward is the number the world hands back after each move, good or bad. (RL Basics)
  • A token is a word or a piece of a word; a language model writes its reply one token at a time. (Aligning Language Models with RL)
  • Here the agent is the language model. Here the state is the prompt plus the tokens written so far, and the move is the next token. Here the reward is one number for the whole reply, given when the reply ends.
  • RL, short for reinforcement learning, is learning which moves to make from rewards rather than from labelled answers. (RL Basics)
  • A policy is the agent’s rule for choosing a move in each state; it may always pick the same move or pick by chance. The policy is the language model being trained to write replies. (RL Basics, Aligning Language Models with RL)

1People are the slow, costly part

Collecting people’s comparisons of two replies is slow and costly. Constitutional AI, which has a language model check harmful replies against a written list of rules, swapped people out only for harm labels; can an AI write every kind of label?

The running example is Reddit TL;DR, a set of Reddit posts with a short summary written underneath. A model writes two summaries of one post, and the task is to say which summary is better.

One example carries the whole page. The cat laptop post and its two summaries follow the labeler from its first pick to its last failure.

RLAIF, reinforcement learning from AI feedback, answers yes for many tasks by letting an existing language model write the labels.

Those labels come from asking the model which of two summaries is better.

A reward model is a language model with a one-number output, trained on people’s comparisons so the reply they preferred scores higher. (Reward Models from Human Preferences)

RLHF, reinforcement learning from human feedback, trains a language model to earn a score from such a network, trained on people’s comparisons of two replies.

RLAIF keeps this pipeline and changes only who writes the labels.

2Ask a strong language model which is better

An AI labeler is an existing language model, not trained for this, that is given instructions and two candidate replies and asked which is better.

Its probability of answering “1” or “2” is the label.

The labeler reads the post and both summaries, then answers which is better.

2.1Slot preference

Slot preference, which the paper calls position bias, means the AI labeler prefers a slot, first or second, whatever the two replies say.

That is why the cat laptop pair is shown both ways in section 3.1: the two orders average out the slot pick.

A small labeler shows it most.

3Labels from probabilities

3.1One label from both orders

A soft label is the model’s probability for each choice instead of a hard pick (Constitutional AI).

Here the reward model is trained with the Bradley-Terry loss, which turns the score gap into the chance the preferred summary wins, trained to match the soft label instead of a hard yes or no (Reward Models).

So a pair the labeler finds close pushes the two scores apart only a little, and a clear pair pushes them far apart.

\(\displaystyle \quad+\ \htmlClass{eqt sym-pai}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-pai)}{P_{\mathrm{AI}}}}\big(\text{"2"}\mid \htmlClass{eqt sym-x}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-x)}{x}},\htmlClass{eqt sym-y}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-y)}{y_2}},\htmlClass{eqt sym-y}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-y)}{y_1}}\big))\)
In the swapped prompt \(y_1\) sits in slot 2, so we read “2”. So L2 adds the same chance with the seating swapped, and the half averages the two.

The average cancels the slot preference; if the labeler cannot tell the summaries apart, \(p_1\) ends near one half instead of favouring the better summary.

Averaging stops the slot from mattering; it does not make a weak labeler right.

3.2Skipping the reward model

Once the labels exist, the language model is trained by writing summaries and getting a score for each one.

Direct RLAIF skips the reward model: the direct labeler rates each fresh summary of the cat laptop post itself, and that rating, rescaled, is the reward.

Each summary of the post in Figure 1 is only a sentence or two long.

In RLAIF the reward is the score of a reward model trained on AI labels or, in direct RLAIF, the AI labeler’s own rating.

For summaries, the paper’s RL prompts are the Reddit posts of the training set.

SFT, supervised fine-tuning, trains the model on replies people wrote with the ordinary next-token loss; RL usually starts from that model. (Aligning Language Models with RL)

For summaries, the SFT model is trained on Reddit posts and the summaries people wrote for them.

Direct RLAIF skips the reward model.

During RL the AI labeler rates each summary the policy writes on a rating scale.

An expected value is the average over everything that can happen by chance, weighted by how likely each outcome is. (Probability and Bayes Rule, RL Basics)

The labeler’s expected rating is the reward.

In the paper, raters preferred direct RLAIF’s summaries over those of the reward-model route when both used a labeler the size of the policy.

The reference model \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\) is a frozen copy of the SFT model. (Aligning Language Models with RL)

KL measures how different two probability distributions are, zero when they match; the KL penalty charges the policy for drifting away from the reference model. (Aligning Language Models with RL)

Direct RLAIF skips the reward model because a trained one grows out of date as the policy’s summaries drift away from the ones it learned on.

Equation 2. The direct RLAIF reward. The AI labeler’s expected rating, stretched to run from minus one to one, used as the reward itself.
\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt sym-rai}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-rai)}{r_{\mathrm{AI}}}}={\sum_{v=1}^{10} \htmlClass{eqt sym-v}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-v)}{v}}}\;{\htmlClass{eqt sym-pai}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-pai)}{P_{\mathrm{AI}}}}\big(\htmlClass{eqt sym-v}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-v)}{v}}\mid \htmlClass{eqt sym-x}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-x)}{x}},\,\htmlClass{eqt sym-y}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-y)}{y}}\big)}\)
The AI labeler rates one fresh summary of the cat laptop post from 1 to 10. Here \(y\) is one summary the policy wrote, and \(v\) is a rating. \(P_{\mathrm{AI}}(v \mid x, y)\) is the AI labeler’s probability of writing that rating, and \(r_{\mathrm{AI}}\) is the probability-weighted average rating, so line 1 is the rating the labeler gives on average.
\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt sym-rai}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-rai)}{\tilde r_{\mathrm{AI}}}}=\dfrac{2\,\big(\htmlClass{eqt sym-rai}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-rai)}{r_{\mathrm{AI}}}}-1\big)}{9}-1\)
\(\tilde r_{\mathrm{AI}}\) is the same score stretched to run from minus one to one, and it is used as the reward \(r(x, y)\) in the Aligning Language Models with RL objective, so line 2 only changes the range, not the order of the summaries. The stretch puts the middle of the 1-10 scale at zero, so replies rated above the middle get a positive reward and below it a negative one, bounded in [-1, 1].

The paper says the score is normalized to [-1, 1] but gives no formula; line 2 is our straight-line rescale.

So in direct RLAIF the labeler’s rating is the reward itself, with no reward model in between.

3.3How often the AI labeler agrees with people

The paper calls how often the AI labeler agrees with people AI labeler alignment; it measures agreement with people, not the alignment of the trained model.

The labeler’s prompt can carry a few worked example labels, though the main summary labels used none.

The pairs people labeled, kept apart from any examples in the labeler’s prompt and from the pairs the labels were trained on, are written \(\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{test}}\).

Agreement is counted on those pairs only.

Agreement is counted on held-out pairs like the cat laptop pair, where the person’s pick is Summary 1.

On the Reddit summaries, the largest labeler agreed with people more often than the smaller ones.

Equation 3. Agreement with people. How often the AI labeler’s pick matches the person’s pick on held-out pairs.
\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt sym-agree}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-agree)}{\mathrm{agreement}}}={\htmlClass{eqt sym-e}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-e)}{\mathbb{E}}}_{\htmlClass{eqt sym-dtest}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-dtest)}{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{test}}}}}\big[\htmlClass{eqt sym-ind}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-ind)}{\mathbb{1}}}[\htmlClass{eqt sym-what}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-what)}{\hat w}}=\htmlClass{eqt sym-w}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-w)}{w}}]\big]}\)
\(w\) is Summary 1, the summary the person picked on the cat laptop post, and \(\hat w\) is the summary the AI labeler picked, the one with the larger soft label in the both-order soft label card. \(\mathbb{1}[\cdot]\) is the indicator, a marker that is one for a matching pair and zero otherwise.
\(\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{test}}\) is the held-out pairs like the cat laptop pair, taught in How often the AI labeler agrees with people, and \(\mathbb{E}\) is the average over them, so the agreement is the share of held-out pairs where the picks match.

The paper writes \(z_{\mathrm{acc}}\).

So agreement is the share of pairs where the AI labeler and the person picked the same summary.

4From both-order labels to an RL update

5Both-order labels and direct scores

labeler returns, after the prompt, the AI labeler’s next-token logits, the model’s raw scores for each next token before they are turned into probabilities; id_1 and id_2 are the token ids of “1” and “2”.

The code below reads the cat laptop post and its two summaries: the "1" vs "2" probabilities, the both-order label, and the direct reward.

import torch
def labeler_probs(logits, id_1, id_2):                      # [1] read "1" vs "2"
    return torch.softmax(torch.stack([logits[id_1], logits[id_2]]), 0)
def both_orders_label(labeler, post, s1, s2, id_1, id_2):   # [2] the both-order soft label card
    a = labeler_probs(labeler(post, s1, s2), id_1, id_2)[0] # s1 first
    b = labeler_probs(labeler(post, s2, s1), id_1, id_2)[1] # s1 second
    return 0.5 * (a + b)
def d_rlaif_reward(rating_probs):                           # [3] the direct RLAIF reward card
    r_ai = (torch.arange(1, 11) * rating_probs).sum()       # expected rating
    return 2 * (r_ai - 1) / 9 - 1                           # straight-line rescale
  1. 1Reads the “1” vs “2” probabilities.
  2. 2The both-order soft label card: both seatings read and averaged.
  3. 3The direct RLAIF reward card: the expected rating, then the straight-line rescale.

6Labeler size and order

7How close AI labels came to human labels

AI labels came close to human labels.

Two settings shape the labels: the labeler’s size, and whether it reasons before it picks.

The AI labeler plugs into the loop in two places: up front, it labels pairs to train the reward model, or during RL, it rates each reply the policy writes.

Those two settings shape the labeler’s labels on pairs like the cat laptop pair.

8When an AI labeler is good enough

  • You have a strong language model but few labels.
  • The labeler can read both candidates.
  • Labels must be refreshed often, because labels go stale as the policy’s replies drift.

The checklist fits the cat laptop post: a strong model but few labels can label its summaries, the labeler reads both summaries, and the labels are refreshed as the policy’s summaries drift.

10Learning from AI-labelled pairs without RL

Do we even need a reward model and an RL loop to learn from these pairs?

The cat laptop pair is a preference pair: one post, two summaries, a label saying which reply is better, and DPO trains on pairs like it with no reward model and no RL loop.

A preference pair is one prompt, two replies, and a label saying which reply a person preferred. (Reward Models from Human Preferences)

DPO trains on preference pairs directly, and AI-labelled pairs work too.

GRPO and DeepSeek-R1 replace the labeler with a program where answers can be checked.
