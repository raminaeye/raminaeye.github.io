Policy-Based and Actor-Critic Methods: REINFORCE, PPO and SAC

Learn the move itself, judge it with a critic, keep each update small, and act with real numbers.

These methods learn the policy, the rule that picks the move, directly: a network turns the state into move probabilities, or into the action itself, and training shifts the policy toward moves that led to more reward. The goal throughout is the largest expected return, the reward collected from now on. REINFORCE learns once per episode; actor-critic adds a critic that judges every step; PPO reuses each batch for several small updates; DDPG, TD3 and SAC output real-number actions and learn from stored steps.

A few ideas come from Value-Based RL. A Q-value Q(s, a), a score, is the future reward an agent expects from move a in state s; a value-based agent moves it toward a target built from one step, as in Value-based methods (Sutton and Barto’s RL textbook, 2018):

The Q-learning target adds the reward now to the best score one step later.
target = r + γ · max Q(s′, a′)
r is the reward the move earned and s′ the state it led to; max Q(s′, a′) is the highest score over the moves a′ there. The discount γ, from 0 to 1, shrinks rewards that come later.

An ε-greedy agent plays its best-scoring move except for a small chance ε of a random one. An on-policy method learns only from moves its current policy just made; an off-policy one can also learn from older moves, so it can train on random batches from a replay buffer of past steps. A target network is a frozen or slowly updated copy of the network that computes the targets so they hold still. Because the max over noisy scores tends to pick one that came out too high, Q-learning overestimates.

1 Policy-based methods

A policy-based agent skips the scores. Its network turns the state into a probability for each move, and the agent samples from them. Sampling matters when any fixed move can be beaten, as in rock-paper-scissors, where the best play is each move a third of the time (Prisner 2014).

REINFORCE (Williams 1992), the simplest policy-based method, learns once per episode: at the end, every move becomes more likely in proportion to the return that followed it, the reward collected from that move on:

REINFORCE makes each move more likely in proportion to the reward that came after it.
loss = −Σt log π(at |⁠ st) · Gt
π(at | st) is the probability the policy gave the move it made at step t (the bar reads "given"). Gt is the return from step t on, each later reward shrunk once per step of delay by the discount γ. Taking the log makes the gradient the gradient of π divided by π. A move the policy already favors is sampled often, and dividing by π cancels that, so a move rises by its return, not by how often it was picked. Σt adds over the episode. The minus sign makes it a loss: moves before a large return become more likely, moves before a negative one less likely.

One REINFORCE episode on gymnasium’s CartPole:

import gymnasium as gym
import torch
from torch.distributions import Categorical

gamma = 0.99
env = gym.make("CartPole-v1")
policy = torch.nn.Sequential(
    torch.nn.Linear(4, 128), torch.nn.ReLU(),
    torch.nn.Linear(128, 2))
optimizer = torch.optim.Adam(policy.parameters(), lr=1e-2)

s, info = env.reset()
log_probs, rewards, done = [], [], False
while not done:
    dist = Categorical(logits=policy(torch.as_tensor(s)))
    a = dist.sample()
    s, r, terminated, truncated, info = env.step(a.item())
    log_probs.append(dist.log_prob(a))
    rewards.append(r)
    done = terminated or truncated
G, returns = 0.0, []
for r in reversed(rewards):
    G = r + gamma * G
    returns.insert(0, G)
G_t = torch.tensor(returns)
loss = -(torch.stack(log_probs) * G_t).sum()
optimizer.zero_grad()
loss.backward()
optimizer.step()
  1. 1Sets γ, CartPole and a small policy network with its optimizer.
  2. 2Plays one full episode, keeping each move’s log-probability and reward.
  3. 3Computes the return Gt backward from the last reward.
  4. 4The loss from the first eq card in Section 1, then one gradient step.

Its weakness is noise: Gt includes the luck of every later reward, so updates vary a lot between episodes. Two additions help (the RL textbook, section 13.4):

A baseline steadies the update, and an entropy bonus keeps the policy exploring.
At = Gt − b(st)
The baseline b(st) is any guess that depends on the state but not on the move, usually a learned guess of the return from st. At says how much better the return was than that guess.
loss = −Σt log π(at |⁠ st) · At
The same loss as REINFORCE with At in place of Gt. On average the baseline does not change the gradient, so the method still moves the same way; it only lowers the variance of each update.
H(π(· |⁠ s)) = −Σa π(a |⁠ s) · log π(a |⁠ s)
H, the entropy, measures how spread out the move probabilities are: largest when every move is equally likely, zero when one move is certain. The dot in π(· | s) stands for all moves at once, and Σa adds over them.
losstotal = loss − β · Σt H(π(· |⁠ st))
β is a small weight. Subtracting the entropy rewards keeping options open, so the policy does not lock onto one move too early.

2 Actor-critic methods

Waiting for the end of an episode to judge every move is slow and noisy. An actor-critic agent judges each move one step later, by whether things went better or worse than it expected.

In actor-critic, the actor is the REINFORCE policy. The critic V(s) is a learned baseline that estimates the return from a state. Scoring both the state before and the state after a move lets it judge that move one step later, not at the episode’s end (the RL textbook, section 13.5):

In one actor-critic update the critic’s estimate is the baseline, and the actor’s move is judged against it.
A(s, a) = Q(s, a) − V(s)
A(s, a), the advantage, is how much better move a is than the critic expects from state s; Q(s, a) is the return expected after playing a.
A ≈ δ = r + γ · V(s′) − V(s)
≈ reads "is estimated by". One real reward plus the guess from the next state, minus the guess from this one. δ is the TD error (temporal difference), how much one step changed the guess. REINFORCE with a baseline used G − V(s) instead, which waits for the episode’s end; δ is less noisy, but only as good as the critic.
actor loss = −log π(a |⁠ s) · A
The REINFORCE line with the advantage in place of the return: the move gets more likely only if it beat the guess.
critic loss = (r + γ · V(s′) − V(s))2
δ squared, so a miss in either direction counts. Making it smaller pulls V(s) toward one real reward plus the guess at s′, which is held fixed as the target.

One update on the last B steps the current policy played, where no_grad holds V(s′) fixed.

import torch
from torch.distributions import Categorical

gamma = 0.99

def actor_critic_update(actor, critic, optimizer, batch):
    s, a, r, s_next, done = batch   # tensors, B steps each
    v_s = critic(s).squeeze(-1)
    with torch.no_grad():
        v_next = critic(s_next).squeeze(-1)
    delta = r + gamma * v_next * (1 - done) - v_s
    A = delta.detach()
    log_pi = Categorical(logits=actor(s)).log_prob(a)
    actor_loss = -(log_pi * A).mean()
    critic_loss = delta.pow(2).mean()
    optimizer.zero_grad()
    (actor_loss + critic_loss).backward()
    optimizer.step()
  1. 1Critic values and δ, eq card in Section 2, line 2.
  2. 2Actor loss with A = δ, line 3, the up arrow in Figure 3.
  3. 3Critic loss, line 4, the arrow in Figure 3 that trains the critic.
  4. 4One gradient step on both losses.

A3C, asynchronous advantage actor-critic (Mnih et al. 2016), runs many workers on copies of the world, all updating one shared network; their varied experience steadies learning without replay. In place of the one-step δ it uses an n-step return and adds the entropy bonus. A2C, advantage actor-critic, is the synchronous version: all copies step, then make one joint update. Both are on-policy.

3 PPO clipping

A batch of steps only describes the policy that played it, so learning from it several times is safe only while the policy stays close to that one.

PPO, proximal policy optimization, is actor-critic that reuses each on-policy batch for several updates but stops rewarding any change that moves the policy far from the one that collected it. Without that stop, every extra pass pushes the same moves further using advantages measured under the old policy, the new policy drifts far from it, and performance can collapse (Schulman et al. 2017):

PPO stops rewarding a change once the new policy leaves a band around the old one.
ratio = πnew(a |⁠ s)πold(a |⁠ s)
How much more likely (above 1) or less likely (below 1) the policy being trained makes the move than πold, the policy that collected the batch.
clipped = clip(ratio, 1 − ε, 1 + ε)
clip holds the ratio inside the band from 1 − ε to 1 + ε. ε here is the band’s half-width, 0.2 in the paper, not the exploration chance.
objective = min(ratio · A, clipped · A)
min keeps the smaller of the plain and clipped terms. Past the band, moving the ratio further earns nothing, while a change that hurts still counts in full. Training makes the objective larger; the loss is its negative.

One PPO update over a rollout, the steps just played, EPOCHS passes over shuffled minibatches:

import torch
from torch.distributions import Categorical

EPS_CLIP, EPOCHS, MINIBATCH = 0.2, 4, 128   # CleanRL ppo.py

def ppo_update(actor, optimizer, s, a, old_log_pi, A):
    old_log_pi, A = old_log_pi.detach(), A.detach()
    for epoch in range(EPOCHS):
        for mb in torch.randperm(len(s)).split(MINIBATCH):
            dist = Categorical(logits=actor(s[mb]))
            ratio = torch.exp(dist.log_prob(a[mb]) - old_log_pi[mb])
            clipped = ratio.clamp(1 - EPS_CLIP, 1 + EPS_CLIP)
            objective = torch.min(ratio * A[mb], clipped * A[mb])
            loss = -objective.mean()
            optimizer.zero_grad()
            loss.backward()
            optimizer.step()
  1. 1Freezes old log-probabilities and advantages; loops over epochs and minibatches.
  2. 2Ratio, eq card in Section 3, line 1.
  3. 3Clip, line 2, the band in Figure 5.
  4. 4Objective, line 3, turned into a loss.
  5. 5One gradient step per minibatch.

PPO gets A from GAE (Schulman et al. 2016), a weighted blend of the one-step δ over the next steps. The paper’s full loss adds the critic’s loss and an entropy bonus. PPO is also the RL step of RLHF, reinforcement learning from human feedback, in Aligning Language Models with RL.

4 Continuous actions

A continuous action is any real number in a range rather than a pick from a list, like the four joint torques of my Reacher arm on the DDPG page. There is no list of moves to take the max over, so the actor outputs the action and the critic tells it which way to nudge it. DDPG, deep deterministic policy gradient (Lillicrap et al. 2016), has a critic Q(s, a) that scores a state with an action, and an actor μ(s) that outputs one action and is trained to raise that score. It is off-policy, with replay and target copies that move a small share τ toward the networks each update.

One DDPG update on a replayed batch:

import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

gamma, tau = 0.99, 0.001   # DDPG paper

def ddpg_update(nets, opt_actor, opt_critic, batch):
    actor, critic, actor_t, critic_t = nets
    s, a, r, s_next, done = batch   # 64 steps, shape (B, 1)
    with torch.no_grad():
        q_next = critic_t(s_next, actor_t(s_next))
        target = r + gamma * (1 - done) * q_next
    critic_loss = F.mse_loss(critic(s, a), target)
    opt_critic.zero_grad()
    critic_loss.backward()
    opt_critic.step()
    actor_loss = -critic(s, actor(s)).mean()
    opt_actor.zero_grad()
    actor_loss.backward()
    opt_actor.step()
    for net, net_t in ((actor, actor_t), (critic, critic_t)):
        for p, p_t in zip(net.parameters(), net_t.parameters()):
            p_t.data.copy_(tau * p.data + (1 - tau) * p_t.data)
  1. 1Unpacks the four networks and 64 replayed steps.
  2. 2Target from the target networks, built like the Q-learning target in Value-Based RL, with the actor’s action in place of the max.
  3. 3Critic step toward the target.
  4. 4Actor step that raises the critic’s score of the actor’s own action.
  5. 5Soft update of both target networks by τ.

DDPG inherits Q-learning’s overestimation. TD3, twin delayed DDPG (Fujimoto et al. 2018), makes three changes, two in the target:

TD3 smooths the next action, then trusts the lower of two critics.
ã = μ−⁠(s′) + clip(noise, −c, c)
μ− is the slowly updated copy of the actor. Small random noise, clipped to stay within c of zero (standard deviation 0.2, c = 0.5 in the paper), is added to its action, so the target cannot rest on one narrow, too-high peak of the critic.
qmin = min(Q1−⁠(s′, ã), Q2−⁠(s′, ã))
Two critics learn side by side, and qmin is the smaller of their slowly updated copies’ scores for that action.
target = r + γ · qmin
Using the lower score counters overestimation instead of adding to it.

The third change updates the actor and target copies once per two critic updates, as the variants section of Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG) shows.

SAC, soft actor-critic (Haarnoja et al. 2018), has a stochastic actor, one that outputs a spread of actions, and is paid for that spread. A spread-out policy keeps exploring, keeps every action that looks about as good, and does not bet everything on one narrow peak of an imperfect critic, the failure TD3 fights with noise:

SAC is paid for reward and for keeping its actions spread out.
objective = Σt E[rt + α · H(π(· |⁠ st))]
E, the expectation, averages over the states and actions the policy meets. H is the entropy from Section 1, now earned at every step. α, the temperature, sets how much spread is worth against reward; the paper sets it through the reward scale, and CleanRL tunes it automatically.
V(s) = E[min(Q1, Q2)(s, a)
− α · log π(a |⁠ s)]
The soft value of a state: for an action a drawn from the policy, the lower of the two critics’ scores plus α times −log π, which is large when the policy spreads out. Averaged over a, −log π is the entropy H.
target = r + γ · V−⁠(s′)
Each critic learns toward this soft target, so a state that leaves room to explore scores higher. V− is a slowly updated copy of a value network trained toward line 2; CleanRL computes it straight from the two critics’ copies instead.
actor loss = E[α · log π(a |⁠ s)
− min(Q1, Q2)(s, a)]
The actor raises the critics’ score of the actions it samples while keeping log π low, that is, keeping its actions spread. Each action is the actor’s output for a fixed noise draw, so the gradient flows from Q back into the actor.

It is off-policy with replay. In the SAC paper its runs varied far less across random seeds than a version with a deterministic actor.

Every method here learns only from real steps. What if the agent also learned how the world responds, and practiced inside that model before acting? Model-Based RL: Dyna, AlphaZero and MuZero covers that family.
