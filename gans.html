Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)

One network draws, another judges.

Two networks are trained against each other. The generator turns random noise into an image. The discriminator looks at an image and outputs the chance that it is real. The discriminator gets better at telling real from generated, the generator gets better at producing images the discriminator accepts, and over training the generated images start to look real. No hand-written loss says what a good digit is, so the discriminator is the loss.

As in Generative Models, section 2, \(x\) is one image, \(p_{\text{data}}\) the distribution of real images and \(y\) what we ask for, such as a digit label.

1 Generator and discriminator

Builds on sampling from a Gaussian (Common Probability Distributions, section 5)

The noise \(z\) is a vector of random numbers drawn from a fixed distribution \(p_{z}\). The deep convolutional GAN (DCGAN) draws \(z\) uniformly. Common PyTorch code draws \(z\) from \(N(0, I)\), the standard Gaussian, independent numbers of mean 0 and variance 1, with torch.randn. Either works because the generator \(G\) learns to reshape whatever \(p_{z}\) gives it.

2 The discriminator is a binary classifier

Builds on binary cross-entropy (Cross-Entropy Loss, section 1) and the average over many draws (Probability and Bayes Rule, section 2)

Take a DCGAN discriminator on handwritten digits, shown one real 7 and one generated 7.

D wants this as high as possible, which means confident and right on both kinds of image.
\(\displaystyle V(D, G) = \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=real}{E_{x \sim p_{\text{data}}}[\log D(x)]}}\)
\(\displaystyle + \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=fake}{E_{z \sim p_{z}}[}}\)
\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=fake}{\log (1 - D(G(z))) ]}}\)
\(\log D(x)\) rewards calling real images real, so the first half grows when D trusts real data. \(\log(1 - D(G(z)))\) rewards calling generated images generated, so the second half grows when D rejects fakes. \(E_{x \sim p_{\text{data}}}\) is read “the average over real images” and \(E_{z \sim p_{z}}\) “the average over noise draws”, so \(V\) is D’s score over both kinds at once. \(D(x)\) is a probability, the sigmoid of the network’s last number, the logit (Logistic Regression, section 2).

This is the binary cross-entropy (BCE) of Cross-Entropy Loss, section 1, with real = 1, generated = 0 and the sign flipped.

3 The minimax objective

Builds on gradient descent (Gradient Descent, section 1)

D climbs the value and G descends it.
\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=fake}{\min_{G}}} \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=real}{\max_{D}}} V(D, G)\)
max over D is the inner contest, so for each G there is a best judge. min over G is the outer one, so G is graded by the best judge. G appears only in the second term of \(V\), so G can only lower \(V\) by making \(D(G(z))\) larger.

4 The optimal discriminator and Jensen-Shannon divergence

Builds on the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence (KL Divergence, section 2)

The best judge says “real” in proportion to how much more often real images land at \(x\) than generated ones.
\(\displaystyle D^{*}(x) = \frac{\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=real}{p_{\text{data}}(x)}}}{\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=real}{p_{\text{data}}(x)}} + \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=fake}{p_{g}(x)}}}\)
The numerator is the real density at \(x\). The denominator is both densities at \(x\), so \(D^{*}\) is the real share of all images that land at \(x\). Equal densities leave the judge guessing. \(p_{g}\) can be sampled, but its value at an image cannot be computed, so \(D^{*}\) is a picture of the ideal, not something training computes. \(D^{*}\) is the best D for the current G if D could be any function.
The generator, facing the best judge, is minimizing a distance between its images and real ones.
\(\displaystyle V = \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=real}{-\log 4}}\)
\(\displaystyle + \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=fake}{2\cdot\text{JS}(p_{\text{data}} \| p_{g})}}\)
This is \(V\) with \(D^{*}\) plugged in. \(-\log 4\) is \(V\) when the two match. \(2\cdot\text{JS}\) is the extra \(V\) the judge earns from any mismatch.
\(\displaystyle \text{JS}(p \| q) = \frac{1}{2}\cdot \operatorname{KL}(p \| m)\)
\(\displaystyle + \frac{1}{2}\cdot \operatorname{KL}(q \| m)\)
\(\displaystyle \text{with } m = \frac{p + q}{2}\)
\(p\) and \(q\) are any two distributions and \(m\) their half-and-half mix. \(\operatorname{KL}(p \| m)\) is read KL of \(p\) from \(m\), so JS averages how far each is from their middle.

This describes the minimax objective; in practice G trains on a different loss. Training GANs, section 3 shows where JS stops helping.

5 Why the generator uses a different loss in practice

Builds on the sigmoid and the logit (Logistic Regression, section 2)

Early in DCGAN training, D rejects the generator’s static easily.

This has the same goal, make D say “real”, but is steep exactly when G is losing badly.
\(\displaystyle L_{G} = -E_{z \sim p_{z}}[\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=fake}{\log D(G(z))}}]\)
\(L_{G}\) is the generator’s loss. The minus sign makes it a loss to minimize. \(\log D(G(z))\) is the log-chance D gives to the generated image, so G gains most where D is most sure the image is fake. A loss “saturates” when its curve goes flat, so its gradient goes to zero; the original second term saturates at D near 0 and this loss does not.

This loss replaces the second term of the value V for the G update only.

8 Conditional GANs

Builds on the conditional goal, the model’s images matching real ones of the asked label (Generative Models: What They Learn and How They Differ, section 2)

On handwritten digits conditioned on their label, feed the label \(y\) to both G and D as a one-hot vector, ten numbers with a 1 in the label’s place.

Row 3 is why G obeys \(y\), since D has only seen real images under “7” that look like 7s, so a 3 under that label is easy to reject.

The judge now asks whether this is a real image of the class it claims.
\(\displaystyle V(D, G) =\)
\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=real}{E_{x, y}[\log D(x | y)]}}\)
\(\displaystyle + \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=fake}{E_{z, y}[\log (1 - D(\tilde{x} | y))]}}\)
\(\displaystyle \text{with } \tilde{x} = G(z | y)\)
\(E_{x, y}\) averages over real images drawn with their labels, and \(E_{z, y}\) over noise draws and asked labels, so both terms are averaged over labels as well. \(D(x | y)\) scores an image together with its label, so real pairs get high scores. \(\tilde{x}\) is the image G draws for label \(y\) from noise \(z\).

This is the value V of Section 2 with \(y\) added to every D and G. Its goal is the conditional goal of Generative Models, section 2, and Guided Diffusion, section 1 gives the denoiser \(y\) in the same way.

9 GANs that make images

Builds on strided convolution (CNN Basics: Convolution, Stride and Pooling, section 2), downsampling (section 3), batch normalization (Normalization Layers, section 2.1) and activations (Multilayer Perceptrons (MLP), section 7)

DCGAN made GANs work on images. Its generator upsamples with transposed convolutions, which run a convolution backward: each input value stamps a copy of the kernel onto a larger output.

The first step projects and reshapes \(z\); each later step is one transposed convolution with kernel 4, stride 2, padding 1, a common modern choice. G ends in tanh, a squashing function whose outputs lie in [−1, 1], which is why images are scaled to [−1, 1].

10 A GAN training step in PyTorch

Builds on Adam (Adaptive Optimizers: AdaGrad, RMSProp and Adam, section 4)

Each training step is the loop of Section 6, run once per batch; it carries the two networks and their optimizers and costs several forward and backward passes. Training uses stochastic gradient descent plus momentum, a running average of past gradients; Adam arrives with DCGAN.

One training step of a tiny DCGAN, on a random stand-in batch for real digits.

def run(NOISE_DIM, CH, S, CH_HALF, KERNEL, STRIDE, PAD, SLOPE, LR, BETA1, BETA2, BATCH, IMG):  # pass your own sizes and settings
    import torch, torch.nn as nn
    G = nn.Sequential(  # noise to an image, growing by doubling
        nn.Linear(NOISE_DIM, CH * S * S), nn.Unflatten(1, (CH, S, S)),
        nn.ConvTranspose2d(CH, CH_HALF, KERNEL, STRIDE, PAD), nn.BatchNorm2d(CH_HALF), nn.ReLU(),
        nn.ConvTranspose2d(CH_HALF, 1, KERNEL, STRIDE, PAD), nn.Tanh())
    D = nn.Sequential(  # image to one logit
        nn.Conv2d(1, CH_HALF, KERNEL, STRIDE, PAD), nn.LeakyReLU(SLOPE),
        nn.Conv2d(CH_HALF, CH, KERNEL, STRIDE, PAD), nn.BatchNorm2d(CH), nn.LeakyReLU(SLOPE),
        nn.Flatten(), nn.Linear(CH * S * S, 1))
    opt_D, opt_G = [torch.optim.Adam(m.parameters(), lr=LR, betas=(BETA1, BETA2)) for m in (D, G)]
    bce, real = nn.BCEWithLogitsLoss(), torch.rand(BATCH, 1, IMG, IMG) * 2 - 1
    ones, zeros = torch.ones(BATCH, 1), torch.zeros(BATCH, 1)

    fake = G(torch.randn(BATCH, NOISE_DIM))
    loss_D = bce(D(real), ones) + bce(D(fake.detach()), zeros)
    opt_D.zero_grad(); loss_D.backward(); opt_D.step()

    loss_G = bce(D(G(torch.randn(BATCH, NOISE_DIM))), ones)  # fresh noise
    opt_G.zero_grad(); loss_G.backward(); opt_G.step()
  1. 1A tiny DCGAN for digits whose G projects and reshapes \(z\) with nn.Linear, then grows by doubling, the doubling of Figure 11, and whose D returns a logit; Adam for each network; the targets ones (real) and zeros (generated).
  2. 2D’s loss, real images against 1 and generated ones against 0, the value V with its sign flipped.
  3. 3The D step.
  4. 4G’s loss on fresh noise, the generator loss \(L_{G}\).
  5. 5The G step. Only G’s optimizer steps, so D’s weights stay fixed.

  • D returns a logit here, and BCEWithLogitsLoss applies the sigmoid inside the loss for numerical stability.
  • Detach the fakes for the D step, cutting them off from G so the D step trains D only and leaves G untouched.
  • The two losses do not fall like a classifier’s, so watch samples, not losses.
