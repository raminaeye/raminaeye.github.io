The classics that still run the world. How much can you learn without a single GPU?

  • Linear RegressionFitting a line by walking downhill. Gradient descent versus the closed-form answer, and what learning rate does to the walk.
  • Logistic RegressionA line that says yes or no. The sigmoid turns a score into a probability, and 0.5 draws the boundary.
  • Multilayer Perceptrons (MLP)When a line is not enough. One dial grows the model from a straight boundary to a smooth curve to a crazy overfit.
  • Decision Trees, Random Forests, and BoostingTwenty questions, then a forest. One tree memorizes, many vote, and boosting fits the last round's mistakes.
  • Support Vector Machines (SVM)Maximum margin. Hinge loss, support vectors, and the C knob that trades margin width against violations.
  • k-Nearest Neighbors (k-NN)Ask the neighbors. No training, just voting, and one slider that shows bias versus variance live.
  • Naive BayesClassifying by counting. Each word moves the class bars, and smoothing stops zero counts from zeroing everything.
  • k-Means ClusteringFind the clumps with no labels. Two steps repeated: points pick the nearest center, centers jump to the middle.
  • Principal Component Analysis (PCA)Faces from parts. The notebook's eigenfaces, rebuilt in your browser: one slider from ghost to person.
  • t-SNEPCA's nonlinear follow-up. Neighbor probabilities, the heavy-tailed t, and the perplexity knob that remakes the map.
  • Recommender SystemsPeople who liked this. Taste as geometry: same-taste users versus same-content movies, side by side.

No labels, no dataset, just a scoreboard. How does anything learn to win?

  • Reinforcement Learning (RL)Learning from rewards: the agent acts, the world answers with a score. Bananas included.
  • Deep Q-Networks (DQN)Teach an agent the value of every move, then it just picks the best one. Watch it stop eating blue bananas.
  • Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG)DQN picks buttons; DDPG turns dials. Continuous control with an actor that moves and a critic that judges.
  • Multi-Agent Reinforcement LearningTwo rackets, both learning at once. Your opponent never sits still.

Half the bits, same model. Which weights can you cut and get away with it?

  • QuantizationThe lazy version of quantization, and that is not a bad thing. Fewer bits, same model, almost free.
  • Neural Architecture Search (NAS)Let the machine design the model. The search space matters more than the search, so build it carefully.
  • Knowledge DistillationShrink a giant model into a small one without much performance loss. The teacher's soft labels do the teaching.
  • PruningCut the weights that do not matter and keep the accuracy. Sparsity is free compression if you do it right.

About

Currently: AI research scientist at Meta, working on multitask models for sensory data.

I studied at the University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign and started in audio ML, interning at several industries, including the Advanced Digital Sciences Center in Singapore and Adobe. I later joined Dolby Labs, working on deep learning and audio codecs. Later, as the first hire at CurieAI, I helped develop models that detect respiratory symptoms from noisy audio, scaling the technology to hundreds of patients.

Looking to push myself into unfamiliar territory, I pivoted to NLP. At UnitedHealth Group, I was again the first ML lead on the team. I built natural language search for health benefits that became the foundation for an enterprise-wide semantic search platform. As the ML lead for the enterprise team, I trained language models from scratch, scaled the team to over 50 people, and reshaped how ML was applied to search through my experiences with sensory signals.

At Precision Neuroscience, I applied cutting-edge signal processing and machine learning to brain-computer interfaces. Basically, translating brain signals into real-time actions. Currently, I work at Meta as an AI research scientist on multitask models for sensory data.
