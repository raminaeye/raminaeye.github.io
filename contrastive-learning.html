Contrastive Learning and the InfoNCE Loss

One loss that turns matching into a multiple choice question: score every candidate, keep the right one, and the bound on what the loss can show.

Before you start Views Linear probe Cross-Entropy Loss

Contrastive learning trains an encoder, the network that turns an image into a vector, its embedding. Every training step, each view in the batch answers one multiple-choice question: which candidate is its positive, the other view (randomly edited copy) of the same image? (Views are introduced in Self-Supervised Learning.) The loss rewards the right pick, which pulls each positive pair together and pushes other images apart. SimCLR does this with one big batch, and MoCo with a queue of past batches.

1 Positives pull, negatives push

In each batch, the two views of one image are a positive pair and every other image is a negative. The view asking the question is the anchor.

SimCLR answers this question with one big batch.

2 InfoNCE picks the true pair out of the candidates

SimCLR and MoCo both use this loss: each method fills the candidates differently, and the loss stays the same. \(N\) is the number of images in the batch.

InfoNCE is the loss behind the multiple-choice question. An anchor faces \(M\) candidates, its positive plus \(M - 1\) negatives. InfoNCE (named after noise-contrastive estimation, from Contrastive Predictive Coding, van den Oord et al. 2018) scores every candidate, turns the scores into probabilities and applies cross entropy, the standard loss for picking the right answer from a set, with the positive as the right answer.

Each view scores every candidate, the scores become probabilities, and training minimizes minus the log of the positive’s probability, averaged over every view.
\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=s}{\htmlStyle{color:#1f6fa8}{s_{i,k}}}} = \frac{\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=sim}{\htmlStyle{color:#1f6fa8}{\operatorname{sim}(z_{i}, z_{k})}}}}{\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=tau}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{\tau}}}}\)
The score \(s_{i,k}\) compares anchor view \(i\) with candidate view \(k\). Each embedding \(z\) has length 1, so sim is their cosine similarity: at its highest when they point the same way, zero at right angles, and at its lowest when they point opposite. The temperature \(\tau\) divides every score. A small \(\tau\) stretches the gaps between scores, and the next line turns stretched gaps into lopsided shares, so the closest wrong views take most of the push. A large \(\tau\) flattens the gaps, and every negative is pushed about equally. CPC scored with a learned matrix instead and had no \(\tau\).
\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=p}{\htmlStyle{color:#1a6b3c}{p_{i,j}}}} = \frac{\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=num}{\htmlStyle{color:#1a6b3c}{\exp(s_{i,j})}}}}{\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=den}{\htmlStyle{color:#c0392b}{\sum_{k\in C} \exp(s_{i,k})}}}}\)
\(p_{i,j}\) is the probability the model gives to view \(i\)’s positive \(j\). exp raises \(e\) (about 2.718) to each score, so every term is positive and a higher score gets an exponentially larger one. \(C\) is the set of view \(i\)’s \(M\) candidates (\(\in\) means in), and \(\sum\) adds the terms of all of them. Dividing the positive’s term by that sum makes the shares add to 1, so raising the positive’s share must lower every negative’s; that coupling is what pushes negatives away. The fraction is a softmax share; InfoNCE is minus its log, on the next line. In SimCLR, \(C\) is every view except \(i\), so \(M = 2N - 1\); SimCLR calls the loss NT-Xent.
\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=ell}{\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\ell_{i,j}}}} = -\log \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=p}{\htmlStyle{color:#1a6b3c}{p_{i,j}}}}\)
The loss \(\ell _{i,j}\) of view \(i\) is minus the log of its positive’s share. It is near 0 when the positive wins clearly and grows without bound as \(p_{i,j}\) falls toward 0, so the views the model gets most wrong pull hardest. It is cross entropy with the positive as the right class.
\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=L}{\htmlStyle{color:#a84300}{L}}} = \frac{1}{2N} \sum_{i=1}^{2N} \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=ell}{\htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\ell_{i,t(i)}}}}\)
Every view takes one turn as the anchor, so the loss \(L\) averages over all \(2N\) views. \(t(i)\) is the positive of view \(i\). Both views of a pair appear, so each pair is scored from both sides.

The snippet computes \(L\) for one toy batch, where z1 and z2 hold the unit-length embeddings of the same \(N\) images’ two views, row for row. \(D\) is the size of each embedding, the number of values in one \(z\).

def run(BATCH_SIZE, EMBED_DIM, TEMPERATURE):  # pass your own sizes and settings
    import torch
    import torch.nn.functional as F

    N, D, TAU = BATCH_SIZE, EMBED_DIM, TEMPERATURE
    z1 = F.normalize(torch.randn(N, D), dim=1)
    z2 = F.normalize(torch.randn(N, D), dim=1)

    z = torch.cat([z1, z2])                  # [2N, D]
    s = z @ z.T / TAU                        # [2N, 2N]
    is_self = torch.eye(2 * N, dtype=torch.bool)
    s = s.masked_fill(is_self, -torch.inf)   # never pick itself
    t = torch.arange(2 * N).roll(N)          # positive of view i
    L = F.cross_entropy(s, t)
  1. 1Unit-length embeddings \(z\) of the two views of one toy batch, Section 2 eq line 1.
  2. 2Scores \(s_{i,k}\) divided by \(\tau\), Section 2 eq line 1.
  3. 3Removes each view’s own score, leaving the set \(C\) of Section 2 eq line 2.
  4. 4Positive index \(t(i)\) and cross entropy averaged over all \(2N\) views, Section 2 eq lines 2 to 4.

3 More candidates raise how much a loss can show

More candidates make the question harder, so a low loss says more about how much the two views share. Guessing at random among \(M\) candidates gives the positive probability 1/\(M\), a loss of log \(M\); a perfect model drives it toward 0.

A low loss proves that the two views share information, up to a limit set by the number of candidates.
\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{I}(v_{1}; v_{2}) \ge \log \htmlStyle{color:#1f6fa8}{M} - \htmlStyle{color:#a84300}{L}\)
The mutual information \(I\) counts how much seeing view \(v_{1}\) tells you about its positive \(v_{2}\), in nats (information counted with the natural log); \(\ge\) means at least. The right side is a floor that rises as the loss \(L\) falls. \(L\) never falls below 0, so the floor can never pass log \(M\). So a loss can show at most log \(M\) nats of shared information, and only more candidates raise that bound, one reason large batches and queues help (CPC paper, sec. 2.3).

A loss stuck at log \(M\) means every candidate scores the same, the signature of collapse (every image gets the same embedding).

4 When the loss goes wrong

Three ways the loss fails, each with its cause, its symptom, and its fix.

  • Too few candidates. The ceiling log \(M\) stays low, so even a perfect loss says little. A loss stuck at log \(M\) means every candidate scores the same, the signature of collapse. Fix: raise the candidate count, with a bigger batch or a queue.
  • Temperature set wrong. Too low, and the push gathers on the closest negatives only. Too high, and every negative is pushed about equally, so the signal weakens. Fix: keep \(\tau\) near the paper default for the method using it.
  • No negatives at all. The loss has nothing to push against, so pulling alone collapses every image to one embedding. That is why BYOL, SimSiam, and DINO exist: each holds collapse off without negatives.

Compare the loss against log \(M\). A loss near log \(M\) says the candidates score alike, and a loss far below it says the positive wins clearly.
