Optimizers from SGD to Adam

  1. Optimizers from SGD to Adam
  2. Optimizers in Practice: AdamW, SAM, Schedules and Clipping

Each update rule fixes a failure of the one before it, from plain stochastic gradient descent (SGD) to Adam.

An optimizer turns each step's gradient into a weight update that lowers the training loss; the ones here differ in how they smooth that slope and how large a step each weight gets. Neural nets, unlike linear regression, have no closed-form answer, so they are trained step by step (Gradient Descent).

Each row fixes a failure of the row above.

The controls a training loop wraps around these rules are on Optimizers in Practice: AdamW, SAM, Schedules and Clipping.

Each acts once per training step on one minibatch's gradient and keeps its own buffers between steps (PyTorch's torch.optim documentation).

1 SGD and adaptive optimizers

1.1 Gradient descent and SGD

Stochastic gradient descent (SGD) steps against the gradient of one fresh random minibatch instead of the whole dataset (Robbins and Monro 1951): a noisier slope for one minibatch's forward and backward pass per step, with no state beyond the weights.

Plain SGD moves every weight by one shared step size times its minibatch slope.
θ ← θ − α·∇L(θ)
The weights θ move against the slope ∇L(θ), scaled by the learning rate α, exactly as in gradient descent. The slope is read on a random minibatch, so each step is a cheap, noisy estimate of the full slope, and the noise averages out over many steps. One α serves every weight, so a weight on a steep direction and one on a flat direction get the same multiplier, the weakness the rest of this page fixes.

Plain SGD on a three-weight linear model in NumPy; every weight in w (θ) moves by the same lr (α) times its own slope.

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
X = rng.normal(size=(256, 3))
y = X @ rng.normal(size=3)
w = np.zeros(3)
lr, bs = 0.1, 32  # α, batch
for i in range(0, 256, bs):
    xb = X[i:i + bs]
    yb = y[i:i + bs]
    err = xb @ w - yb
    grad = 2 * xb.T @ err / bs
    w = w - lr * grad
  1. 1Data, zero weights, α and the batch size.
  2. 2Takes the next minibatch of rows.
  3. 3Slope of the batch's mean squared error.
  4. 4Steps every weight by the same α.

Too large an α oscillates or diverges, too small converges slowly; recent versions of PyTorch's SGD default α to 0.001, older ones such as 2.1 require it, and the documentation example uses 0.1. A larger batch gives a less noisy slope but fewer steps per pass over the data: the whole set is plain gradient descent, and 1, the default of PyTorch's DataLoader, follows a single example. The noise is cheap and helps near a saddle point:

At a saddle point the gradient is zero, yet the point is not a minimum.
L(x, y) = x2 − y2
In this two-weight toy loss L, the squared x term rises on both sides, so along x the point is a minimum; the subtracted y term falls on both sides, so along y it is a maximum. Curvatures of opposite sign are what make a saddle.
∇L(0, 0) = (0, 0)
At the origin the slope is zero in both directions, so gradient descent started exactly there never moves, and started near it moves very slowly because the slope is tiny. Minibatch noise or momentum moves the weights off the saddle and down along y.

High-dimensional losses are full of saddles, and full-batch descent can sit near one for a long time.

1.2 SGD with momentum

Momentum damps SGD's oscillation with a velocity v, a running sum of past slopes kept per weight, a model-sized buffer for one extra multiply and add (PyTorch's momentum_buffer; Sutskever et al. 2013), written as in PyTorch's SGD:

Momentum builds a velocity from past slopes, then steps by it.
v ← μ·v + ∇L(θ)
The new velocity v is the old one shrunk by μ (mu), typically 0.9, plus today's slope. Slopes that keep pointing the same way pile up, so speed grows along a consistent direction, up to about ten single slopes at 0.9; slopes that flip sign from step to step cancel, so the oscillation dies down. A larger μ averages over more past slopes: smoother and faster on long slopes, slower to turn.
θ ← θ − α·v
The weights move by α times the velocity instead of the raw slope, so every step carries the memory of past slopes.

Momentum in NumPy on a quadratic loss 20 times steeper in one direction.

import numpy as np

# loss ½(x² + 20y²)
# its slope is curv·θ
curv = np.array([1.0, 20.0])
theta = np.array([-4.0, 1.0])
v = np.zeros_like(theta)
lr, mu = 0.04, 0.9  # α, μ
for t in range(100):
    grad = curv * theta
    v = mu * v + grad
    theta = theta - lr * v
  1. 1Loss, start, zero velocity, α and μ.
  2. 2Reads today's slope.
  3. 3Shrink v by μ, add the slope, the first momentum line.
  4. 4Steps by α times the velocity, the second momentum line.

At μ = 0 this is plain SGD; as μ nears 1 old slopes never fade, so the weights overshoot and swing around the minimum. PyTorch's SGD defaults to μ = 0; its documentation example uses 0.9. 1.3 Nesterov accelerated gradient (NAG)

NAG (Nesterov 1983) computes the gradient at the lookahead point, where the velocity is about to carry the weights, instead of at the current weights, so it sees an overshoot one step early and overshoots less; its state is the same one velocity per weight.

NAG is momentum with the slope read where the velocity is taking you.
v ← μ·v + ∇L(θ − α·μ·v)
The velocity update is the same, except the slope is read at the lookahead point the current velocity is about to carry the weights to. If that point has overshot, its slope already points back, so the velocity slows down one step earlier than plain momentum and swings less around a minimum.
θ ← θ − α·v
The step itself is the same as momentum; only where the slope was read changed. The momentum paper writes the velocity with α inside it and the opposite sign; the path is the same.

1.4 AdaGrad

The next optimizers give every weight its own step size: a weight tied to a rare input feature needs a large step when that feature appears, and one with a gradient every step needs small steps.

AdaGrad keeps, per weight, the running sum of its squared gradients, a model-sized buffer that only grows (Duchi et al. 2011).

AdaGrad divides each weight's step by that weight's own gradient history.
Gt = Gt−1 + gt2
Gt adds today's squared gradient gt to a running total for one weight. Squaring makes every gradient count as a positive size, so Gt measures how large that weight's gradients have been so far. It never shrinks.
θ ← θ − α·gt√Gt+ε
Each weight's step is divided by the square root of its own total, which brings the total back to gradient units. A weight that gets large gradients often builds a big total and takes small steps; a rarely updated weight keeps a small total and large steps. Because the total only grows, every step keeps shrinking, which can stall training late. ε is a tiny guard against dividing by zero.

α scales every step by one factor, so a larger α moves further before the growing total stalls it; ε matters only while a total is near zero, where a larger ε keeps the first steps smaller. PyTorch defaults to α = 0.01 and ε = 1e-10.

1.5 RMSProp

RMSProp swaps the sum for a decaying average per weight, again model-sized, so old gradients fade and the step follows the recent scale (Tieleman and Hinton 2012).

RMSProp lets old gradients decay, so the step tracks the recent scale.
Et = β2Et−1 + (1−β2)gt2
Et keeps the β2 share of the old average and takes the rest from today's squared gradient. Old gradients fade by a factor β2 each step, so at 0.9 the average reflects roughly the last ten steps; a β2 nearer 1 reacts more slowly to a change in gradient size, and β2 = 0 uses only today's gradient. PyTorch's RMSProp defaults to 0.99, with α = 0.01 and ε = 1e-8. Adam, in Section 1.6, reuses this average and this rate.
θ ← θ − α·gt√Et+ε
AdaGrad's step with Et in place of the growing sum. Because Et falls again when gradients get small, the step recovers instead of shrinking forever.

1.6 Adam (adaptive moment estimation)

Adam combines momentum with RMSProp's per-weight scale. It carries two averages per weight, m and v, plus a step counter t for the zero-start correction, twice the model's size in state (Kingma and Ba 2015).

Adam keeps two running averages: one smooths the slope, the other tracks each weight's own scale.
mt = β1mt−1+(1−β1)gt
The momentum mt keeps the β1 share of its old value and takes the rest from today's raw slope gt. A slope that flips sign cancels out while a steady one survives; at 0.9 the average spans about ten steps.
vt = β2vt−1+(1−β2)gt2
Unlike momentum's velocity v, vt averages the squared slope, with β2 near 0.999, so it spans about a thousand steps. Squaring drops the sign, so vt tracks how large each weight's gradients are, not which way they point.
Both averages start at zero, so early on they are scaled back up.
m̂t = mt1−β1t
The divisor is one minus the decay rate raised to the step count t. On the first step it is small, so mt is scaled up to a real slope size; as t grows the divisor heads to 1 and the correction disappears.
v̂t = vt1−β2t
The same correction for the squared average. It matters more here: with β2 at 0.999 the divisor stays far below 1 for hundreds of steps, and without it the early steps would be far too large.
Adam steps by the smoothed slope, scaled per weight.
θ ← θ − α·m̂t√v̂t+ε
The step is the corrected momentum divided by the root of the corrected squared average, times α. That ratio is the slope over its own typical size, so for a steady direction it sits near one whatever the units: steep directions are scaled down, flat ones up, and α alone sets the step length. When the slope is noisy the ratio shrinks, so Adam takes smaller steps where the slope keeps changing sign. ε guards the division; a larger ε caps the step where the squared average is tiny and pushes Adam toward plain momentum. Raising β1 toward 1 smooths the slope over more steps but turns more slowly, and β1 = 0 drops momentum; lowering β2 lets a single very large gradient shrink the step quickly. The Adam paper's defaults are α = 0.001, β1 = 0.9, β2 = 0.999 and ε = 1e-8.
read the slope smooth it track each axis's own scale fix the startup bias step

PyTorch's Adam keeps the two averages in opt.state, one entry per weight tensor; three steps on a tiny model show them.

import torch
net = torch.nn.Linear(3, 1)
ps = list(net.parameters())
opt = torch.optim.Adam(ps)
x = torch.randn(8, 3)
for t in range(3):
    opt.zero_grad()
    loss = net(x).pow(2).mean()
    loss.backward()
    opt.step()
s = opt.state[net.weight]
m = s['exp_avg']
v = s['exp_avg_sq']
  1. 1A tiny linear model, Adam and one batch.
  2. 2Three Adam steps on that batch.
  3. 3Reads the weight's state; exp_avg is mt and exp_avg_sq is vt, each the weight's shape, so Adam keeps two extra copies of the weights (Figure 8).

1.7 Nadam (Nesterov-accelerated Adam)

Nadam brings Nesterov's idea to Adam: it adds a larger share of the current slope to the smoothed momentum, so it reacts faster, without moving the weights to a lookahead point; its state is Adam's (Dozat 2016).

Nadam is Adam's step with an extra share of the current gradient.
m̄t = β1m̂t + (1−β1)gt1−β1t
m̄t blends the corrected momentum with a bias-corrected share of today's raw gradient gt. Mixing today's gradient in directly is Nesterov's lookahead in Adam form: the step points further toward where the momentum is heading instead of waiting a step for the average to catch up.
θ ← θ − α·m̄t√v̂t+ε
Adam's step with m̄t in place of m̂t; the per-weight pacing is unchanged. This follows the Nadam paper, simplified to a constant β1; the rest of the step is the one Figure 5 walks through.
