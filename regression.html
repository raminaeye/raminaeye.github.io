Linear Regression

Fitting a line, walking downhill.

You know me, I am going to start the classical machine learning tour with the humblest model of them all: a straight line. Linear regression. The "hello world" of learning from data, and I mean that as a compliment.

The line is the simplest machine that still learns

Here is the question I love: why does the oldest trick in machine learning still work? You collected all this data, and the best you can do is draw a line through it?

Here is the thing. A line is the simplest machine that can still learn. One weight per feature, one bias, and you are done. It trains in milliseconds. You can read its mind: every weight tells you exactly how much its feature matters. And the punchline: half of deep learning is lines stacked on lines with bends in between. Master the line, and the fancy stuff gets a lot less scary.

Every feature gets a vote

Forget the jargon for a second. Linear regression says: every feature gets a vote, the votes add up, and a bias shifts the total. That is the entire model.

the features are plain numbers (for a house: size, bedrooms, age); the weights, one per feature, say how loudly each feature votes (big positive weight: this feature pushes the prediction up); the bias, also called the intercept, is the prediction when every feature is zero, sliding the whole line up or down; and the hypothesis is a fancy word for "our prediction".

Every feature gets a vote, and this line is the whole model.
ŷ = w1x1 + w2x2 + b
Read it as a sentence: our prediction ŷ, the hypothesis, is each feature's vote added up, its value times its weight, then shifted by the bias b. The weights w1 and w2, one per feature, say how loudly each feature votes, and b, also called the intercept, is the prediction when every feature is zero.

The notebook I learned this from fits exactly this shape: two features, 300 samples, true weights 5 and 2 with bias 2.

Fit the line by minimizing the cost

A line has knobs, the weights and the bias. We need a score that says how wrong the current knob settings are. That score is the cost, written J in the tool below, and for regression it is the mean squared error:

The cost: one score for how wrong the current knob settings are.
J = 12MΣi (ŷi − yi)²
The cost J is half the average, over all M samples, of the squared residual: Σ (sigma) means sum over every sample i, and ŷi minus yi is how far off we are on sample i, prediction minus truth. Squaring makes big misses hurt more than small ones while the sign stops mattering, and averaging keeps one bad sample from running the show. The half, the 2 in 2M, is there so the slope comes out clean: squaring brings down a factor of 2 when you take the slope, and the half cancels it, which is why the gradient line divides by M alone.

The gradient points uphill; step the other way

Now the clever part. This cost is a bowl: convex, one bottom, no traps. To walk downhill you need the slope under your feet, which is the gradient: for each weight, the average over samples of (how wrong we are) times (the feature value).

If the residual is negative (we predicted too low) and the feature is positive, the gradient is negative, so stepping against it pushes the weight up. Makes sense: raise the weight, raise the prediction, shrink the miss.

The gradient: the slope under your feet, one per weight.
∂J∂wj = 1MΣi (ŷi − yi) xij
For each weight wj, the slope is the average over samples of (how wrong we are) times (the feature value): the fraction is the partial slope, ∂ (the partial symbol) J over ∂ wj, how J changes when only that one weight moves, and Σ (sigma) means sum over every sample i. ŷi minus yi is how wrong we are on sample i, and xij is that sample's value of feature j. The sign does the steering: a negative residual with a positive feature gives a negative slope, so stepping against it pushes the weight up and shrinks the miss.

Gradient descent: step downhill, repeat

That is the whole algorithm in one repeated step: take the gradient, step against it (downhill, not up) by a small step size, the learning rate, and update every knob at the same time. Repeat a few hundred times.

Gradient descent: step downhill, repeat.
wj ← wj − lr · ∂J∂wj
The arrow means gets replaced by: wj becomes itself minus lr, the learning rate, times the slope, the partial slope ∂J over ∂wj from the gradient line, so every knob steps downhill at the same time, and the same update runs for the bias, with its own gradient. The step size is the whole control: tiny steps crawl, oversized steps bounce off the rails.

Iterations are how long each walk runs: too few and the walk stops short, never arriving.

The closed form solves for the best weights directly

You do not have to walk. For squared error you can solve for the bottom of the bowl directly. That solve is the least squares method, named for what it finds: the weights whose squared misses add up to the smallest total.

Stack the whole dataset as a matrix, one row per sample, stack the targets alongside, and one matrix inverse teleports you to the answer. The notebook ran this on a one-feature line, with a whisper of regularization (a small penalty added to the cost that keeps the weights from growing wild, here just enough to nudge the answer slightly off the exact fit), and got 4.9999. Basically 5; the 0.0001 is the regularization's fault. On clean data it recovers the truth exactly: 5, 2, 2. One matrix solve lands on the exact weights at once, where gradient descent walks there step by step.

The closed form: the best weights in one solve, no walking.
w = (XTX)−1XTy
This line solves for the best weights directly: X is the whole dataset stacked as a matrix, one row per sample, with a column of ones carrying the bias, the raised T is the transpose, X with its rows and columns flipped, y is the targets stacked alongside, the factors join by multiplication where they sit side by side, so the first pair is X transposed times X and the inverse then multiplies X transposed times y, and the −1 is the one matrix inverse, so one solve teleports you to the answer instead of hundreds of steps. The solved w folds the bias in as one of its entries.

How to play

  • The point: Gradient descent walks the line onto the data one small step at a time, while the closed form teleports to the exact answer. Two routes, same destination.
  • Learning rate: The dashed line previews where the current settings would land, before anything moves.
  • Snap to closed form jumps straight to the solved weights.

Next: polynomials bend the line, the sigmoid squashes it

Polynomials: same trick, bent. Square your features and a line in the squared features is a curve in the originals. The notebook fits a quadratic surface with the exact same gradient descent code, because as far as the math is concerned, a squared feature is just another feature. Lines all the way down.

Classification needs a squashed line. A line predicts numbers, but yes-or-no needs a probability. So next we take this exact line, squash it through a curve that pins it between 0 and 1, and teach it to say yes or no. That is logistic regression.
