Diffusion Models: Adding Noise on a Schedule

Slowly turning an image into noise.

Start from a real image and add a little Gaussian noise many times until only noise is left. That direction needs no learning. A network then learns to undo one small step. To generate, start from pure noise and undo step after step. This is how Stable Diffusion and DALL-E 2 make images and DiffWave, a speech synthesizer, makes audio, the diffusion branch of the map on Generative Models. Where a generative adversarial network (GAN) trains two networks in a game (Training GANs, section 8), diffusion trains one network with a plain regression loss, and pays for it with many steps at sampling time. This page covers the noising direction and its schedule. Training a Diffusion Model learns to undo it, Faster Diffusion Sampling generates with fewer steps, and Guided and Latent Diffusion steers it with a label or a prompt.

1 The forward and reverse processes

Builds on why GANs hand over to diffusion (Training GANs: Mode Collapse, WGAN and the Move to Diffusion, section 8)

Noising and learning to denoise come from Sohl-Dickstein et al. (2015) and denoising diffusion probabilistic models (DDPM; Ho et al. 2020); score-based models (Song and Ermon 2019), which learn the gradient of the log of the data density, reach the same models (Faster Diffusion Sampling, section 2).

2 One noising step

Builds on the Gaussian and variance scaling (Common Probability Distributions, section 5) and independent noise per pixel (Common Probability Distributions, section 7)

Follow one bright pixel on the edge of the 7’s stroke, \(x_{0} = 0.8\), with images scaled to [−1, 1] as DDPM scales its data.

Shrink the pixel slightly, then add a little fresh noise. Line 1 says it as a distribution and line 2 as one draw.
\(\displaystyle q(x_{t} | x_{t-1}) =\)
\(\displaystyle N(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=real}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{\sqrt{1 - \beta _{t}}\cdot x_{t-1}}}}, \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=fake}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\beta _{t}\cdot I}}})\)
N(mean, variance) is the Gaussian, so \(q\) says where \(x_{t}\) can land. \(\sqrt{1 - \beta_{t}}\) is the shrink, the mean of that Gaussian. \(\beta _{t}\) is the variance of the noise added at step \(t\), and the list of all \(\beta _{t}\) is the schedule. \(I\) says every pixel gets its own independent noise. \(q\) is the fixed noising process with no weights. \(x_{t}\) is the pixel after step \(t\) and \(x_{t-1}\) the one before; the bar reads “given”. Scaling by \(\sqrt{1 - \beta}\) and adding variance \(\beta\) keeps a unit-spread pixel at spread 1 (the line under Figure 2), by the variance rules on Common Probability Distributions, section 5. Pixels in [−1, 1] have a spread below 1, and the shrink still pulls them toward N(0, 1).
\(\displaystyle x_{t} = \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=real}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{\sqrt{1 - \beta _{t}}\cdot x_{t-1}}}} + \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=fake}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\sqrt{\beta _{t}}\cdot \varepsilon}}}\)
\(\varepsilon\) is the noise that was added, one draw from N(0, \(I\)), so the noisy pixel is the shrunk pixel plus \(\sqrt{\beta_{t}} \cdot \varepsilon\), and \(\sqrt{\beta _{t}}\) is the noise’s standard deviation (the first row of Figure 2).
This is the only step defined by hand; every later formula on this page follows from it. The same “independent noise powers add” rule is on Spectral Subtraction for Denoising, section 1.

3 Jumping straight to any step

Builds on the sum of independent Gaussians (Common Probability Distributions, section 5)

Training needs \(x_{t}\) at a random \(t\) for each image, and walking the step of Section 2 one step at a time would take one sequential noise draw per step for each image; the jump takes one.

Step 1 of 4

Figure 6. One look-up and one mix give any step.

Pick step 500.
After \(t\) steps the pixel is a weighted mix of the original and one fresh noise draw, with weights fixed by the schedule.
\(\displaystyle \alpha _{t} = 1 - \beta _{t}\)
\(\displaystyle \bar\alpha_{t} = \alpha _{1}\cdot \alpha _{2}\cdot \dots \cdot \alpha _{t}\)
\(\alpha _{t}\) is the fraction of variance kept at step \(t\), so this line multiplies the kept fractions. \(\bar\alpha_{t}\), read “alpha-bar”, is their product up to \(t\), never a learning rate, so it shrinks toward 0 as \(t\) grows.
\(\displaystyle q(x_{t} | x_{0}) =\)
\(\displaystyle \begin{aligned} N(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=real}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t}}\cdot x_{0}}}}, \\ \quad \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=fake}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{(1 - \bar\alpha_{t})\cdot I}}}) \end{aligned}\)
\(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t}}\) is how much of the clean pixel survives. \(\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_{t}}\) is the noise weight. The two squared weights add to 1, so the pixel’s spread stays near 1.
\(\displaystyle x_{t} = \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=real}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t}}\cdot x_{0}}}} + \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=fake}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_{t}}\cdot \varepsilon}}}\)
Line 3 is line 2 written as a recipe: one clean pixel, one noise draw, one mix. At the last step, step \(T\) or 1000, almost none of the clean pixel survives and the noise weight is almost 1, so \(x_{T}\) is about N(0, \(I\)), as the last row of Figure 5 shows; sampling starts there (Faster Diffusion Sampling, section 1).
Line 2 is the noising step of Section 2 applied \(t\) times. One draw is enough because the sum of independent Gaussians is a Gaussian (the variance rules on Common Probability Distributions, section 5). Diffusion Training, section 1 uses line 3 to build its input in one shot.

4 Signal-to-noise ratio

Builds on the signal-to-noise ratio (The Wiener Filter, section 1)

The signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) falls across the rows of Figure 5, from well above 1 early to far below 1 late.

Above 1 the image dominates; below 1 the noise does.
\(\displaystyle \operatorname{SNR}(t) = \frac{\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=real}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{\bar\alpha_{t}}}}}{\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=fake}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{1 - \bar\alpha_{t}}}}}\)
The numerator \(\bar\alpha_{t}\) is the surviving signal’s variance factor and the denominator 1 − \(\bar\alpha_{t}\) the noise variance, both from the jump of Section 3. This is the schedule’s SNR: it treats the data as unit variance, so it is a property of the schedule, not of any one pixel.
It is the same signal-to-noise idea as the Wiener gain on The Wiener Filter, section 1, which turns down frequency bins where noise dominates.

5 Linear versus cosine schedules

The schedule is the list of \(\beta _{t}\) (DDPM’s “variance schedule”). DDPM uses \(T = 1000\), \(\beta\) rising linearly from 0.0001 to 0.02.

\(\bar\alpha\) falls slowly at both ends and steadily in the middle, so fewer steps are spent on images that are already pure noise.
\(\displaystyle \bar\alpha_{t} = \frac{f(t)}{f(0)}\)
\(\displaystyle f(t) = \cos^{2}(\frac{\frac{t}{T} + s}{1 + s}\cdot \frac{\pi }{2})\)
\(\displaystyle s = 0.008\)
\(f\) is the \(\cos^{2}\) curve. Dividing by \(f(0)\) makes \(\bar\alpha_{0}\), the value before any noise, equal 1. \(s\) is a small offset that keeps \(\beta\) from being tiny near \(t = 0\). \(t\) divided by \(T\) is the fraction of the way through, and the factor \(\frac{\pi}{2}\) turns it into a quarter turn, so \(\cos^{2}\) falls from 1 to 0.
\(\displaystyle \beta _{t} = 1 - \frac{\bar\alpha_{t}}{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\)
\(\displaystyle \text{clipped at } 0.999\)
Line 2 turns the \(\bar\alpha\) curve back into per-step \(\beta\), because \(\bar\alpha_{t}\) divided by \(\bar\alpha_{t-1}\) is \(\alpha _{t}\) (Section 3). \(\beta\) is capped at 0.999 so no step erases the whole pixel.
The cosine schedule replaces the linear \(\beta\) list of Section 2; the jump and the schedule’s SNR stay the same. The cosine schedule comes from Improved DDPM (Nichol and Dhariwal 2021).
At 64x64 and 32x32 the linear schedule leaves the last quarter of steps “almost purely noise”, while it “worked well for high resolution images”, in Improved DDPM’s words.

6 The forward process in code

The schedule is computed once before training and never learned. At each training step one step number per image is drawn uniformly, and that image’s noisy version is made in one shot by the jump; nothing else is carried, and the cost is one multiply-add per pixel. The schedule and the jump in PyTorch, on a random stand-in batch:

import torch

x0 = torch.rand(8, 1, 28, 28) * 2 - 1  # stand-in digits in [-1, 1]

betas = torch.linspace(1e-4, 0.02, 1000)
alphas_cumprod = torch.cumprod(1 - betas, dim=0)

def q_sample(x0, t, noise):
    a = alphas_cumprod[t].view(-1, 1, 1, 1)  # one value per image
    return a.sqrt() * x0 + (1 - a).sqrt() * noise

t = torch.randint(0, 1000, (8,))  # code counts steps from 0
xt = q_sample(x0, t, torch.randn_like(x0))
  1. 1Eight stand-in 28x28 images in [−1, 1].
  2. 2The linear schedule of the noising step (Section 2); the diffusers library’s DDPMScheduler takes the same settings as beta_schedule="linear".
  3. 3Line 1 of the jump (Section 3), all 1000 values at once.
  4. 4The jump’s recipe, one random step per image; the code counts steps from 0, the paper from 1.
  • Scale images to [−1, 1] first.
  • Keep the same betas for sampling.
  • If noise is drawn with torch.normal, its second argument is the standard deviation, not the variance, so pass \(\sqrt{\beta}\), never \(\beta\) (the trap is on Common Probability Distributions, section 9).
