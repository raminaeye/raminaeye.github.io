1 What Adam fixes

Momentum smooths the direction of each step but still uses one step size for every weight (SGD, Momentum and Nesterov). RMSProp (Tieleman and Hinton 2012) gives every weight its own step size but has no momentum, and its first steps are too long because its average starts at zero. Adam, from Kingma and Ba (2015), keeps both averages and fixes the zero start of each.

It became the default optimizer of deep learning. Transformers were trained with it from the start (Vaswani and others 2017), and its decoupled form, AdamW, trains large language models such as LLaMA. Diffusion models and most speech and vision models use it or AdamW too.

2 The whole step first

Before the parts, the step Adam takes. Everything after this section explains one piece of it.

Adam’s whole step
\({\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\theta_t} = \htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\theta_{t-1}} - \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{\alpha}\,\frac{\htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{\hat m_t}}{\sqrt{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\hat v_t}} + \epsilon}}\)
Each weight moves by the learning rate \(\alpha\) times a ratio. The top, \(\hat m_t\), is the recent average gradient: which way this weight has been pushed lately, with momentum’s smoothing. The bottom, \(\sqrt{\hat v_t}\), is the recent typical gradient size, as in RMSProp; \(\epsilon\) is a tiny constant that keeps the bottom from reaching zero. The ratio is about one when the pushes agree and smaller when they point different ways, so a weight’s step is about \(\alpha\) long whatever its gradient’s size. The hats mark averages corrected for their zero start; the next sections build each piece.

3 Two running averages

Adam keeps two exponential moving averages per weight, one of the gradient and one of its square.

Adam’s two moving averages
\({\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{m_t} = \beta_1\,\htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{m_{t-1}} + (1-\beta_1)\,\htmlStyle{color:#c0392b}{g_t}}\)
The first moment \(m_t\) is the moving average of the gradient (the average from RMSProp, section 2): the recent average direction. It is momentum’s velocity rescaled into an average (SGD, Momentum and Nesterov), so noise that flips sign from batch to batch cancels out of it.
\({\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{v_t} = \beta_2\,\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{v_{t-1}} + (1-\beta_2)\,\htmlStyle{color:#c0392b}{g_t^{2}}}\)
The second moment \(v_t\) is the moving average of the squared gradient: the recent typical size. This is RMSProp’s \(E\) renamed; it is not momentum’s velocity, even though both are often written \(v\).

4 How far back each average looks

Each beta sets its average’s window, about \(\frac{1}{1-\beta}\) steps (RMSProp, section 3 draws it). The direction average looks back a short way, so it can turn; the size average looks back much further, so step sizes stay steady.

Both betas must stay below one: at one an average never updates and its correction divides by zero.
knobraise itlower itdefault

5 Correcting the zero start

Both averages start at zero, so early on they are too small. Adam divides each by the weight it has collected so far.

Bias correction: divide each average by the weight it has collected
\({\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{\hat m_t} = \frac{\htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{m_t}}{1-\beta_1^{\,t}}}\)
An average that starts at zero has collected only the weight \(1-\beta_1^{\,t}\) after \(t\) steps; the rest is still on the zero. Dividing by that weight turns \(m_t\) into a true average of the gradients seen so far. The factor nears one within a few windows of \(\beta_1\), so the fix fades on its own.
\({\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\hat v_t} = \frac{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{v_t}}{1-\beta_2^{\,t}}}\)
The same fix for the size. \(\beta_2\) is much closer to one, so \(1-\beta_2^{\,t}\) grows much more slowly and \(v\) needs its fix far longer than \(m\). Correcting both keeps the ratio in the step line honest from the very first step; correcting only the top would make the early steps far too long.

To watch Adam without the correction, switch it off in the tool of section 7: the early steps come out too long, grow for a while, and settle only slowly.

Divide direction by size and step.

The same step in code, one line per card line. In a multitask model the per-weight division is also why a balancer that rescales one task’s loss sees less effect than it would under SGD (Gradient Balancing Inside the Training Loop, section 7, and the tilt in section 7.2).

import numpy as np
def adam_step(theta, g, m, v, t, lr=1e-3, b1=0.9, b2=0.999, eps=1e-8):
    m = b1 * m + (1 - b1) * g          # direction average
    v = b2 * v + (1 - b2) * g * g      # size average
    m_hat = m / (1 - b1 ** t)          # zero-start fixes
    v_hat = v / (1 - b2 ** t)
    theta = theta - lr * m_hat / (np.sqrt(v_hat) + eps)
    return theta, m, v

8 Why the gradient’s scale drops out, and where it stops

Figure 3 showed equal steps for different gradient sizes. The first line of the card says why; the second says where it stops.

Why Adam ignores the gradient’s scale, and where that stops
\({\displaystyle \frac{c\,\htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{\hat m}}{\sqrt{c^{2}\,\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\hat v}}} = \frac{\htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{\hat m}}{\sqrt{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\hat v}}}\qquad (c>0)}\)
Multiply every gradient by a positive number \(c\): the direction average grows by \(c\) and the size average by \(c^2\), whose root is \(c\) again, so the ratio is unchanged. This is what Kingma and Ba mean by “invariant to diagonal rescaling of the gradients”: summing a loss instead of averaging it scales every gradient by one common factor and changes nothing in Adam’s step, while SGD’s step would scale by \(c\). A balancer that scales all task losses together drops out the same way. Scaling one task’s loss is different: it changes the mix inside each shared weight’s gradient, which is not a common factor, so Adam’s step turns with it (Gradient Balancing Inside the Training Loop, section 7.1, and the tilt in section 7.2). The line ignores \(\epsilon\).
\({\displaystyle \frac{\text{step}}{\htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{\alpha}} = \frac{\lvert \htmlStyle{color:#c0392b}{g}\rvert}{\lvert \htmlStyle{color:#c0392b}{g}\rvert + \epsilon}}\)
For a steady gradient the ratio is \(\lvert g\rvert\) over \(\lvert g\rvert+\epsilon\). When the gradient is far above \(\epsilon\) it is one, a full step. When the gradient is as small as \(\epsilon\) the step halves, and below that it shrinks with the gradient, as SGD’s would. Only weights whose gradients are nearly zero, such as a rarely seen token’s row, feel this.

Invariance also holds only for a steady scale. If the gradient size changes, the size average needs about one \(\beta_2\) window to catch up, and steps are off by the change until it does; RMSProp’s version of this is the tool in RMSProp, section 6.

9 What momentum carries after the gradient changes

When the gradient changes, the two averages react at different speeds.

What the averages do when the gradient stops
\({\displaystyle \begin{aligned} \htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{m_{t+k}} &= \beta_1^{\,k}\,\htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{m_t} \\ \htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{v_{t+k}} &= \beta_2^{\,k}\,\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{v_t} \end{aligned}}\)
If the gradient drops to nothing for \(k\) steps, each average just keeps its share of itself every step. The direction average fades within about one \(\beta_1\) window; the size average, with its longer memory, has barely moved. Their ratio fades like \(\hat m\), so Adam coasts a short way in the old direction and stops, much like momentum.

After a turn rather than a stop, the long size memory still holds the old axis’s large gradients, so the divide shrinks the old direction and boosts the new one; a multitask run where one task’s gradient changes direction shows this (Gradient Balancing Inside the Training Loop, section 6.1).

10 Nadam

Nadam: the momentum the next step will use
\({\displaystyle \begin{aligned} \htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{\bar m_t} &= \frac{(1-\beta_1)\,\htmlStyle{color:#c0392b}{g_t}}{1-\beta_1^{\,t}} \\ &\quad + \frac{\beta_1\,\htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{m_t}}{1-\beta_1^{\,t+1}} \end{aligned}}\)
Nadam (Dozat 2016) adds the Nesterov look-ahead (SGD, Momentum and Nesterov) to Adam. Adam’s next average will be the share \(\beta_1\) of today’s average plus the share \(1-\beta_1\) of today’s gradient; Nadam uses that next average now, each part corrected for the zero start it will have. So a change of direction shows up in the step one step sooner.
\({\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\theta_t} = \htmlStyle{color:#2c3e50}{\theta_{t-1}} - \htmlStyle{color:#7d3c98}{\alpha}\,\frac{\htmlStyle{color:#1f618d}{\bar m_t}}{\sqrt{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{\hat v_t}} + \epsilon}}\)
The step is Adam’s with \(\bar m_t\) on top. The size average is unchanged.

PyTorch ships it as torch.optim.NAdam, which also warms the momentum weight up over the first tens of thousands of steps.

11 When to use it

Adam is the default for most deep networks: transformers, diffusion models, speech and vision models, and any model whose weights get gradients of very different sizes, such as an embedding table next to dense layers. Its defaults need little tuning. When the model is regularized with weight decay, as large language models are, use AdamW instead. On some image classifiers, such as ResNets, SGD with momentum is still common and can generalize a little better (Wilson and others 2017), and it keeps half the optimizer state.

12 When it runs and what it carries

Adam runs once per training step, after backward(). Per weight tensor it carries two tensors the size of the weights, the direction average \(m\) and the size average \(v\), plus the step count \(t\) that the corrections need. That is twice the model’s size in extra memory, a real cost for large models; opt.state_dict() must be saved with the model, or a resumed run restarts both averages at zero and repeats the corrected start.

13 Adam in PyTorch

import torch
model = torch.nn.Linear(16, 4)
opt = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=1e-3, betas=(0.9, 0.999), eps=1e-8)
loss = model(torch.randn(8, 16)).pow(2).mean()
opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
s = opt.state[model.weight]
m, v, t = s["exp_avg"], s["exp_avg_sq"], s["step"]
nadam = torch.optim.NAdam(model.parameters(), lr=2e-3)

14 Failure modes and tips

Most Adam trouble traces back to \(\epsilon\), \(\beta_2\), the bias correction or the decay.
symptomcausefix
some weights never movetheir gradients are far below \(\epsilon\)lower eps, or rescale the loss
huge first steps and an early loss spikebias correction missing in a hand-written Adamdivide each average by \(1-\beta^{t}\)
noisy, jumpy steps\(\beta_2\) lowered too farraise \(\beta_2\) back toward its default
steps tiny long after the loss settledthe size average still remembers large old gradientswait one window, or lower \(\beta_2\)
regularization weaker than expected on large weightsL2 added to the loss passes through Adam’s divideuse AdamW’s decoupled decay
sudden loss spikes late in a long large-model runthe size average lags a sudden gradient change with \(\beta_2\) near onelower \(\beta_2\) (LLaMA used 0.95), and clip gradients (Gradient Clipping)
