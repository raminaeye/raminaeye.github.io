Gradient Conflicts in Multitask Learning

The three ways joint training breaks: scale, speed, and direction.

On this page

The naive recipe Try it: the naive sum Three diseases The math kit The map

The obvious recipe is linear scalarization: add the losses together with some weights, and run ordinary gradient descent on the sum.

It is the first thing everyone tries, and it looks completely reasonable. One model, K tasks, K loss numbers. Gradient descent needs a single number to minimize, so fold the K scores into one the cheapest way possible: a weighted sum. The weights are volume knobs, one per task, and the sum is the whole training objective.

But the sum carries a hidden promise. A step that lowers the total can secretly raise one of the terms, as long as the others fall by more. Nothing in the recipe forbids robbing one task to pay another. The weights are the only control over how much say each task gets in that bargain, and as you will see, they are a weak control.

The naive recipe, written down

Here it is as an equation, then symbol by symbol, then read aloud with real numbers.

$$L(\theta) = \sum_i w_i L_i(\theta) \qquad \theta \leftarrow \theta - \alpha \nabla L(\theta)$$

Symbol by symbol. \(\theta\) ("theta") is all the model's adjustable knobs packed into one list. \(L_i(\theta)\) is task \(i\)'s loss, one number saying how badly the model is doing on that task, where lower is better. \(w_i\) is a volume knob for task \(i\): \(w_i = 2\) makes that task count double. The big \(\sum\) just means "add up tasks 1 through K". \(\alpha\) ("alpha") is the step size, a small number like 0.001. The arrow \(\leftarrow\) means "replace with": take the current knobs, step downhill on the total loss, and that becomes the new knobs.

Read it aloud with real numbers, one computation per line. Two tasks: a depth loss of 600 and a classification loss of 0.4, both weights at 1.

  1. \(w_1 \cdot L_1 = 1 \times 600 = 600\)
  2. \(w_2 \cdot L_2 = 1 \times 0.4 = 0.4\)
  3. \(L = 600 + 0.4 = 600.4\)
  4. task 1's share: \(600 / 600.4 = 0.9993\), about 99.9%
  5. for task 2 to reach half the sum: \(w_2 = 600 / 0.4 = 1500\)

The sum listens to task 1 almost exclusively. Task 2 is a rounding error. That is not a bug in the arithmetic; it is the naive recipe working exactly as written. And the fix is not obvious: to give task 2 an equal vote you must crank its weight into the thousands, and the right number changes as training proceeds, because the losses themselves move.

Three diseases, one symptom

With equal weights, "train on the sum" is what most codebases do first. Sometimes it works. Often it quietly fails: one task stalls far behind what it could do alone, or all of them get worse. When the joint model loses to separate single-task models, researchers call it negative transfer. The failure is usually diagnosed from a loss curve, but the loss curve hides which of three different diseases you actually have. They look alike from the outside and demand different medicine.

Scale: the loud task wins every vote

A depth loss near \(10^3\) and a classification loss near 1 are just numbers to gradient descent. The task with big numbers produces the big gradient, which swamps the others. Hand-tuning weights means searching K knobs, and doing it again every time the task set changes.

The scale panel above starts level: at 1x the two bars match. Press Play and stage 2 sweeps the slider up: by 300x task 2 owns 99.7% of every shared update, and task 1's bar is a sliver you can barely see. Nothing about task 2 is more important here. It is just louder.

Speed: the fair deal expires

One task may be nearly solved in the first 10% of training while another improves until the end. The fast task's gradients shrink as it finishes, so the weighting that was fair at step 100 is unfair at step 10,000. The balance must move with training.

The speed panel starts at step 2 with both tasks near full strength. Press Play and stage 3 sweeps the marker across training: passing step 30, the fast task's gradient has shrunk to 19% of its start while the slow task still has 72%. No fixed pair of weights is right at both step 2 and step 100, which is why a weighting you tuned early in a run can be wrong by the end of it.

Direction: the arrows fight

Two gradients can point against each other (cosine < 0), so the average update contains a piece that actively raises one task's loss. Task gradients fight on the large majority of training steps, and a whisper turned up is still pointing the wrong way.

The direction panel starts at 0 degrees with the arrows in full agreement. Press Play and stage 4 rotates task 2's gradient: near 100 degrees the cosine turns negative and the badge turns amber, the arrows are fighting. Keep rotating to 180 degrees: the two gradients point opposite ways, the cosine reads -0.99, and the amber sum arrow pushes task 2 uphill, which is exactly what its negative score means. This is the disease no volume knob can fix. Scaling a gradient changes its length, never its direction.

The math kit: five ideas, four pictures

All of this was geometry: arrows with sizes and directions, added together, sometimes fighting. The conflict is geometry, and the fixes are geometry too. Every method in the rest of this series does one of three things to gradient arrows: it measures how loud they are, checks how much they agree, or cuts out the part that fights. So the whole math kit is five ideas, and each one is simple once seen the right way.

A vector is just an arrow on a map: (3, 1) means walk 3 east, 1 north. A model's parameters \(\theta\) are a vector too, one with millions of entries; I cannot draw that map, but every fact about flat 2-D arrows stays true in it, which is why every picture here gets away with flat arrows. The norm is the arrow's length

The name: the norm, written \(\lVert v \rVert\). The plain idea: it is the arrow's length. The recipe: square every entry, add them up, take the square root. That is Pythagoras wearing a hat. For the arrow in panel (a):

$${\lVert (3,1) \rVert} = \sqrt{3^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{10} \approx 3.16$$

The payoff for this series: whenever I call a task's gradient "loud", I mean exactly this number is large. Loudness is a norm. Drag v in panel (a) to a short stub and a long spear and watch the number track the length.

The dot product is an agreement meter

The name: the dot product, written \(\langle u, v \rangle\). The plain idea: an agreement meter. The recipe: multiply matching entries and add:

$$\langle (3,1),\, (-1,2) \rangle = 3\cdot(-1) + 1\cdot 2 = -3 + 2 = -1$$

The meter also equals the two lengths times the cosine of the angle between the arrows, so its sign is the verdict: positive means roughly the same direction, zero means exactly perpendicular, negative means they partly oppose each other:

$$\langle u,v \rangle = {\lVert u \rVert}{\lVert v \rVert}\cdot\cos\theta \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \cos\angle(u,v) = \frac{\langle u,v \rangle}{{\lVert u \rVert}{\lVert v \rVert}}$$

Dividing by the lengths gives the cosine similarity, a scale-free agreement score between -1 and 1. Scale-free because a loud arrow and a quiet arrow pointing the same way score the same. In panel (b), both pulls are draggable: this sign check is the exact test PCGrad runs on every pair of task gradients, and a negative result is what sets off its surgery.

Projection is casting a shadow

The name: the projection of g1 onto g2. The plain idea: the shadow g1 casts on g2's line. The recipe: take the agreement score, divide by g2's squared length, and scale g2 itself by that:

$${\text{proj}_{g_2}(g_1)} = \left( \frac{\langle g_1,g_2 \rangle}{{\lVert g_2 \rVert}^2} \right) \cdot g_2$$

For the pair in panel (c), g1 = (3, 1) and g2 = (-1, 2), one computation per line:

  1. \(\langle g_1, g_2 \rangle = 3\cdot(-1) + 1\cdot 2 = -3 + 2 = -1\)
  2. \(\lVert g_2 \rVert^2 = (-1)^2 + 2^2 = 1 + 4 = 5\)
  3. \(t = -1 / 5 = -0.2\)
  4. shadow = \(-0.2 \cdot (-1, 2) = (0.2, -0.4)\)
  5. leftover = \((3, 1) - (0.2, -0.4) = (2.8, 1.4)\)
  6. dot check: \(2.8 \cdot (-1) + 1.4 \cdot 2 = -2.8 + 2.8 = 0\)

Subtracting the shadow from g1 leaves the part perpendicular to g2, and the dot check reading 0 in panel (c) is the proof: perpendicular means dot product zero. When the dot product is negative, the shadow points against g2, so the leftover is g1 with its anti-g2 part removed. This one subtraction is all of PCGrad: cut the shadow, keep the rest.

For the curious: the shadow lies on g2's line, so it must be some scaled copy \(t \cdot g_2\). A shadow meets the line at a right angle, and "right angle" means dot product zero: \(\langle g_1 - t\cdot g_2, g_2 \rangle = 0\). Expand, solve for \(t\), and the formula pops out.

A gradient is a pull

The name: the gradient, written \(\nabla L(\theta)\). The plain idea: the arrow pointing straight uphill from where I stand on the loss landscape, with length equal to the steepness. Panel (d) draws the hill \(L = x^2 + 2y^2\) as contours; its pull is \(\nabla L = (2x, 4y)\), which always points across the contours.

I treat each task's gradient as a pull on the shared parameters: direction \(\nabla L\), strength \(\lVert \nabla L \rVert\). Training follows the opposite pull, \(-\nabla L\), straight downhill. Each task has its own landscape and therefore its own pull, and those pulls need not point the same way. That sentence is the seed of every problem in this series.

Drag the dot in panel (d) toward the center and watch the red arrow shrink: flatter ground, weaker pull. Drag it outward and the pull grows.

A convex blend is a way to split 100%

The name: a convex combination. The plain idea: a blend where every weight is at least zero and the weights add to one, so each task gets a percentage of the mix. The set of all legal weight lists is the simplex, written \(\Delta\):

$$w_1 g_1 + w_2 g_2 + \cdots + w_K g_K, \quad w_i \ge 0, \quad \sum_i w_i = 1$$

Two more bits of vocabulary I will use everywhere: \(\min_x f(x)\) is the lowest value f can reach; \(\text{argmin}_x f(x)\) is the setting of x that reaches it: the best knob position, not the best score.

A worked blend with real numbers, g1 = (2, 0), g2 = (-1, 1.6), weights (0.5, 0.5):

  1. \(0.5 \cdot (2, 0) = (1.0, 0)\)
  2. \(0.5 \cdot (-1, 1.6) = (-0.5, 0.8)\)
  3. sum = \((1.0 - 0.5, 0 + 0.8) = (0.5, 0.8)\)

Why blends matter so much: blending is the cheapest way for tasks to compromise, and one fact pays off twice later: a blend can never do worse than its worst ingredient. If every task's pull likes a step, every blend likes it too. So "search over all blends" often turns out to be the same problem as "protect the worst task", only much cheaper to solve. On the next page I will search over these blends for the shortest arrow I am legally allowed to make.

The map for the rest of the series

GradNorm fixes scale and speed: it learns the weights \(w_i\) so every task is equally loud and nobody falls behind. PCGrad and CAGrad fix direction: when task gradients fight, they reshape the update itself. The two families work on different problems, so you can combine them. The comparison page lines them all up side by side on the same landscape.

All of this was geometry: arrows with sizes and directions, added together, sometimes fighting. The conflict is geometry, and now the moving parts are named: loudness, agreement, and shadows. The next question is what to do when the arrows fight, and the answer has a name, the Pareto front.
