1 Why the scoring network rates moves too high

Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG) trains one network to output a robot arm's turning forces and a second to score them, and the scorer often rates some of them too high, so the first network chases those errors. On a real arm, a torque picked from a score that is too high is a wasted or harmful move.

Twin Delayed DDPG (TD3) is DDPG with three changes that curb this. Each change tackles one way those errors grow.

The agent is the learner that picks the moves. (RL Basics) A state is the agent's situation right now. A move, or action, is one choice the agent can make in a state. A reward is the number the world hands back after each move, good or bad. (RL Basics) On a robot, sensing, moving and scoring repeat many times every second.

The running example is the Reacher task: a robot arm with two joints whose actions are four torques. A torque is a turning force a motor applies at a joint. On the Reacher each torque is a number from -1 to 1. Each joint can turn about two axes, so each joint gets two torques. Every step the arm senses its state, numbers describing the position, rotation and speed of its parts, and earns a small reward whenever its hand is inside a moving goal. (Continuous-Action RL) So the hand must follow the goal as it moves, not just reach it once.

Figure 1 shows the Reacher arm TD3 trains: a shoulder joint and an elbow joint, each turning about two axes, so the arm takes four torques. The hand sits near a dashed goal circle that moves, so the hand must follow the goal, not just reach it once. Take one step: the arm reads its pose and speed, sends four torques, and earns a small reward when its hand is inside the goal.

The return is all the reward still to come, with later rewards counted a little less. (RL Basics) A move's value \(Q(s,a)\), also called its score, is the return the agent expects if it makes move \(a\) in state \(s\) and plays on from there. (RL Basics) DDPG, which TD3 changes, learns two networks.

The deterministic actor \(\mu_\theta\) is a network with weights \(\theta\) that outputs one action every time, with no random draw. The critic \(Q_\phi\) is a network with weights \(\phi\) that scores a state and an action together; its score stands for the return the agent expects. (Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG): Actor and Critic) On the Reacher nothing stops the critic scoring some of the arm's torques too high.

1.1 The actor climbs into the critic's mistakes

The DDPG actor update moves the actor's weights so its torques shift the way the critic's score rises. (Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG): Actor and Critic) That works only as well as the critic's scores. Value methods take a max, the highest of the scores their network gives the possible next moves, when they learn.

Overestimation means the values climb above the return the agent really earns, because a max over noisy guesses picks lucky ones. (Double DQN) In DDPG the actor plays the role of the max, since its actor update pushes the torques toward wherever the critic is highest, including where it is wrong.

Figure 2 plots the critic's estimate against the true value over time. The true value stays near its level while the critic's estimate climbs above it and stays there, so the actor chases scores that were never real. Take one late step in training: the critic's estimate sits well above the true value, and the actor's torques follow the inflated score.

1.2 Why the value methods' fix is not enough

The critic learns by being pulled toward a target, the step's reward plus its own score for what comes next, counted a little less. (Continuous-Action RL) That target moves whenever the critic moves. The Deep Q-Network (DQN) scores its moves with one network, its Q-network. DQN had the same problem of a target that moves, and fixed it with a second network. On the arm, a target that moves would let the critic's scores chase their own changes.

The target network is a slow copy of the Q-network, with weights \(\phi'\), that computes the targets. (Deep Q-Networks (DQN)) Slow means the copy, written with a prime on its weights, is updated only a little at a time. In this family the critic plays the Q-network's part. (Training DDPG: Target Networks, Replay and Noise) This holds the target still while the critic learns.

In DDPG both the actor and the critic have a slow copy, marked by a prime on the weights, and the targets are built from those copies. (Training DDPG: Target Networks, Replay and Noise) The live networks are the actor and critic being trained, with weights \(\theta\) and \(\phi\). (Training DDPG: Target Networks, Replay and Noise) This holds the target still while the critic learns. TD3 keeps these slow copies just as DDPG does.

For value methods, overestimation already has a known fix. Double DQN is a fix for DQN that uses two networks inside the target; it has only one live network, the Q-network. Double DQN's target lets the live network pick the next move and the target network score it. (Double DQN) TD3 borrows this check of one network's score against another's, so one lucky high score gets caught.

In DDPG the same trick would let the live actor pick the next torques and a slow critic score them, but the slow copies stay close to the live networks, so pick and score come from nearly the same opinion, and the score stays too high.

1.3 Errors pile up through the target

Bootstrapping means putting your own current guess for the next state inside the target; each target is built from the critic's own next score, so an error at one step is carried into the next. (Actor-Critic: A2C and A3C)

Figure 3 chains three value boxes, each feeding the next target. The error bar under each box grows from box to box: a small error, carried forward, then grown larger. Take the first box: its small error feeds the second target, so the second box inherits it and grows it.

That chain is why a single fix is not enough. A lower score alone would still feed the next target, and a slower actor alone would still climb an inflated critic. TD3 cuts the chain in three places at once.

2 Twin critics, a smoothed target and delayed actor updates

Figure 4 stacks DDPG's update as five boxes, with the three TD3 changes highlighted. Random past steps feed the target, which feeds the critic, which feeds the actor, which feeds the slow copies. Take the target box: a small nudge on the next torques smooths what the critics score, so no narrow spike sets the target.

TD3 makes three changes to DDPG's update, one for each way the errors grow:

  1. A policy is the agent's rule for choosing a move in each state; it may always pick the same move or pick by chance. (RL Basics)
  2. Here the policy is the actor.
  3. The first change, target policy smoothing, adds a small clipped random nudge to the next torques inside the target, so one narrow spike in the critic cannot be exploited.
  4. The nudge sits only inside the target, not in the torques the arm plays, so the critic learns that torques close together score about the same and the actor has no lone spike to climb into.
  5. TD3 trains two critics side by side and builds the target from the lower of their two scores.
  6. This is called clipped double Q; clipped there means capped by the lower of the two scores, unlike the nudge's clip, which holds it within a limit.
  7. Both critics learn from the same batches and the same target.
  8. TD3's delayed update moves the actor and the slow copies only once every \(d\) critic updates, where \(d\), the delay, is 2, so the actor climbs a critic that has settled.
  9. The lower score is the safer side, because a torque the critic underrates is simply avoided by the actor, so that error is not chased and carried forward.

3 TD3's update, rule by rule

3.1 Smoothing the next torques

Replay keeps a memory of past steps, and training draws random batches from it. (Deep Q-Networks (DQN)) The discount \(\gamma\) sets how much a reward one step later still counts. (RL Basics) The DDPG critic's target is the step's reward plus the discounted score the slow critic gives to the slow actor's next torques. (Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG): Actor and Critic)

TD3 changes this target twice: it nudges the next torques and keeps the lower of two scores.

Equation 1. Smoothing the next torques. A critic that scores one exact set of torques can grow a narrow spike there, and the actor then climbs into the spike instead of learning good torques. A panel that judges a dive from several nearby angles, not one frozen frame, faces the same need.
1\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{n} = \operatorname{clip}(\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{\tilde\sigma}\,\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{\xi},\ \htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{-c},\ \htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{c})\)
Intuition. One exact set of torques can hide a narrow spike in the critic, so the target scores the torques around the pick too.
The mathematics. \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{n}} \) is the nudge, one small random shift per torque. Clip holds its result between minus \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{-c}} \) and \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{c}} \), the clip limit: a nudge inside stays as it is, and one past a limit is capped at it. \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{\tilde\sigma}} \) is the nudge's size, and \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{\xi}} \) is a fresh random draw from a standard normal bell, one per torque; standard means centred at zero with width one, so most draws land near zero. \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{\tilde\sigma}} \) times \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{\xi}} \) is a draw scaled to that size. So \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{n}} \) equals a draw scaled to the nudge's size, clipped between minus \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{-c}} \) and \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{c}} \). This nudge joins the slow actor's torques in Equation 1, line 2, which Equation 2 scores.
Example. Take the slow actor's next torques: the nudge shifts each one a touch, so the target scores a little neighbourhood, not one point.
2\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\tilde a} = \htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\mu_{\theta'}}(\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s'}) + \htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{n}\)
Intuition. The target must score the torques the arm will actually meet, smoothed, not the raw pick.
The mathematics. \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\tilde a}} \) is the smoothed next torques, the torques the target will score. \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\mu_{\theta'}}} \) of \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s'}} \) is the slow actor's next torques for the next state \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s'}} \), the 33 numbers describing the arm after the step, from the slow actor network with weights \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\theta'}} \); the prime marks the slow copy. Plus \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{n}} \) adds the nudge from Equation 1, line 1, one per torque. So \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\tilde a}} \) equals the slow actor's next torques plus the nudge. Adding the nudge means similar torques score about the same, so scoring a little neighbourhood flattens narrow spikes the actor could climb into, and Equation 2 scores these smoothed torques.
Example. Take one next state: the slow actor names its torques, the nudge shifts them, and those smoothed torques go to the critics.
3\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\tilde a} \leftarrow \operatorname{clip}(\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\tilde a},\ \htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{-1},\ \htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{1})\)
Intuition. A smoothed torque past the motor's limit is still a torque the arm cannot give.
The mathematics. \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\tilde a}} \) on the left is the smoothed torques. The arrow means is replaced by: the smoothed torques are replaced by the clipped version on the right. Clip keeps each torque inside minus \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{-1}} \) and \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{1}} \): a torque above \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{1}} \) becomes \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{1}} \), a torque below minus \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{1}} \) becomes minus \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{1}} \), and one inside stays as it is, so the target never scores a torque the arm cannot give. So each next torque gets a small clipped nudge, the nudge joins the slow actor's torques, and the result is clipped into what the arm can give. This \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\tilde a}} \) is what Equation 2 scores, and it is not exploration noise: the nudge sits inside the target, not in the torques the arm plays.
Example. Take a smoothed torque the nudge pushed past 1: clip pins it at 1 before the critics score it.
Take an arm whose hand is hovering just outside the moving goal, so the slow actor's next torques point the elbow a little harder. The nudge shifts each torque a touch in a random direction and the clip keeps them in range, so the target scores the torques around the pick as well as the pick itself, and a narrow spike in one critic cannot set the target on its own.

3.2 The lower of two critics

The nudged torques from Equation 1 go to both slow critics.

Equation 2. The lower of two slow critics. One critic on its own can score its own torques too high and lift the target, and the actor then chases a score that was never real. Two house appraisers valuing the same house, with the lower valuation setting the loan, face the same need.
1\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{y} = \htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{r} + \htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{\gamma}\,\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{\min_{i=1,2}}\ \htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{Q_{\phi'_i}}(\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s'},\ \htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\tilde a})\)
Intuition. One critic on its own can lift the target with a lucky high score, so the target trusts the lower of two.
The mathematics. \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{y}} \) is the target, the number the live critics' scores are pulled toward. \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{r}} \) is the reward stored with this step, earned when the hand is inside the moving goal. \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{\gamma}} \) is the discount, how much a reward one step later still counts. \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{\min_{i=1,2}}} \) takes the lower of the two scores, one from each slow critic: \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{Q_{\phi'_1}}} \) and \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{Q_{\phi'_2}}} \) each score the next state \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s'}} \) with the smoothed torques \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\tilde a}} \) from Equation 1, line 3. The plus joins the reward and the discounted future score. So the target is the stored reward plus the discounted lower score for the nudged next torques. This is the shared target both live critics learn toward in Equation 3.
Example. Take the two slow critics scoring the same nudged torques: the lower score sets the target, so one hopeful guess cannot lift it alone.
Take a step where the hand is near the goal, so one slow critic scores the nudged torques high, while the other, which has seen those torques miss before, scores them lower. The target takes the lower score, so the live critics are pulled toward the cautious number, not the hopeful one.

The TD3 paper writes \(\phi\) for the actor, \(\theta\) for the critics, \(\pi_\phi\) for the actor and \(\epsilon\) for the target nudge; we keep the series letters.

3.3 Training both critics

Each live critic then shrinks its own gap to that shared target.

Equation 3. Training both critics. Two critics that learn from their own scores chase themselves upward, each new target built from scores that are already too high. Two students grading against the same answer key, instead of against each other's drafts, face the same need.
1\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{L(\phi_i)} =\)
Intuition. Each live critic needs its own loss, since each has its own weights to move.
The mathematics. \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{L(\phi_i)}} \) is the loss for live critic \(i\), with \(i\) 1 or 2, so there is one loss per critic. The loss is a function of \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{\phi_i}} \), the live critic's weights: changing the weights changes the scores and so changes the loss. The equals sign points to Equation 3, line 2, which counts the loss against the shared target from Equation 2. So this line names each live critic's loss, and Equation 3, line 2 counts it.
Example. Take critic 1's weights: changing them changes its scores, so it changes critic 1's loss, not critic 2's.
2\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{\mathbb{E}}\big[(\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{y} - \htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{Q_{\phi_i}(s,a)})^2\big]\)
Intuition. The two critics must learn toward the same number, or they drift apart.
The mathematics. \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{\mathbb{E}}} \) is the expected value, the batch average: add the squared gaps over the batch and divide by its steps. \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{y}} \) is the shared target from Equation 2, the same for both. \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{Q_{\phi_i}(s,a)}} \) is live critic \(i\)'s score for the stored state \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{s}} \) and torques \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{a}} \). The minus takes the score away from the target, so \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{y}} \) minus \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{Q_{\phi_i}(s,a)}} \) is the gap. The square makes the gap always positive, so both directions count, and big misses count more. So this half counts each live critic's loss as the average squared gap to the one shared target. Sharing \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{y}} \) trains both critics toward the same number, so the actor climbs one agreed score in Equation 4, line 1.
Example. Take one stored step: both critics compare their own scores with the same shared target and shrink the gap.
Take a batch where most stored steps score close to their target, while a few steps where the hand swung wide miss by a lot. Squaring makes those few wide misses count for more than the many small ones, so the loss pulls the critics hardest where they are most wrong, and both critics move toward the same shared target.

3.4 The delayed actor and slow copies

Until that update comes, the actor keeps its old weights.

Equation 4. The delayed actor and slow copies. The actor climbs the critic, so it should move only once the critic has settled a little, and the slow copies should trail the live networks instead of jumping with them. A coach who lets players practise every session but changes the game plan only every other session faces the same need.
1\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{g_1} = \htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{\nabla_a}\ \htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{Q_{\phi_1}(s,a)}\)
Intuition. The actor needs a direction, not a number: which way to shift each torque so the score rises.
The mathematics. \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{g_1}} \) is critic 1's uphill direction for the torques, the way to shift them so its score rises. \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{\nabla_a}} \) is the gradient with respect to the torques, how the score changes as the torques change. \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{Q_{\phi_1}(s,a)}} \) is critic 1's score for the state \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{s}} \) and the torques \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{a}} \) the actor picks. So \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{g_1}} \) equals the gradient of critic 1's score, and it points the way each torque should shift to raise the score. This uphill direction is chained through the actor in Equation 4, line 2 to move the actor's weights.
Example. Take the elbow torque: critic 1's slope says which way to shift it to raise the score.
2\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{\nabla_\theta}\ \htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{J} \approx \htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{\mathbb{E}}\big[\,\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{g_1}\,\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{\nabla_\theta}\ \htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\mu_\theta(s)}\big]\)
Intuition. The actor's weights move through the torques, so the slope chains through the actor to the weights.
The mathematics. \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{J}} \) is the actor's expected return, and \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{\nabla_\theta}} \) \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{J}} \) is how \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#0e6b59}{J}} \) changes when the weights \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\theta}} \) move. \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{\mathbb{E}}} \) is the batch average, the same average Equation 3's loss counts. \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{g_1}} \) is critic 1's uphill direction, times \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{\nabla_\theta}} \) \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\mu_\theta(s)}} \), how the actor's torques move when its weights move, so the product is how the return changes through the torques. The \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{\approx}} \) sign means the batch average stands in for the true average. So the return's slope is about the batch average of the uphill direction times the actor's slope. This is the DDPG actor update using critic 1 only, with the lower score needed only inside the target in Equation 2, and it runs only every d-th update.
Example. Take one state in the batch: the slope through the torques times the slope through the actor gives the slope for the weights.
3\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{\phi'_i} \leftarrow \htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{\tau}\,\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{\phi_i} + (1-\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{\tau})\,\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{\phi'_i}\)
Intuition. The slow critics must trail their live critics, or the target chases the live networks.
The mathematics. \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{\phi'_i}} \) are the slow critics' weights, with \(i\) 1 or 2, the prime marking a slow copy. \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{\phi_i}} \) are the live critics' weights, and \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{\tau}} \) is the small share. \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{\tau}} \) times \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{\phi_i}} \) takes a small share of the live weights, and one minus \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{\tau}} \) times \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#8e44ad}{\phi'_i}} \) keeps the rest of the slow weights, the two shares adding to one, so each slow weight moves only a hair toward its live weight. The arrow means is replaced by: each slow weight is replaced by this blend. Here it runs every d-th update, not after every update. The slow actor's weights follow the same blend in Equation 4, line 4.
Example. Take one slow critic weight: it moves only a hair toward its live weight, so the target barely moves.
4\(\displaystyle \htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\theta'} \leftarrow \htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{\tau}\,\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\theta} + (1-\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{\tau})\,\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\theta'}\)
Intuition. The slow actor trails the same way, so the next torques it names stay close to the old ones.
The mathematics. \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\theta'}} \) are the slow actor's weights, the prime marking the slow copy. \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\theta}} \) are the live actor's weights, blended with the same small share \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{\tau}} \): \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{\tau}} \) times \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\theta}} \) takes a small share of the live weights, and one minus \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{\tau}} \) times \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\theta'}} \) keeps the rest of the slow weights, the two shares adding to one. The arrow means is replaced by, and it runs every d-th update, as in Equation 4, line 3. So the whole equation says this: critic 1 reports the uphill direction, the actor's weights move that way, and every d-th update both slow critics and the slow actor blend a small step toward the live networks.
Example. Take one slow actor weight: it moves only a hair toward its live weight, so its next torques barely change.
Take a batch where critic 1 has settled on which way the elbow torque should shift, while the slow actor still points the arm the old way. The actor's weights move a step along the slope, and the slow copies take only a small share of the move, so the target the critics chase next stays almost where it was.

4 One TD3 update on a Reacher batch

One TD3 update starts from a random batch of those stored steps.

Figure 5 shows one TD3 update as a loop of boxes, from a random batch to the slow copies blending. The batch reaches the slow actor; nudged torques are clipped; slow critics score them; lower score plus the reward make the target; live critics move toward it; every d-th update the slope moves the actor and the slow copies blend. Take one batch: its hand missed the goal on most steps, so the targets stay low and the actor moves only once the critics have settled.

One Reacher batch shows how the four equations join into one update. You have now seen each piece on the earlier cards, so this walkthrough follows one batch through Equations 1, 2, 3 and 4.
1\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=n}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{n}}} = \operatorname{clip}(\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=sig}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{\tilde\sigma}}}\,\htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=xi}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{\xi}}},\ \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=c}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{-c}}},\ \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlData{k=c}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{c}}})\)
Intuition. At stage 2 the update draws the nudge that will smooth the next torques.
The mathematics. \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{n}} \) is the nudge, one small random shift per torque. Clip holds its result between minus \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{-c}} \) and \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{c}} \), the clip limit: a nudge inside stays as it is, and one past a limit is capped at it. \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{\tilde\sigma}} \) is the nudge's size, and \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{\xi}} \) is a fresh random draw from a standard normal bell, one per torque; standard means centred at zero with width one, so most draws land near zero. \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{\tilde\sigma}} \) times \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{\xi}} \) is a draw scaled to that size. So \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{n}} \) equals a draw scaled to the nudge's size, clipped between minus \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{-c}} \) and \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#5d6d7e}{c}} \). This nudge joins the slow actor's torques in Equation 1, line 2, which Equation 2 scores.
Example. One nudge per torque is drawn and clipped small, so the smoothing stays a touch, not a shove.

The mathematics. \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\tilde a}} \) is the smoothed next torques, the torques the target will score. \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\mu_{\theta'}}} \) of \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s'}} \) is the slow actor's next torques for the next state \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#2471a3}{s'}} \), the 33 numbers describing the arm after the step, from the slow actor network with weights \( \htmlClass{eqt}{\htmlStyle{color:#a04000}{\theta'}} \); the prime marks the slow copy. 5 TD3's update in PyTorch

The four equations above become one update function below. An episode is one run from the start until it ends. (RL Basics) Each episode is also cut off after a fixed number of steps; that cut is not a real end, so the future value is kept.

import torch.nn.functional as F
def td3_update(s, a, r, s2, done, step):
    # smooth the next torques
    noise = (torch.randn_like(a) * target_noise).clamp(-c, c)
    a2 = (actor_t(s2) + noise).clamp(-1, 1)
    # the target from the lower slow score
    with torch.no_grad():
        y = r + gamma * torch.min(q1_t(s2, a2), q2_t(s2, a2))
    # both critics toward the shared target
    loss = F.mse_loss(q1(s, a), y) + F.mse_loss(q2(s, a), y)
    loss.backward()
    critic_opt.step()
    # the actor and the slow copies, every d-th update
    if step % d == 0:
        actor_loss = -q1(s, actor(s)).mean()
        actor_loss.backward()
        actor_opt.step()
        for target, source in ((actor_t, actor), (q1_t, q1), (q2_t, q2)):
            for t, p in zip(target.parameters(), source.parameters()):
                t.lerp_(p, tau)
  1. 1First, the smoothing of Equation 1: target_noise is the nudge's size and c is the clip limit. The outer clamp keeps each torque in range.
  2. 2Next, the target of Equation 2: under torch.no_grad() the target is a fixed number, not trained. The snippet assumes non-terminal steps, so the target always counts the discounted slow score of what comes next.
  3. 3Then, the loss of Equation 3: both live critics move toward the one shared target. F.mse_loss is the mean squared gap, as Equation 3 shows.
  4. 4Last, the actor and slow copies of Equation 4: every d-th update only, the actor loss is minus the critic's score of the actor's torques, so the code minimises minus the score, and the loop blends each slow copy toward its live network, one pair at a time, each slow weight taking a small share of its live weight.

7 How TD3 is trained and how it compares

7.1 One critic update per step, actor updates less often

Every step the arm takes stores one step and runs one critic update; the actor and the slow copies update every \(d\) critic updates. Exploration noise is a small random nudge added to the actor's actions while it learns, so the agent tries moves it is not sure about. (Training DDPG: Target Networks, Replay and Noise) While the arm acts, TD3 draws this noise fresh each step.

Each step costs one critic update over both critics on a batch of 100, about twice the critic work of one DDPG step; every second step adds one actor update and the slow-copy blends. Memory is DDPG's plus one extra critic and its slow copy: two live critics, one actor, three slow copies, plus the replay buffer.

Evaluation means testing the trained actor with exploration switched off, so the tests are noise-free. (Training DDPG: Target Networks, Replay and Noise)

Before learning starts, the first stretch of steps uses random actions, a warm-up whose length is in the settings table. Noise drawn fresh each step worked as well as DDPG's Ornstein-Uhlenbeck noise, a nudge with memory taught on Training DDPG. So TD3 keeps the simpler noise.

Stable-Baselines3, a widely used reinforcement learning code library, runs this update. It runs the same TD3 update, with two critics and a delayed actor.

7.3 TD3 against other methods on simulated robot tasks

A seed is one full training run started from its own random start. Each method was trained on every task from several seeds.

Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), another way to train an actor that makes small, careful updates and learns only from fresh steps, is one of the methods compared. TD3 reuses each stored step many times from its replay memory. Soft Actor-Critic (SAC), the method of the next page, is compared too.

The comparison runs seven simulated robot tasks in MuJoCo, a physics simulator used for robot tasks, through Gym, a standard collection of reinforcement learning tasks. Each task is a simulated body learning to move or balance: a running half cheetah, a one-legged hopper, a two-legged walker, a four-legged ant, a small two-joint arm reaching a goal point (Reacher), and a pole or double pole balanced on a cart. The Reacher in the comparison is a smaller simulated arm from Gym, not this one.

Taking the lower of two critics greatly reduces this overestimation (Figure 6). TD3 posted the best score on five of the seven tasks, tied at the ceiling on the pendulum, and trailed DDPG slightly on the double pendulum.

8 When TD3 fits

TD3 fits when the moves are continuous, old steps are worth reusing, and a deterministic actor is wanted, since one actor that writes its torques straight out is cheap to run at deployment. It is a near drop-in fix when DDPG's critic scores too high or training is unstable: the same networks and the same replay memory, with the three changes above.

When exploration is hard, or the settings must work across tasks without tuning, Soft Actor-Critic, the next page, is the better pick.

10 From one torque to a range of torques

TD3 fixes the critic, but its actor still commits to one set of torques in each state. SAC keeps two critics and the lower score and replaces the deterministic actor with a range of torques.
