k-Means Clustering

Find the clumps.

Your eyes already do this

Imagine I hand you a thousand dots on a blank page. No labels, no legend, no colors. Sort them into two piles, I say. You would not hesitate. Your eyes see which dots belong together before you have finished reading the sentence. You never learned a "pile detector". You just see the clumps.

k-means is the algorithm version of that glance, and it runs on maybe the simplest loop in all of machine learning: guess some centers, let every point join its nearest center, move each center to the middle of its joiners, repeat. The notebook in this series tries it on two crescent moons tangled together. I gave the toy two datasets that teach more than moons: round clumps, where k-means shines, and long streaks, where it falls on its face. The failure is the lesson.

Two steps, repeated

That is the entire algorithm, and I am not simplifying. Step one: every point picks the nearest center. Step two: every center jumps to the middle of the points that picked it. Nobody trains a network, nobody computes a gradient, nobody tunes a learning rate. The centers just walk, round by round, until they stop moving. The notebook in this series runs this on 5,000 noisy moon points and the centers settle after a handful of rounds. My toy below uses 600 points so your browser stays happy, and two honest datasets instead of moons: three round delivery zones, and two long delivery routes.

Assign, then move

Two equations, one per step. First, the assignment: every point joins its nearest center.

$$ z_i = \arg\min_k \lVert x_i - \mu_k \rVert^2 $$

Term by term. $z_i$ is point $i$'s new membership card: the index of its cluster. $\mu_k$ is the current position of center $k$. $\lVert x_i - \mu_k \rVert^2$ is the squared distance from the point to that center, and $\arg\min_k$ picks the $k$ that makes it smallest. Squared distance, not plain distance, because the square root is monotonic and skipping it saves a fortune over thousands of points. This is a nearest-neighbor vote, nothing fancier.

Second, the move: every center jumps to the middle of its members.

$$ \mu_k = \frac{1}{|C_k|} \sum_{i \in C_k} x_i $$

Term by term. $C_k$ is the set of points that picked center $k$ this round, $|C_k|$ is how many of them there are, and the sum divided by the count is their average position: the middle of the clump. The new center is exactly that. If a center somehow loses every point, it stays put rather than dividing by zero (the notebook guards this too).

Then you check whether anything is still moving. The notebook stops when the centers' mean movement drops to 0.01 or below. My tool stops when even the most restless center moves 0.01 or less in a round. Either way, the loop ends the moment the centers get bored.

Where it shines, and where it fails

The notebook builds two noisy moons, drops two random centers on them, and lets Lloyd's algorithm walk. The numpy run settles at epoch 5, the PyTorch rerun at epoch 7; same idea, different random starts. On my zones with k = 3, the walk is over in 4 rounds: the centers land near (-2.36, -1.26), (0.05, 2.25), and (2.62, -0.83), one per zone, sitting dead center. This is k-means' home turf: round, separated, roughly equal clumps. Now switch to the routes with k = 2. Six rounds later the two centers sit near (-1.28, 0.08) and (1.86, -0.11): both parked on the middle line, each claiming the left or right half of both routes. k-means cannot see a street. It only knows nearest center, so it chops every route in half and hands each driver the wrong halves. The notebook also notes something worth pocketing: the boundary k-means draws between the clumps looks like a logistic or linear regression decision boundary. Same straight-line energy, arrived at with zero labels.

Let me hand you the three check values I verified the toy against, so you trust the animation:

  1. The first point of the zones dataset lands at (-2.878, -1.337): inside the west zone, which is centered at (-2.4, -1.2) with a spread of 0.5.
  2. On three points (0,0), (1,0), (10,0) with centers starting at (0,0) and (10,0), the assign step gives memberships [0, 0, 1] and the move step puts the centers at (0.5, 0) and (10, 0). The middle point joins the left clump, and the left center slides to the midpoint.
  3. Squared distance skips the root: the distance from (0,0) to (3,4) is stored as 25, and the argmin never notices the difference.

Here is the notebook's loop, stripped to the parts that matter:

def k_means(data, num_clusters, epochs, center_thr):
    # start: pick num_clusters random data points as centers
    centers = data[np.random.choice(num_samples, num_clusters, replace=False)]
    for epoch in range(epochs):
        # step 1: every point joins its nearest center
        distances = np.sqrt(np.sum((data[:, None] - centers[None, :]) ** 2, axis=2))
        assignments = np.argmin(distances, axis=1)
        previouse_centers = copy.deepcopy(centers)
        # step 2: every center jumps to its members' mean
        for cluster_idx in range(num_clusters):
            cluster_data = data[assignments == cluster_idx]
            if len(cluster_data) > 0:
                centers[cluster_idx] = cluster_data.mean(axis=0)
        # stop when the centers barely move
        if check_center_movement(centers, previouse_centers, center_thr) and epoch > 3:
            break
    return new_clusters, centers

Try it: two steps until nobody moves

This is Lloyd's algorithm on two datasets. Start with the zones, then switch to the routes. Each Play beat is one half of one round: first the points pick their nearest center and recolor, then the centers jump to their new middles.

How to play

  • The point: Two steps repeated until nobody moves: points pick the nearest center, centers jump to their points' middle. The failure mode is the lesson: the same loop that nails round zones falls apart on long routes.
  • What you will see: Three round delivery zones with the centers starting at random drop-off points. Play walks the rounds half a second at a time: on the assign beat the point colors snap to their nearest center, on the move beat the ringed centers jump to their new middles, and the walk stops when the centers barely budge. Then switch to the routes and run the same walk with k = 2, and watch it converge to the wrong answer.
  • Dataset (zones, routes): Restarts with that dataset. Start on the zones and watch k = 3 finish in 4 rounds with a center dead in each zone. Switch to the routes and watch the same algorithm park both centers on the middle line, chopping each route in half.
  • Clusters k (2 to 4): Restarts with that many centers. Try 4 on the zones and watch the east zone split in two while the others hold, then go back to 3 and watch it land one center per zone.
  • Step: Advances one beat by hand, so you can sit on the assign coloring and stare at it before the centers move. Reset puts the centers back at new random starts.

But what is k?

k-means has one awkward question it cannot answer for itself: what is k? You told the toy, and it obeyed. Give it k = 4 on three zones and it happily splits the east zone in two, because k-means finds clumps, not shapes. It does not know what a zone is. And k is only half the trouble: the routes fail with the right k, because the clumps it finds are always round. Next up is PCA, which stops looking for clumps entirely and looks for directions instead: the axes along which the data actually varies.
