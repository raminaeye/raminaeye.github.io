k-Nearest Neighbors (k-NN)

The classifier that trains nothing.

k-nearest neighbors: a classifier with no training step at all.

The laziest classifier that works

What if you skipped training entirely? No weights (the numbers a trained model adjusts until its answers improve), no loss function (the score that says how wrong an answer is), no gradient descent (the routine that nudges those weights, step by step, toward lower loss), no epochs at all (an epoch is one full pass through the training data, and this classifier never makes one). Nothing to fit.

You move to a new street and want to know which bakery is best. You could build a statistical model of bakeries. Or you could ask your five nearest neighbors and go with the majority. kNN is the second option, formalized: to classify a new point, find the k training points closest to it and take a majority vote.

There is no training to speak of. "Training" is just memorizing the dataset, the model is the data, and all the thinking happens at query time. That laziness is also its charm: there is nothing to tune except k, and as you are about to see, that one knob is the entire bias-variance tradeoff made visible: bias is how rigid the boundary is, variance is how much its shape wobbles with noisy points, and k trades one against the other.

How it works: distance, then a vote

Two ingredients. First, a way to say who the neighbors are: the ordinary straight-line distance from the query to each training point, measured across every feature (a feature is one measured coordinate of a point, one of the numbers that describe it). In 2D it is the hypotenuse you met in school; in n dimensions it is the same idea with more features.

Distance first: who counts as a neighbor.
d(x, q) = √( Σj (xj − qj)² )
For each feature j, square the gap between the training point x and the query q, the Σ sign (sigma) sums the squares across every feature, and take the square root of that sum: the ordinary straight-line distance, the hypotenuse you met in school, and the number that decides who the neighbors are.

Ranking by the squared value orders the points the same way, so when only the order matters the root can be skipped.

Second, the vote: take the k nearest and count the labels; whichever label is most common wins the query. That is the whole algorithm: sort by distance, take k, count. (k is usually odd, which is not a coincidence. It dodges ties.)

Gotcha, and it is a good one: kNN has no training step, so overfitting, fitting the noise in the data instead of the pattern underneath it, cannot show up as a loss curve doing something funny. It shows up at query time, as a jagged boundary. Small k listens to every neighbor, including the noisy ones, so the boundary wraps itself around individual points. Big k averages the noise away and the boundary goes smooth. The walkthrough in Classifying one query, step by step takes one query through the steps. The toy in The k dial trades bias for variance has one slider, k, and that one slider is everything it does.

And the gotcha that belongs with the distance step itself: k-NN compares raw feature values, so one feature measured in thousands drowns one measured in fractions. Normalize first, meaning rescale each feature onto a comparable scale so no feature wins just because its numbers are bigger, or the distance is a lie.

Classifying one query, step by step

The algorithm is three verbs: plot the query, find its neighbors, count the vote. Walk one query through all three, then the fourth move: change who gets to vote.

Then the vote: the neighbors decide.
ŷ = mode{yi : i ∈ Nk(q)}
Take a vote: look at the k nearest neighbors of the query q (the same q as in the distance card above, where x is a training point) and go with whatever label most of them have. ŷ is the query's verdict, yi is each voter's label, Nk(q) is the set of k voters around q, mode is the most common value, and ∈ means is a member of.

How to play

  • The point: One query, four steps: the query is plotted, its neighbors are found, they vote, and then a different voter set flips the verdict.
  • What you'll see: The winner depends on who votes, not on the data: the same query elects one color with a small voter set and the other color with a bigger one.

The k dial trades bias for variance

Same family as the data I used: 2D, two classes, two clusters (tight clumps of points) per class (I used 3000 points; the toy uses 800 so the boundary re-contours live). One slider, k, from 1 to 25. The diamond is the query point; it sits wherever it is placed, and its k nearest neighbors light up with the vote.

Here is what that slider does to the boundary before you touch it.

At 3 neighbors the boundary is jagged: little inlets and islands wrapped around individual points, including the noisy ones sitting in the wrong cluster. Smaller k overfits more, and now you can see exactly how. With 3 votes, one noisy neighbor swings the verdict.

At 17 neighbors the boundary is smooth: big clean regions, the noise averaged out. Same data, same distance, same vote. The only thing that changed is how many neighbors get a say.

This is the bias-variance tradeoff with the math removed. Small k: low bias, because the boundary can bend into any shape the data suggests, but high variance, because the shape wobbles with every noisy point. Large k: high bias, because the boundary can only draw smooth blobs, but low variance, because one weird point cannot move it. The slider is the tradeoff, and the boundary is the receipt.

  • Click to move the query: Click anywhere on the plot and the diamond jumps there; its k nearest neighbors light up with lines and the readout shows the vote split. On the contested ground between two clusters, a small k and a large k disagree.

The setup, for the record

For the record, my setup: make_classification (the synthetic-data maker in scikit-learn, a widely used Python library of ready-made machine learning methods) with 3000 points, 2 features, 2 clusters per class (a cluster is a tight clump of points sitting near each other, away from the other clumps), and a 90/10 train/test split: the points are divided so 90% are the training points the classifier memorizes and 10% are held back as test points it has never seen, so its score on them is honest. The two plots use 17 neighbors (smooth) and 3 neighbors (jagged overfit). The toy uses the same recipe with 800 points so every step re-contours live.

Five ways k-NN grows up

The plain vote treats the nearest neighbor and the farthest voter as equals. Weighted k-NN gives closer neighbors a louder voice, usually weighted by the inverse of the distance. When a far neighbor keeps spoiling a good verdict, weighting quiets it down.

Voting only works for labels. For numbers, k-NN regression averages the neighbors' values instead: same plot-the-query, find-the-neighbors steps, but the last step is a mean instead of a count.

Euclidean distance is the default, not a law. Manhattan distance adds up the gap along each feature one axis at a time, like walking city blocks, so it fits grid-like features, and cosine distance measures the angle between the two feature arrows, so it fits direction over magnitude, like text.

Naive k-NN scans the whole dataset for every query, which stops being cute around a million points. A KD-tree sorts the points into nested boxes by splitting one feature at a time, so a query only visits the boxes near it, and a ball tree does the same with nested balls of points instead of boxes; both index the points so most of the scan is skipped. Approximate methods like HNSW (Hierarchical Navigable Small World) link each point to its near neighbors in layered graphs, sparse long jumps on the top layers and dense short hops below, and a query hops across those links toward the closest points instead of scanning at all; it trades a little accuracy for a lot of speed.

k-NN assumes near means similar, and in high dimensions that breaks: everything is far from everything, so the nearest neighbors are barely nearer than the rest. This is the curse of dimensionality. k-NN shines with a few meaningful features, and wilts on a thousand raw ones.
