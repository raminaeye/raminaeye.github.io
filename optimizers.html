The simplest fit is a line

Fitting a Line with Least Squares

How do you fit a line through a list of data points? Obvious question, and a perfect warm-up for optimizers. You want parameters for the line, and each point misses the line by its residual: how far the point's height sits above or below the line's height at that x. Least squares picks the parameters that minimize the sum of those squared misses.

Squaring penalizes larger errors more and gives a smooth, convex loss (convex means bowl shaped, so walking downhill cannot get stuck anywhere but the bottom). For a line it also has a closed form: set the partial derivatives (each one the slope with respect to a single parameter while the others stay fixed) to zero, the normal equations, and out drops a formula.

Least squares for a line: the loss, and the fit it gives in closed form.
L(a, b) = Σi (yi − (a xi + b))2
L is the loss. For each point i, yi − (a xi + b) is its residual: the point's height minus the line's height at xi, where a is the slope and b the intercept. Each residual is squared and Σ adds them over all points.
a = Σ (xi − x̄)(yi − ȳ)Σ (xi − x̄)2
Setting both partial derivatives of L to zero gives this. x̄ and ȳ are the averages of x and y. The top of the fraction is how x and y move together, the bottom is how much x moves on its own.
b = ȳ − a x̄
The intercept b puts the line through the average point.

In NumPy, with one check on the fit: the residuals sum to zero, so shifting the line up or down cannot reduce the error any further.

xm, ym = x.mean(), y.mean()                               # x, y: NumPy arrays of the points
a = ((x - xm) * (y - ym)).sum() / ((x - xm) ** 2).sum()   # slope
b = ym - a * xm                                           # intercept
r = y - (a * x + b)                                       # residuals
print(r.sum())                                            # 0, up to rounding

Neural nets get no such closed form, so we walk downhill instead. Here is what breaks when you walk it the plain way, and the fix each optimizer adds.

Each optimizer fixes a failure of plain SGD

Gradient Descent and SGD

Gradient descent walks the parameters downhill. Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) does it on a random minibatch instead of the whole dataset: same update idea, noisier gradient, way faster per step.

One step reads the gradient on a minibatch and moves every parameter by the same learning rate lr times it:

for xb, yb in loader:                  # a random minibatch each step
    loss = loss_fn(model(xb), yb)
    model.zero_grad()
    loss.backward()                    # gradient on this minibatch only
    with torch.no_grad():
        for p in model.parameters():
            p -= lr * p.grad           # one learning rate for every parameter

The noise is cheap and helps: it shakes the walk loose from local minima, the low points that are lowest only nearby, not overall. Too large a learning rate zigzags or diverges, the loss rising instead of settling; too small crawls; with a constant rate SGD keeps bouncing around near a minimum.

The noise also helps at a saddle point:

A saddle point: the gradient is zero, but it is not a minimum.
L(x, y) = x2 − y2,   ∇L(0, 0) = (0, 0)
A two-weight toy loss L. At the origin its gradient ∇L, the slope in every direction, is zero, so the walk would stop there. Along x the surface curves up like a minimum; along y it curves down like a maximum.

High-dimensional loss surfaces are full of saddles. SGD can shake itself loose from them, while full-batch GD (gradient descent on the whole dataset at once) may get stuck because there's no noise.

SGD with Momentum

Momentum smooths the zigzag of SGD with a velocity v, a running average of past gradients, so updates keep a more consistent direction:

v = np.zeros_like(theta)
for t in range(steps):
    v = mu * v + lr * grad(theta)   # keep the mu share of the old velocity, add the new slope
    theta = theta - v               # step by the whole velocity

The momentum coefficient μ (mu, typically 0.9) is the share of velocity carried over: higher is like rolling downhill with less friction. The velocity builds up, so a step can be bigger than plain SGD's. It's slower to react but faster to converge in a consistent direction.

Nesterov Accelerated Gradient (NAG)

With NAG, the gradient is read not at your position but where the velocity is already taking you; the update is otherwise momentum's:

NAG in one line: momentum's update, but the gradient is measured at the lookahead point, where the velocity is already carrying the weights.
v ← μv + lr·∇L(θ − μv), θ ← θ − v
Same as momentum: v, the velocity, is μ (mu, the share kept) times the old velocity plus lr, the learning rate, times the gradient ∇L, and the weights θ then step by v. The one change: the gradient is read at θ − μv, the lookahead point where the velocity is already carrying the weights, so NAG sees a flattening slope early and overshoots less.

Momentum asks, "I'm here, what's the slope?" Nesterov asks, "I'm about to be over there, what's the slope?" That early warning makes it more stable and less oscillatory when the optimum is close.

Same update otherwise: only the point where the slope is measured moves.

AdaGrad

The next optimizers give every parameter its own step size: adaptive learning rates. A rare feature that barely ever fires wants a big step when it finally does; a feature that fires constantly wants small, careful steps. AdaGrad keeps, per parameter, a running sum of squared gradients and divides the step by its square root:

AdaGrad in one line: each parameter gets its own step size, so a rare feature can take a big step when it finally fires and a busy one takes small, careful steps.
Gt = Gt−1 + gt2, θ ← θ − lr·gt√Gt+ε
Gt is the running sum of squared gradients for one parameter: last step's sum plus gt squared, where gt is today's gradient. The step divides lr, the learning rate, by the square root of that sum, with ε (epsilon) a small guard against dividing by zero. Large gradients build a large sum and get small steps; rare, small gradients keep large ones.

The catch: the sum only grows, it never shrinks, so the learning rate shrinks forever.

RMSProp

RMSProp swaps the sum for an exponentially decaying average, so older gradients decay away and don't dominate forever. The denominator follows the recent gradient scale and adapts quickly when gradients grow or shrink, which helps with both exploding and vanishing gradients (a gradient whose size compounds, or shrinks away, as it travels back through the layers).

RMSProp in one line: AdaGrad's accumulator grows forever and its steps shrink forever, so RMSProp forgets old gradients and lets the step size track the recent scale instead.
Et = ρEt−1 + (1−ρ)gt2, θ ← θ − lr·gt√Et+ε
Et is a decaying average of squared gradients: it keeps the ρ (rho, the decay rate) share of the old average and mixes in the 1−ρ share of today's gt squared. The step is AdaGrad's, with Et in place of the sum and ε as the guard. Because the average forgets, the step size tracks the recent gradient scale, not the whole history.

Adam (Adaptive Moment Estimation)

Adam combines momentum with RMSProp's per-parameter scale. Per parameter it keeps two exponential moving averages (EMAs): the first moment, the mean gradient, and the second moment, the mean squared gradient. Both start at zero, so a bias correction inflates them back to realistic values early on. Four lines: smooth the slope, track each axis's scale, correct the startup bias, step.

Adam's two running averages: smooth the slope into momentum, and track each axis's own scale.
mt = β1mt−1+(1−β1)gt
The first running average, the mean gradient: the new average mt keeps the β1 share of the old average and mixes in the 1−β1 share of today's raw slope gt. The coefficient controls the smoothing: higher means I carry more velocity from the past. That smoothing is what steadies the zigzag path of SGD, so noisy steps cancel out.
vt = β2vt−1+(1−β2)gt2
The second running average, the mean squared gradient: vt keeps the β2 share of its old average and mixes in the 1−β2 share of today's slope squared, so it tracks the size of the gradient on each axis, which directions are steep. That per-axis scale is what later lets each parameter get its own well-scaled step.

Next, correct the startup bias:

The bias corrections: both averages, corrected for their zero start.
m̂t = mt1−β1t
The bias-corrected momentum: m̂t is mt divided by 1−β1t, the memory rate raised to the power of the step count t. That share starts near zero and grows toward the whole as the step count rises, so the division inflates the moment back to a realistic value early on, and as the step count grows, the correction goes away.
v̂t = vt1−β2t
The same correction for the per-axis scale: v̂t is vt divided by 1−β2t. The running averages start at zero, so this divides out the startup drag; as the step count grows, the correction goes away.

Then step:

Adam's step: the smoothed gradient, paced per axis.
θ ← θ − α·m̂t√v̂t+ε
The weights θ get replaced by themselves minus the step size α times the corrected mean m̂t divided by the square root of the corrected squared mean v̂t plus ε, the tiny stabilizer that guards the division. So each parameter gets its own well-scaled step: smaller steps on steep axes, larger ones on flat axes.

That is the theory. Here it is in motion: same hill, same start, three runners, one per archetype (plain, momentum, adaptive). The rest of the zoo is variations on these themes.

The first runner, plain SGD:

Plain SGD in one line: two moves, no velocity, no per-axis scaling.
θ ← θ − lr·∇L(θ)
Read it as the two moves: the weights θ get replaced by themselves minus the learning rate times the gradient of L, the loss, and that gradient is read on a random minibatch instead of the whole dataset. The lr in this line is that learning rate, the same step size the Adam lines above write as α. The learning rate is the same across all parameters; there is no adaptation, which is exactly the failure the rest of this page fixes.

The second, momentum:

Momentum in two lines: build the velocity, then step by the whole of it.
v ← μv + lr·∇L(θ)
The velocity v, a running average of past gradients, gets replaced by a share of itself, μv, where μ is the momentum coefficient, the share of the old velocity the line keeps, plus the learning rate times the new gradient of L, the loss. Accumulating that average smooths the zigzag path of SGD, so updates keep a more consistent direction.
θ ← θ − v
Then step by the whole velocity: the weights θ get replaced by themselves minus v. That is the glide: momentum glides down the ravine instead of zigzagging across it.

AdamW, Nadam, SAM: variations on the themes

AdamW (Adam with Weight Decay)

L2 regularization keeps the model simpler with a penalty that grows with the size of the weights; weight decay is the same pressure applied as a direct shrink of the weights toward zero each step. Adam implements L2 inside the gradient, which distorts the decay because it gets adaptively rescaled. AdamW decouples it:

Adam with L2 versus AdamW: where the weight decay term goes.
gt = ∇L(θ) + λθ
Adam with L2. λ (lambda) is the decay strength, and λθ is the gradient of the L2 penalty, λ times half the sum of squared weights. Added into the gradient, it flows into both moments, so the division by the square root of v̂t rescales the decay per parameter.
θ ← θ − α·(m̂t√v̂t+ε + λθ)
AdamW. Its moments see only the true gradient ∇L(θ). The decay λθ is subtracted directly, outside the fraction, so it is never adaptively rescaled.
# L2 added into the gradient, then rescaled per parameter by Adam
opt = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=1e-3, weight_decay=1e-2)
# decoupled: the weights shrink by lr * weight_decay * theta each step
opt = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=1e-3, weight_decay=1e-2)

So remember that weight decay is not L2 regularization for adaptive methods; that's why there's a separate AdamW optimizer.

Nadam (Nesterov-accelerated Adam)

Nadam borrows Nesterov's lookahead idea for Adam: in the step, blend the current gradient back into the momentum term.

Nadam's step: Adam's step with a fresh dose of today's gradient.
θ ← θ − α√v̂t+ε·(β1m̂t + (1−β1)gt1−β1t)
Same symbols as Adam's lines above. Where Adam's step uses m̂t alone, Nadam mixes the β1 share of m̂t with the 1−β1 share of today's raw gradient gt, bias-corrected the same way.

This isn't much like Nesterov, which actually moved the weights and read the slope there; it just adds a stronger dose of the current slope to the smoothed mean so it can react more quickly.

SAM (Sharpness-Aware Minimization)

SAM is not really an optimizer. It is a wrapper you bolt onto one. Plain optimizers chase the lowest training loss and can land in a sharp minimum that generalizes badly. SAM hunts for flat minima instead: valleys where the loss stays low even if you wiggle the weights a little.

It minimizes the worst-case loss in a small neighborhood, asking, "Would the loss remain low if I wiggle my weights a little bit?"

SAM: find the worst wiggle nearby, then descend from there.
δ = ρ·∇L(θ)‖∇L(θ)‖
Here ρ (rho) is the radius of the neighborhood, a new use of the letter. ‖∇L(θ)‖ is the gradient's norm, its length: square every entry, add them up, take the square root. So δ (delta) is a wiggle of length ρ pointing straight uphill, the worst case for a small radius.
gSAM = ∇L(θ + δ)
The SAM gradient is the slope read at the wiggled weights. The optimizer then steps from the original weights θ using gSAM.

In PyTorch it is two forward and backward passes per step. SAM wraps any base optimizer; here AdamW runs as usual but feeds gSAM into its moments instead of the raw gradient:

params = list(model.parameters())
opt.zero_grad()
loss_fn(model(x), y).backward()                          # 1. raw gradient
with torch.no_grad():
    norm = torch.sqrt(sum((p.grad ** 2).sum() for p in params))
    delta = [rho * p.grad / norm for p in params]        # 2. worst-case wiggle
    for p, d in zip(params, delta): p.add_(d)              #    shift the weights
opt.zero_grad()
loss_fn(model(x), y).backward()                          # 3. gradient at the shifted weights
with torch.no_grad():
    for p, d in zip(params, delta): p.sub_(d)              # 4. shift back
opt.step()                                               # step with the SAM gradient

SAM prefers the flat valley: wiggle the weights and the loss stays low.

One caveat: you can reparameterize a network, rewriting the same function with different parameter values, to make any minimum look sharper or flatter without changing what it computes, so flatness is a useful heuristic, a rule of thumb that helps without promising, not a guarantee.

Two training extras: schedulers and clipping

Learning Rate Schedulers

One learning rate for the whole run is almost never right. Too high and training blows up; too low and it crawls. Schedulers fix that by moving the learning rate around as training goes.

  • Step decay drops the learning rate by a factor every few epochs (an epoch is one full pass over the training data).
  • Exponential decay shrinks it smoothly by a fixed fraction every step or epoch.
  • Cosine annealing glides from the maximum down to the minimum along a cosine curve. It stays higher for longer, while exponential decay can shrink the rate too early and settle into suboptimal minima, low points that are not the best one available.
  • Warm restarts jump back to the maximum after each glide, to explore multiple basins, the valley regions of the loss surface.
  • One-cycle rises first, then falls, in one cycle: escape bad minima early, settle into flat minima later. Momentum moves the opposite way, so a large learning rate meets low momentum for large, exploratory steps.
Cosine annealing in one line.
ηt = ηmin + 12(ηmax − ηmin)(1 + cos(π·tT))
ηt (eta) is the learning rate at step t of a T-step schedule; ηmax and ηmin are its top and bottom. At t = 0 the cosine is 1 and the rate is ηmax; at t = T the cosine is −1 and the rate is ηmin.

All five ship with PyTorch:

from torch.optim import lr_scheduler
# pick one; each wraps the optimizer opt
sched = lr_scheduler.StepLR(opt, step_size=30, gamma=0.1)          # step decay
sched = lr_scheduler.ExponentialLR(opt, gamma=0.95)                # exponential decay
sched = lr_scheduler.CosineAnnealingLR(opt, T_max=100)             # cosine annealing
sched = lr_scheduler.CosineAnnealingWarmRestarts(opt, T_0=10)      # warm restarts
sched = lr_scheduler.OneCycleLR(opt, max_lr=0.1, total_steps=1000) # one-cycle, momentum too
sched.step()   # once per epoch (OneCycleLR: once per batch)

Gradient Clipping

Gradient clipping is the emergency brake for exploding gradients: pick a threshold c, and if the gradient is longer than c, shrink it to length c.

Clipping by norm: same direction, length at most c.
g ← g·min(1, c‖g‖)
g is the gradient over all parameters together and ‖g‖ its norm, its length. Below the threshold c the factor is 1 and nothing changes; above it, the factor c over ‖g‖ rescales the length to exactly c without turning the direction.

It sits between the backward pass and the optimizer step:

loss.backward()
torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), max_norm=c)   # rescale if longer than c
opt.step()

The model trains now: it descends without zigzagging off a cliff, and the schedule keeps the step size sane. Given that it trains, how far does scale take you? Scaling Laws covers how test loss falls as you spend more on data, parameters, or compute, and where the next chunk of spending buys the most gain.
