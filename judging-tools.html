Judging Tool Calls

A good judge must spot one wrong argument in an otherwise perfect call.

1 A call can look right and be wrong

Ask for a match for six players. One call sets player_count to 6, another to 5. The two calls match everywhere else: same tool, same shape, same other arguments. One is right and one is wrong, and nothing in the shape of the call says which.

The judge is a reward model trained once on pairs of right and wrong calls, then used as a fixed scorer at inference, with no state carried between calls. Within one judgment, the state is the call and the history being scored: request, tool catalog, and conversation so far.

A general judge trained on plain replies misses this. It reads the call as text, and the text looks fine. The paper's benchmark of such pairs shows general reward models fail on them, so the judge must be trained on tool calls themselves.

2 What makes a tool call good

Four checks decide whether a call is good: the right tool name, every required argument present, every value right, and the right call count, no missing call and no extra one.

The paper catalogs the ways calls go wrong: a wrong value, a wrong tool name, the wrong number of calls, a missing argument, a wrong type, and an extra argument. A wrong value is the quietest: the call runs, the tool answers, and the answer is for the wrong thing.

A tool call is a JSON object. A schema is the tool definition: its arguments, each with a type, and which are required.

{
  "name": "start_match",
  "arguments": {
    "player_count": 6
  }
}
{
  "player_count": "int",
  "mode": "str",
  "required": ["player_count"]
}
  1. 1The call: the tool name and its arguments.
  2. 2The schema: the tool definition, with each argument type and which arguments are required.

Checking calls by hand does not scale, and a general judge misses the quiet errors. A judge is a model trained to run these checks, and once trained it serves three jobs.

3 Teaching a judge from pairs

This section leans on the Reward Models page, section 3.1, for the Bradley-Terry model, and on the DPO page for preference optimization from pairs of preferred and rejected outputs.

Training starts with pairs: one request, a right call and a wrong one. The right call comes from the ground truth. The wrong call comes from a model asked to answer the request: keep the calls that differ from the ground truth.

A preference pair is one input, two outputs, and a label saying which output wins. Here the input is the request plus the tool catalog, and the outputs are tool calls. The judge is a language model with its last layer replaced by a head that outputs one number.

Equation 1. The Bradley-Terry preference probability. The chance the right call wins is the sigmoid of the score gap. It turns two scores into one chance.
\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt sym-p}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-p)}{P}}\big(\htmlClass{eqt sym-y}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-y)}{y_+}} \succ \htmlClass{eqt sym-y}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-y)}{y_-}} \mid \htmlClass{eqt sym-x}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-x)}{x}}\big)\)
The chance the right call wins. \(x\) is the request plus the tool catalog; \(y\) is a tool call; \(y_+\) is the right call and \(y_-\) the wrong call; \(\succ\) reads "is preferred to"; \(P\) is the chance the right call is preferred.
\(\displaystyle \quad = \htmlClass{eqt sym-wait}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-wait)}{\sigma}}\big(\htmlClass{eqt sym-r}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-r)}{r}}(\htmlClass{eqt sym-x}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-x)}{x}}, \htmlClass{eqt sym-y}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-y)}{y_+}}) - \htmlClass{eqt sym-r}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-r)}{r}}(\htmlClass{eqt sym-x}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-x)}{x}}, \htmlClass{eqt sym-y}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-y)}{y_-}})\big)\)
Only the gap matters. \(r(x, y)\) is the judge's score for a call; \(\sigma\) is the sigmoid, which turns any number into a chance between zero and one. Equal scores give even odds, and a wide gap means a near certain win. A small gap means the judge is unsure, and the training in Equation 2 widens the gap on every pair. The paper also writes this chance as a ratio: the winner's score, exponentiated, over the sum of both exponentiated scores.
\(P\) the win chance \(x\) the request and catalog \(y_+\) the right call \(y_-\) the wrong call \(r\) the judge score \(\sigma\) the sigmoid
The paper writes this rule as its equation 1, in section 3.2. The Reward Models page writes the same rule with \(y_w\) and \(y_l\) for winner and loser.

The judge learns by pushing the right call's score above the wrong call's score on every pair.

Equation 2. The reward model training objective. Push the right call above the wrong call on every pair.
\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt sym-x}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-x)}{J}}(\htmlClass{eqt sym-r}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-r)}{r}}) = \max_{\htmlClass{eqt sym-r}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-r)}{r_\theta}}} \htmlClass{eqt sym-x}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-x)}{\mathbb{E}}}_{(\htmlClass{eqt sym-x}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-x)}{x}}, \htmlClass{eqt sym-y}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-y)}{y_+}}, \htmlClass{eqt sym-y}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-y)}{y_-}}) \sim \htmlClass{eqt sym-p}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-p)}{D}}}\)
The objective, maximized over the judge. \(J\) is the objective; \(r_\theta\) is the judge with weights \(\theta\); \(D\) is the set of preference pairs; \(\mathbb{E}\) is the average over pairs drawn from \(D\). Each pair contributes one term to the average, the log of the chance from Equation 1.
\(\displaystyle \begin{aligned}&\log \htmlClass{eqt sym-wait}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-wait)}{\sigma}}\big(\htmlClass{eqt sym-r}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-r)}{r_\theta}}(\htmlClass{eqt sym-x}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-x)}{x}}, \htmlClass{eqt sym-y}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-y)}{y_+}}) \\&\quad - \htmlClass{eqt sym-r}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-r)}{r_\theta}}(\htmlClass{eqt sym-x}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-x)}{x}}, \htmlClass{eqt sym-y}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-y)}{y_-}})\big)\end{aligned}\)
One pair's term. When the right call scores above the wrong call, the chance is near one and its log near zero, so the pair is nearly solved. When the order is wrong, the log is large and negative, and the update pushes hardest there. The loss form on the Reward Models page is the same idea with a minus sign: minimize the negative log chance instead of maximizing the log chance.
\(J\) the objective \(r_\theta\) the judge \(x\) the request and catalog \(y_+\) the right call \(y_-\) the wrong call \(D\) the pairs \(\mathbb{E}\) the average
The paper writes this objective as its equation 2, in section 3.2. The DPO page trains directly from the same pairs without a separate judge.

Scores that drift make calls hard to compare, so training keeps them centered on zero.

Equation 3. The reward centering penalty. Keep the pair's scores centered on zero.
\(\displaystyle \begin{aligned}&\htmlClass{eqt sym-x}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-x)}{J_{\mathrm{reg}}}}(\htmlClass{eqt sym-r}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-r)}{r}}) = \htmlClass{eqt sym-x}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-x)}{J}}(\htmlClass{eqt sym-r}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-r)}{r}}) \\&\quad + \htmlClass{eqt sym-wait}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-wait)}{\eta}}\, \htmlClass{eqt sym-x}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-x)}{\mathbb{E}}}_{(\htmlClass{eqt sym-x}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-x)}{x}}, \htmlClass{eqt sym-y}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-y)}{y_+}}, \htmlClass{eqt sym-y}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-y)}{y_-}}) \sim \htmlClass{eqt sym-p}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-p)}{D}}}\end{aligned}\)
The regularized objective. \(J_{\mathrm{reg}}\) is the training objective with centering added; \(J\) is the objective from Equation 2; \(\eta\) is a small positive weight that sets how strongly centering counts; \(\mathbb{E}\) and \(D\) are the average and the pairs from Equation 2.
\(\displaystyle \begin{aligned}&\big[ \big(\htmlClass{eqt sym-r}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-r)}{r_\theta}}(\htmlClass{eqt sym-x}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-x)}{x}}, \htmlClass{eqt sym-y}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-y)}{y_+}}) \\&\quad + \htmlClass{eqt sym-r}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-r)}{r_\theta}}(\htmlClass{eqt sym-x}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-x)}{x}}, \htmlClass{eqt sym-y}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-y)}{y_-}})\big)^2 \big]\end{aligned}\)
The added term is the squared sum of the pair's scores. It is small when the two scores add to near zero and large when they add far from zero, so training keeps the rewards zero-centered and scores from different calls stay comparable. Without it, the judge could push both scores up together and the gap would stop meaning anything across pairs.
\(J_{\mathrm{reg}}\) the centered objective \(\eta\) the centering weight \(r_\theta\) the judge \(x\) the request and catalog \(y_+\) the right call \(y_-\) the wrong call \(D\) the pairs
The paper writes this penalty as its equation 3, in section 3.2.

4 Using the judge three ways

The trained judge is a fixed scorer. It serves three jobs.

4.1 Best of many

Sample several calls for one request, score each, and keep the highest. The judge costs one score per call, and the winner is often not the call the model wrote first.

4.2 Data filter

Score a pile of training examples and keep only the top half. The model that trains on the kept half beats the one that trains on the whole pile, because the judge drops the noisy calls.

4.3 GRPO reward

Use the judge's score as the reward that trains the policy by reinforcement learning. The policy no longer needs the ground truth: the judge's score stands in for it. The paper trains policies with GRPO on the judge's scores, and they match policies trained with ground truth rewards.

5 Practice

One judge training run, then one score per call judged, at training and at inference. The judge is trained once and reused everywhere: every pick, every filtered example, and every GRPO reward is one more score from the same judge.

The headline setting is the Bradley-Terry objective with the reward centering penalty (paper section 3.2), with the centering coefficient 0.01 (paper section 4).

The paper releases no official implementation. The training pairs and the benchmark are public as datasets: the FC-RewardBench benchmark and the ToolRM training data, both on Hugging Face, verified live.

6 Failure modes

Four failures cover most of what goes wrong, each with a cause, a visible symptom, and a fix.

6.1 A general judge misses the wrong argument

Cause: the judge was trained on plain replies, not tool calls, so it reads the call as text. Symptom: the wrong player_count scores above the right one. Fix: train the judge on tool call pairs, where only the argument differs.

6.2 A call that looks complete but is not

Cause: a missing argument leaves no wrong value to spot, only an absence. Symptom: a call missing a required argument scores well. Fix: train on missing argument pairs, so the judge learns that absence is also an error.

6.3 Scores that cannot be compared

Cause: the judge's scores drift away from zero over training. Symptom: the best of many picks change with the batch, not with the calls. Fix: the reward centering penalty (paper section 3.2).

6.4 A call the tool cannot run

Cause: the judge scores text and never runs the call, so a wrong type or an unknown name can read well. Symptom: a high scored call fails at execution. Fix: check the call against the schema, and pair the judge with a schema check when the score becomes a training reward (paper section 5.3.2).
