Probability and Bayes Rule

Updating a belief when new evidence arrives.

On this page

A test that catches 90% of cases says you have a rare condition. How worried should you be? About 9% worried, and the reason takes two core ideas of probability, careful counting and flipping a conditional.

1 Six ideas behind every model in this series

Every later model in this series, from naive Bayes to hidden Markov models, rests on six ideas. They are chance and averages, tables of joint and conditional chances, Bayes rule, independence, the bell curve, and the tilted 2D bell. Counting with tables and flipping a conditional with Bayes rule are what turn the 90% test into a 9% worry.

2 A random variable is a number chance picks

A die roll, tomorrow’s temperature, whether an email is spam (1 or 0): each is a random variable, a quantity whose value chance decides. A die has a list of values, each with a chance. A height can be any number, so it gets a curve instead, and chances become areas under it.

A random variable’s distribution, the chance of each of its values, follows two rules: the chances add to 1, and the average weights each value by its chance. X names the random variable. x is one value it can take.
Σx P(x) = 1
P(x) is the chance that X takes the value x. The big Σ with x under it adds that chance over every possible value, and the total is exactly 1 because some value always comes up. So chances compete: when one value gets more likely, the others must lose the same amount.
∫ p(x) dx = 1
For a quantity that can land anywhere on a line, the sum becomes an area: the ∫ … dx sign measures the area under p(x), and that area is 1. Here p(x) is a density, a height, not a chance; this series writes chances with a capital P and densities with a lowercase p. One exact value has no width and so no area, which is why only ranges get chances and a tall, narrow density can rise above 1.
E[X] = Σx x P(x)
The expected value E[X] is the long-run average of X: each value times its chance, all added. Likely values pull it toward them, and a rare but huge value can still move it a lot, because its size multiplies its chance. A fair die gives 3.5, a value it never rolls. An expected count is the same weighted sum, taken over counts.

3 One table, read three ways

Suppose you log 100 days of rain and of whether your coworker carried an umbrella. One 2 by 2 table holds it all.

One table of joint chances answers three questions: how often two things happen together, how often each happens alone, and how often one happens given the other. x is the weather. y is the umbrella.
P(x) = Σy P(x, y)
P(x, y), the joint, is the chance of x and y together, one cell of the table. Adding along a row sums over every umbrella value, so each rainy day is counted once whatever the umbrella did. What is left, the marginal P(x), is the row total: y has been forgotten.
P(y | x) = P(x, y)P(x)
The bar | reads given, so P(y | x) is the chance of y given x, the conditional. Dividing by P(x) keeps only x’s row and rescales it to add to 1. Without the division the row would add to the share of rainy days instead. The rarer x is, the smaller the bottom, so each of its few days weighs more.
P(x, y) = P(y | x) P(x)
Multiplying the previous line back out gives the product rule: the joint is the conditional times the marginal. Read it as a two-step process, where chance first picks the weather and then picks the umbrella given that weather. Bayes rule is this line written two ways.

From the table, rain comes with an umbrella 80% of the time, but an umbrella means rain only 24 of 31 times. Those two conditionals point in opposite directions, and mixing them up is the most common mistake in probability.

4 Bayes rule flips a conditional

You usually know how evidence behaves given a cause (a test is positive for 90% of sick people) but want the reverse, how likely sickness is given a positive test. Bayes rule does the flip.

Bayes rule turns the chance of the evidence given a cause into the chance of the cause given the evidence. A is the cause. B is the evidence.
P(A | B) = P(B | A) P(A)P(B)
The chance of A after seeing B is the chance of B under A, times how common A was, divided by how common B is overall. P(A | B), the posterior, is what you want. P(B | A), the likelihood, is how often B shows up when A is true. P(A), the prior, is how common A is before you look. It is the product rule written both ways and solved for the posterior, so a rare A keeps the posterior small even when the likelihood is high.
P(B) = P(B | A) P(A)
The bottom, P(B), adds up every way to see B, starting with the ways where A is true.
+ P(B | not A) P(not A)
Then it adds the ways where A is false. Dividing by both turns the top into its share of all the ways to see B, so the posterior lands between 0 and 1. Many false alarms, positive tests on healthy people, make this second term large and pull the posterior down, which is how a good test can still leave a positive person at 9%.

Counting people instead of chances, a format Gigerenzer and Hoffrage (1995) call natural frequencies, makes the flip easy to see.

Figure 4. 1,000 people; the bar splits the positive tests.

A rare cause needs strong evidence. Suppose 1 die in 100 is loaded and rolls a six half the time (Durbin et al. 1998).

This snippet runs Bayes rule on the test example of Figure 4 and prints the posterior, 0.092; p_positive is the bottom of the card, every way to test positive.

prior = 0.01
catch_rate = 0.90
false_alarm = 0.09
p_positive = catch_rate * prior + false_alarm * (1 - prior)
posterior = catch_rate * prior / p_positive
print(round(posterior, 3))  # 0.092
  1. 1Sets P(A), P(B | A) and P(B | not A) to the numbers in Figure 4.
  2. 2Adds both ways to test positive, eq card in Section 4, lines 2 and 3.
  3. 3Divides to get P(A | B), eq card in Section 4, line 1.

5 Independent, or independent once you know the class

Some variables carry no news about each other: a coin flip and the weather. Then every row of the table has the same shape, and the joint is a product.

Independence turns a whole table into two short lists, because the joint becomes a product.
P(x, y) = P(x) P(y)
x and y are independent when the joint is just the product of the two marginals. Compare the product rule: P(y | x) has become plain P(y), because knowing x no longer changes it. That is why every row of the table has the same shape.
P(x, y | c) = P(x | c) P(y | c)
c is the class, such as a document’s topic. Under conditional independence the same product holds inside each class: once you know c, x and y stop telling you about each other, even if they move together across classes. Naive Bayes assumes this for the words of a document given its class c.

Naive Bayes treats words as independent once the topic is known. A hidden Markov model treats tomorrow as independent of yesterday once today is known.

6 The bell curve has a mean and a standard deviation

Heights, measurement errors and sensor noise pile up around a middle value. Averages of many small effects tend toward this shape, the Gaussian (the central limit theorem, Bishop 2006, sec. 2.3).

The bell curve gives every value a density that depends only on how many standard deviations it sits from the mean, and it falls off fast on both sides.
z = x − μσ
μ (mu) is the mean, the center. σ (sigma) is the standard deviation, the typical distance of a value from the mean; its square σ2 is the variance. z counts how many standard deviations x sits from the mean. Subtracting μ moves the center to 0, and dividing by σ puts wide and narrow bells on one scale, so z = 2 means two standard deviations out for any bell.
p(x) = 1√2π σ exp(−z22)
exp(·) is e, about 2.718, raised to the power in brackets. The power is minus z squared, so the curve is flat near the center and drops very fast in the tails, the same on both sides. π here is the circle constant 3.14…. The front factor only scales the curve so its area is 1, which is why a bigger σ makes the bell both wider and lower.

7 Two variables make a tilted cloud

Height and weight rise together, so many people plot as a tilted oval cloud, a 2D Gaussian described by one matrix. Its axes are the directions PCA (principal component analysis) finds, and a Gaussian mixture fits several such clouds at once.

For two variables, one covariance matrix holds both variances and the tilt.
Σ = σx2ρσxσyρσxσyσy2
Σ, in bold, is the covariance matrix, not the summing sign. It puts each variable’s variance, its standard deviation squared, on the diagonal and how the two move together off it. ρ (rho), the correlation, runs from −1 to 1 and sets the tilt. At ρ = 0 the off-diagonal is 0 and the cloud is an upright oval; as ρ nears 1 the cloud squeezes onto a rising line. The two off-diagonal entries are equal because x moves with y exactly as y moves with x.

Now slice the cloud at one x and keep only the points inside the slice.

Knowing x shifts your guess about y and narrows it: the slice is a smaller bell with its own mean and standard deviation.
E[y | x] = ρx
Here both variables have mean 0 and standard deviation 1. The slice’s mean E[y | x] moves ρ for every unit of x: at ρ = 0 knowing x changes nothing, and at ρ = 0.8 and x = 1 the best guess for y is 0.8.
σy|x = √1 − ρ2
σy|x is the standard deviation of y inside the slice. It is below 1 whenever ρ is not 0: at ρ = 0.8 it is 0.6, and as ρ nears ±1 the slice shrinks to a point on the line.

Figure 8. Drag the slice; purple is y inside it, gray is y overall.

This slicing, guessing a hidden value from a correlated one you can see, is the core of The Wiener Filter, which guesses clean audio from noisy audio.

This snippet reads one height off each bell with scipy.stats; Sigma is the covariance matrix Σ from the card, with the correlation off the diagonal.

from scipy.stats import norm, multivariate_normal
bell = norm(loc=0.0, scale=1.0)
print(bell.pdf(1.0))  # 0.242, a height, not a chance
rho = 0.8
Sigma = [[1.0, rho], [rho, 1.0]]
cloud = multivariate_normal(mean=[0.0, 0.0], cov=Sigma)
print(cloud.pdf([0.5, 0.5]))  # 0.231
  1. 1Reads one height off the bell curve, eq card in Section 6, line 2.
  2. 2Builds the tilted cloud from Σ, eq card in Section 7, and reads one height.

8 Common traps

  • Do not flip the conditional, because P(positive | sick) is not P(sick | positive). With a rare condition they differ hugely; Figure 4 shows by how much.
  • A density above 1 is not a bug; only areas under p(x) must stay at or below 1.
  • Treating correlated features as independent, multiplying P(x) P(y) when x and y move together, counts the same evidence twice, and the answer gets overconfident.
  • A slice of a table or a cloud is a conditional only after you rescale it by its total.
  • A value never seen in a small table gets chance 0, and one zero count wipes out a whole product. Smoothing, adding a small count to every cell, fixes it (see Naive Bayes).

9 How do you turn Bayes rule into a text classifier?

Put a document’s words in the evidence slot and its topic in the cause slot, add the conditional independence line, and you have Naive Bayes. Why counting words gives the right chances is the subject of Maximum Likelihood and EM.
