On a speakerphone call, your voice plays from the other phone’s speaker, bounces around that room and comes back into its microphone, so you hear yourself a moment later. An echo canceller removes it with a filter that learns the room’s echo path while the call runs, using the least mean squares (LMS) rule.

1 The echo problem

Builds on echoes (Reverberation and Dereverberation, section 2)

Call the far-end voice \(x\): it is what the speaker plays. The microphone hears the near talker’s speech \(s\) plus that voice after the room, the echo; that sum is the microphone signal \(d\). An adaptive filter \(w\) runs beside the room. It sees only \(x\), makes its own guess \(y\) of the echo, and the phone sends the leftover \(e\).

What the microphone hears, the filter’s guess, and what is sent.
\(\displaystyle d = s + \text{echo}\)
\(d\) is the microphone signal: the near speech \(s\) plus the echo, which is \(x\) convolved with the room’s echo path (convolution).
\(\displaystyle y = w \ast x\)
The guess \(y\) comes from an FIR filter \(w\), a short list of taps, convolved with the same far-end voice. If \(w\) equals the echo path, \(y\) equals the echo.
\(\displaystyle e = d - y\)
What goes back to the far end. A perfect guess leaves \(e = s\), the near talker alone. Section 2 finds the best \(w\) and section 3 learns it.

2 The best fixed filter

Builds on autocorrelation (Power Spectral Density, section 4), the closed-form solution (Linear Regression, section 2.3), eigenvalues (Eigenvalues, Eigenvectors and SVD, section 2) and the matrix inverse (Vectors and Matrices, section 8)

Take an echo path with two taps: the echo is a share of the far-end sample now plus a smaller share of the one before. A real path spans far more taps than can be drawn, but two taps can be drawn. Speech changes slowly, so neighbouring far-end samples move together. Every figure on this page uses this one input.

The bowl is steepest along the direction where both taps change together, and flattest along the direction where they change in opposite ways.

The best fixed taps.
\(\displaystyle w^{*} = R^{-1}p\)
\(w^{*}\) is the pair of taps with the least squared error, the bottom of Figure 2. \(R\) is the input’s autocorrelation matrix, how much each delayed input overlaps the others: each input’s power on its diagonal and the overlap of neighbours off it. \(p\) says how the mic signal lines up with each delayed input. The inverse \(R^{-1}\) undoes the overlap, so \(w^{*}\) comes out as the echo path itself. This is the Wiener filter in time; The Wiener Filter applies the same idea per frequency bin. Section 3 walks to \(w^{*}\), and section 4 reads the step limit off \(R\).

\(R\) only stretches the two directions of Figure 2: the direction where both taps move together a lot, the one where they move apart a little. Those stretch factors are its eigenvalues. That spread is what makes the bowl long and narrow. With white input \(R\) would be the identity and the bowl round; Figure 4 shows what that changes.

3 Learning it sample by sample

Builds on the gradient descent update (Gradient Descent, section 1.1)

On a real call \(R\) and \(p\) are unknown, and they change when someone moves the phone, which breaks time invariance. LMS never computes them. After every sample it nudges each tap a little in the direction that would have made that sample’s error smaller.

One LMS update, run once per sample.
\(\displaystyle y[n] = w^{\mathsf{T}} x[n]\)
\(x[n]\) holds the last two far-end samples, \([x[n], x[n - 1]]\). The small \(\mathsf{T}\), the transpose, turns the column of taps \(w\) into a row, so \(w^{\mathsf{T}} x[n]\) is each tap times its sample, added up: the convolution of section 1 at one instant.
\(\displaystyle e[n] = d[n] - y[n]\)
The leftover at this sample, the last line of section 1.
\(\displaystyle w[n+1] = {w[n] + 2\mu\, e[n]\, x[n]}\)
\(\mu\) is a step size, not a mean. \(-2 e x\) is the gradient of this sample’s squared error \(e^{2}\) with respect to the taps, so this is gradient descent on one sample with learning rate \(\mu\), the \(\alpha\) of Gradient Descent. A big error makes a big step, and the step points along the input pair. With a small enough \(\mu\) (section 4) the average of the taps settles at \(w^{*}\) of section 2.

That uneven walk is why LMS slows down on voice (section 8).

4 How big a step

Builds on the learning rate (Gradient Descent, section 1.2)

On this page’s correlated input single runs stop settling well below the limit for the average in the next card, “Where the edge sits.” On white input the edge sits higher, so it depends on the input.

Where the edge sits.
\(\displaystyle 0 < \mu < \frac{1}{\lambda_{\max}}\)
The exact condition for the average of the taps to settle, with the \(\mu\) of section 3. \(\lambda_{\max}\), the largest eigenvalue of \(R\), is how strongly \(R\) stretches its most stretched direction, the steep axis of Figure 2: the steeper the bowl, the smaller the safe step. It does not guarantee a single run, which needs a smaller step: the runs of Figure 4 already fail below it.
\(\displaystyle \mu < {\frac{1}{\operatorname{tr} R} = \frac{1}{\text{total input power}}}\)
\(\operatorname{tr} R\), the sum of the diagonal of \(R\), is the total input power and is never smaller than \(\lambda_{\max}\), so this line is proven safe for the average and can be read off the input without finding eigenvalues. For a single run it is a rule of thumb, not a guarantee: the run of Figure 5 blows up a little below it. Louder audio makes the safe step smaller, and section 6 divides it out.

Figure 5 runs LMS on this page’s input. Its second plot shows the power of the echo the filter still misses, in decibels (dB).

Raising \(\mu\) learns faster only up to a point: past it the taps swing wide and settle slowly, and a little below the mark they blow up. Lowering it learns slower and smoother, and at 0 the filter never learns. This run has no noise, so the taps land exactly on the target; with a noisy microphone a big \(\mu\) also leaves them jittering around it. The step used at rest and in the code is an illustrative pick, well below the mark. Real cancellers have no fixed default: Speex, an open-source speech library, computes its step per frequency (Liu et al. 2017).

5 Where it is used, and its limit

Acoustic echo cancellers (AECs) in phones, laptops and conference speakers run this loop for every call. The structure of Figure 1 removes any noise that a second input hears alone: a 1965 canceller took 60 Hz mains hum out of an electrocardiogram (ECG) with a reference input that picked up the hum alone. Like the best fixed filter, LMS assumes access to that reference or to the echo response, while a single recording of a reverberant room has only the wet sound, the voice already mixed with the room’s echoes (Anushiravani 2016). Removing a room from it takes late-tail removal (Reverberation and Dereverberation, section 9) or non-negative matrix factorization with a room model (NMF, section 7.4).

6 In a real echo canceller

A canceller runs for the whole call, every sample. Each tap costs about 2 multiplies per sample, one for the guess and one for the update.

The cost of plain LMS each second.
\(\displaystyle \text{multiplies per second} = {2 \cdot L \cdot f_{s}}\)
Two multiplies for each of \(L\) taps, \(f_{s}\) samples a second. A longer path needs more taps, and a higher sample rate runs those taps more often each second, so the count grows with both. So Speex runs a frequency-domain block version, the multidelay block frequency-domain (MDF) filter (Valin 2006).

The tool of Figure 5 shows the other problem: a louder far end pulls the safe mark down in proportion, so a step that is safe in a quiet moment blows up in a loud one. Real cancellers fix this by dividing the step by the input’s recent power.

Normalized LMS (NLMS): the LMS step divided by how loud the input is right now.
\(\displaystyle w[n+1] = {w[n] + \frac{2\mu}{\varepsilon + x[n]^{\mathsf{T}} x[n]}\, e[n]\, x[n]}\)
The last line of the LMS card with one change: the factor \(2 \mu\) is divided by \(x[n]^{\mathsf{T}} x[n]\), the sum of the squared far-end samples the filter holds, its power right now. \(\varepsilon\) is a small constant that stops a division by zero in silence; a larger \(\varepsilon\) slows learning in quiet passages. On average \(x[n]^{\mathsf{T}} x[n]\) is \(\operatorname{tr} R\), the number that set the mark of section 4, so this division takes the loudness out: one \(\mu\) works at every far-end power, and the four marks of Figure 5 become one.

NLMS still slows on voice, because dividing by the power does not undo the correlation of section 2. Recursive least squares (RLS) does: it re-solves the best fixed filter at every sample from a running estimate of \(R\), so it converges fast even on speech, on the order of \(N^{2}\) multiplies per sample for \(N\) taps instead of \(2 N\). SpeexDSP, Speex’s signal processing library, exposes its canceller as speex_echo_state_init and speex_echo_cancellation, and pyroomacoustics ships LMS, NLMS and RLS filters (Scheibler et al. 2018).

7 LMS in code

import numpy as np, matplotlib.pyplot as plt, scipy.signal as ss
def lms_demo(n_samples, true_path, near_level):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
    x = ss.lfilter([np.sqrt(0.75)], [1, -0.5], rng.standard_normal(n_samples))  # far-end voice
    path = np.array(true_path)                                            # true echo path
    d = ss.lfilter(path, [1], x) + near_level * rng.standard_normal(n_samples)  # mic: echo + near speech
    trR = 2 * np.mean(x ** 2)  # total input power, the trace of R
    w, mu, W = np.zeros(len(path)), 1.0 / trR / len(path) / len(path), []  # well inside the section 4 bound
    for n in range(1, n_samples):
        xn = x[[n, n - 1]]
        e = d[n] - w @ xn
        w = w + 2 * mu * e * xn
        W.append(w)
    plt.plot(W); plt.xlabel('sample n'); plt.ylabel('taps w'); plt.show()
  1. 1The imports, the far-end voice stand-in, unit power with neighbours correlated (seed 0), and the true echo path.
  2. 2The microphone signal is the echo plus a quiet stand-in for near speech.
  3. 3The three lines of section 3, once per sample, with a step size well inside the section 4 bound.
  4. 4Plot the two taps over time; both end on the true echo path.
