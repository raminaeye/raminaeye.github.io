1 Why not just score replies by hand

Training a language model to give good replies needs a score for every reply it writes, including replies no person has seen, and nobody can score millions of replies by hand.

The running example is a prompt from the InstructGPT paper, "Explain the moon landing to a 6 year old". A model writes several replies to it, such as a simple story or a list of dates, and a person says which reply is better.

The InstructGPT paper is OpenAI’s work that trained its GPT-3 language model to follow instructions with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), a recipe that trains a model by trial and error against a learned score.

Scores on a rating scale drift between people; comparisons are more consistent.

A preference pair is one prompt, two replies, and a label saying which reply a person preferred.

A reward model is a language model with a one-number output, trained on people's comparisons so the reply they preferred scores higher.

Some papers call it a preference model.

A prompt and a reply go in, and one score comes out.

2 Learn a score whose gaps predict preferences

The reward model usually starts from a language model already trained on replies people wrote, a step called supervised fine-tuning (SFT); its word-prediction layer is replaced by a layer that outputs one number.

Only the gap between the two scores matters, so the story reply should score above the dates reply by whatever amount the data supports.

3 From score gaps to a training loss

3.1 Scores into win probabilities

The rule in the Bradley-Terry card, which turns the gap between two scores into the chance that one reply wins, is called the Bradley-Terry model.

It needs only the two scores, never a rating scale.

The sigmoid, written \(\sigma\), squashes any number into the range 0 to 1, so a score gap becomes a chance (Logistic Regression).

So when the story outscores the list of dates by a wide margin, the story is almost sure to win.

Equation 1. Bradley-Terry. The chance the winner wins is the sigmoid of the score gap.
\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt sym-prob}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-prob)}{P}}\big(\htmlClass{eqt sym-y}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-y)}{y_w}}\succ\htmlClass{eqt sym-y}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-y)}{y_l}}\mid\htmlClass{eqt sym-x}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-x)}{x}}\big)\)
The example is two moon-landing replies, a simple story and a list of dates; the story should win. \(x\) is the prompt and \(y\) a reply; \(y_w\) is the preferred reply and \(y_l\) the rejected reply; \(\succ\) reads “is preferred to”; \(P\) is the chance a person prefers \(y_w\), so line 1 asks how likely a person is to prefer the winner.
\(\displaystyle \quad=\htmlClass{eqt sym-sigmoid}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-sigmoid)}{\sigma}}\big(\htmlClass{eqt sym-score}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-score)}{r_\psi}}(\htmlClass{eqt sym-x}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-x)}{x}},\htmlClass{eqt sym-y}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-y)}{y_w}})-\htmlClass{eqt sym-score}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-score)}{r_\psi}}(\htmlClass{eqt sym-x}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-x)}{x}},\htmlClass{eqt sym-y}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-y)}{y_l}})\big)\)
Only the gap between two scores matters; a bigger gap means a more confident “A wins”. \(r_\psi(x,y)\) is the reward model’s score for a prompt and a reply, with weights \(\psi\); \(\sigma\) is the sigmoid, so line 2 says that chance depends only on the gap between the two scores, and equal scores give even odds. This builds on Logistic Regression: the score gap plays the role of the logit, the single number the sigmoid turns into a chance.

The InstructGPT paper writes \(r_\theta\) for the reward model; this series keeps \(\theta\) for the language model being trained, and \(\theta\) stands for the weights of the model being trained.

So the bigger the score gap, the surer the win.

3.2 One ranking gives many pairs

\(K\) replies to one prompt, ranked best to worst by one person. Every pair from the ranking is a training pair. A batch is a group of training examples handled together. The InstructGPT paper put all pairs of one prompt into one batch element so the model would not overfit to a prompt it saw many times.

The loss below scores every pair from one prompt’s ranking.

Equation 2. Reward-model loss. The loss pushes hardest on pairs where the winner scores lower.
\(\displaystyle \htmlClass{eqt sym-loss}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-loss)}{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{RM}}}}(\psi)=-\,\htmlClass{eqt sym-avg}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-avg)}{\mathbb{E}_{x}}}\Bigg[\dfrac{1}{\htmlClass{eqt sym-count}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-count)}{\binom{K}{2}}}}\sum_{(\htmlClass{eqt sym-y}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-y)}{y_w}},\htmlClass{eqt sym-y}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-y)}{y_l}})\in\htmlClass{eqt sym-y}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-y)}{\mathcal{P}(x)}}}\)
Binary cross-entropy where the label is always “the winner wins”, averaged first over one prompt’s pairs and then over prompts; this loss is minimized. \(\mathcal{P}(x)\) is the pairs from prompt \(x\)’s ranking; \(\binom{K}{2}\) is the number of those pairs, with \(K\) the number of replies ranked for this prompt, so the bracket adds the log-sigmoid over the prompt’s pairs and divides by their number, and a prompt with many pairs counts no more than one with few.
\(\displaystyle \quad\log\htmlClass{eqt sym-sigmoid}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-sigmoid)}{\sigma}}\big(\htmlClass{eqt sym-score}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-score)}{r_\psi}}(\htmlClass{eqt sym-x}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-x)}{x}},\htmlClass{eqt sym-y}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-y)}{y_w}})-\htmlClass{eqt sym-score}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-score)}{r_\psi}}(\htmlClass{eqt sym-x}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-x)}{x}},\htmlClass{eqt sym-y}{\htmlStyle{color:var(--sym-y)}{y_l}})\big)\Bigg]\)
The example is one prompt’s ranking of \(K\) replies, giving all its pairs. \(\mathbb{E}_{x}\) is the average over labeled prompts; the log-sigmoid is large and negative when the winner scores lower, so the loss pushes hardest on wrong orderings. This builds on the Bradley-Terry card and Cross-Entropy Loss: it is the log of the Bradley-Terry card’s probability, with the winner always the label.

The InstructGPT paper writes this loss as an average over labeled (prompt, winner, loser) triples with the \(1/\binom{K}{2}\) in front, and trains all pairs of a prompt as one batch element; grouping the sum by prompt, as here, is the same loss as the paper trains it.

So the reward model learns to score the preferred reply higher on every pair.

Other pages call this loss the Bradley-Terry loss, after the rule in the Bradley-Terry card.

With the story ranked first, every reply below it gives a pair that the story wins.

5 A reward model and its loss in PyTorch

A token is a word or a piece of a word; a language model writes its reply one token at a time. (Aligning Language Models with RL)

A reward model is a language model, usually the SFT model, with its word-prediction layer replaced by one number. body is any module that turns token ids into hidden states. Hidden states are the numbers the model keeps for each token as it reads. The loss is minimized.

import itertools, torch, torch.nn as nn, torch.nn.functional as F
class RewardModel(nn.Module):                        # [1] language-model body + one-number head
    def __init__(self, body, hidden):
        super().__init__()
        self.body, self.head = body, nn.Linear(hidden, 1)
    def forward(self, ids, last):                    # score read at the last token
        h = self.body(ids)                           # (batch, length, hidden)
        return self.head(h[torch.arange(len(ids)), last]).squeeze(-1)  # torch.arange(len(ids)) counts the replies so each reads its own last token; .squeeze(-1) drops the extra dimension
def pairs_from_ranking(ranked):                      # [2] all K-choose-2 pairs
    return list(itertools.combinations(ranked, 2))   # (better, worse)
def rm_loss(rm, win_ids, win_last, lose_ids, lose_last):   # [3] the reward model loss card, one prompt
    gap = rm(win_ids, win_last) - rm(lose_ids, lose_last)  # line 2 of the Bradley-Terry card, inside sigma
    return -F.logsigmoid(gap).mean()                 # sum over the prompt's pairs / C(K,2)
  1. The reward model: a language-model body with a one-number head; the score is read at the last token.
  2. Every pair from one ranking, better first, as the Bradley-Terry card needs them.
  3. The reward-model loss card for one prompt: the gap is line 2 of the Bradley-Terry card, inside the sigmoid, and the mean is the sum over the prompt’s pairs divided by their number.

Each call takes all the pairs of one prompt as one batch, so .mean() is the sum over that prompt’s pairs divided by their number, the reward model loss card’s bracket; averaging these losses over prompts gives \(\mathbb{E}_x\).

6 Pushing too hard on a learned score

What happens when a language model is trained hard to raise this score?

Training a language model to raise a score, by letting it try replies and learn from how they are scored rather than from labelled answers, is called reinforcement learning (RL).

The language model being trained this way is called the policy.

Training against the reward model is a loop: the policy writes a reply, the reward model scores it, and the policy changes so that high-scoring replies become more likely.

Here the reward model’s score \(r_\psi(x,y)\) is that one score; the RL update is often PPO, proximal policy optimization.

Pushed hard enough, the policy finds replies the reward model over-scores.

The reference model \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\) is a frozen copy of the SFT model. (Aligning Language Models with RL)

KL measures how different two probability distributions are, zero when they match; the KL penalty charges the policy for drifting away from the reference model. (Aligning Language Models with RL)

So a reply far from the start must earn a lot more score to be worth it.

Frozen means its weights never change during training.

The reward model is only a stand-in, a proxy, for what people want, the gold standard.

In one large study, a large reward model plays the people’s part; smaller reward models trained on its labels are the proxies the policy is pushed on, with no KL penalty in the main runs, and the large model then scores the same replies.

The policy is pushed with RL or with best-of-n, which means writing several replies and keeping the one the reward model scores highest.

The large model’s score, the gold score, rises, then falls, as the policy moves further from where it started.

Here the distance, how far the policy’s replies have moved from what the starting model would write, is measured as the square root of the KL, which behaves like a distance.

Larger proxies hold up longer.

Stopping early means halting training before the policy moves too far from where it started.

A KL penalty acted like stopping early rather than a cure.

Best-of-n bends down faster with distance than RL.

A reward for length alone is a score that rewards longer replies and nothing else.

The gains are the improvements in true quality that full RL training brings.

A second study found that a reward for length alone came close to the gains of full RL training on several setups.

Reward hacking is when the policy finds replies that score high without being good (RL Basics).

The policy learned what the scorer likes, not what people like.

The picture of the two curves is the tool in Proxy score versus true quality at rest.

9 When a learned score is the right reward

A learned score is the right reward whenever the reward is a human opinion no rule can check, such as helpfulness, tone or harmlessness. It is not needed when a program can check the answer.

DeepSeek-R1, a model trained to write out its reasoning before it answers, trained on answers a program checked, uses such checks instead of a reward model for its math and code answers.

Opinions such as helpfulness have no such program.

11 Where the score goes next

RLHF uses this score to train the language model. DPO keeps this loss and drops the separate model. Constitutional AI and RLAIF change who labels the pairs, and DeepSeek-R1 swaps the learned score for checks a program can run.

Now that we have a score, how do we train the language model to earn it without breaking it? RLHF answers it.
