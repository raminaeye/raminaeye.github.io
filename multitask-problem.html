Gradient Conflicts in Multitask Learning

The three ways joint training breaks: scale, speed, and direction.

On this page

The naive recipe Try it: the naive sum Three diseases The map

The obvious recipe is linear scalarization: add the losses together with some weights, and run ordinary gradient descent on the sum.

It is the first thing everyone tries, and it looks completely reasonable. One model, K tasks, K loss numbers. Gradient descent needs a single number to minimize, so fold the K scores into one the cheapest way possible: a weighted sum. The weights are volume knobs, one per task, and the sum is the whole training objective.

But the sum carries a hidden promise. A step that lowers the total can secretly raise one of the terms, as long as the others fall by more. Nothing in the recipe forbids robbing one task to pay another. The weights are the only control over how much say each task gets in that bargain, and as you will see, they are a weak control.

The naive recipe, written down

Here it is as an equation, then symbol by symbol, then read aloud with real numbers.

$$L(\theta) = \sum_i w_i L_i(\theta) \qquad \theta \leftarrow \theta - \alpha \nabla L(\theta)$$

Symbol by symbol. \(\theta\) ("theta") is all the model's adjustable knobs packed into one list. \(L_i(\theta)\) is task \(i\)'s loss, one number saying how badly the model is doing on that task, where lower is better. \(w_i\) is a volume knob for task \(i\): \(w_i = 2\) makes that task count double. The big \(\sum\) just means "add up tasks 1 through K". \(\alpha\) ("alpha") is the step size, a small number like 0.001. The arrow \(\leftarrow\) means "replace with": take the current knobs, step downhill on the total loss, and that becomes the new knobs.

Read it aloud with real numbers, one computation per line. Two tasks: a depth loss of 600 and a classification loss of 0.4, both weights at 1.

  1. \(w_1 \cdot L_1 = 1 \times 600 = 600\)
  2. \(w_2 \cdot L_2 = 1 \times 0.4 = 0.4\)
  3. \(L = 600 + 0.4 = 600.4\)
  4. task 1's share: \(600 / 600.4 = 0.9993\), about 99.9%
  5. for task 2 to reach half the sum: \(w_2 = 600 / 0.4 = 1500\)

The sum listens to task 1 almost exclusively. Task 2 is a rounding error. That is not a bug in the arithmetic; it is the naive recipe working exactly as written. And the fix is not obvious: to give task 2 an equal vote you must crank its weight into the thousands, and the right number changes as training proceeds, because the losses themselves move.

Three diseases, one symptom

With equal weights, "train on the sum" is what most codebases do first. Sometimes it works. Often it quietly fails: one task stalls far behind what it could do alone, or all of them get worse. When the joint model loses to separate single-task models, researchers call it negative transfer. The failure is usually diagnosed from a loss curve, but the loss curve hides which of three different diseases you actually have. They look alike from the outside and demand different medicine.

Scale: the loud task wins every vote

A depth loss near \(10^3\) and a classification loss near 1 are just numbers to gradient descent. The task with big numbers produces the big gradient, which swamps the others. Hand-tuning weights means searching K knobs, and doing it again every time the task set changes.

The scale panel above starts level: at 1x the two bars match. Press Play and stage 2 sweeps the slider up: by 300x task 2 owns 99.7% of every shared update, and task 1's bar is a sliver you can barely see. Nothing about task 2 is more important here. It is just louder.

Speed: the fair deal expires

One task may be nearly solved in the first 10% of training while another improves until the end. The fast task's gradients shrink as it finishes, so the weighting that was fair at step 100 is unfair at step 10,000. The balance must move with training.

The speed panel starts at step 2 with both tasks near full strength. Press Play and stage 3 sweeps the marker across training: passing step 30, the fast task's gradient has shrunk to 19% of its start while the slow task still has 72%. No fixed pair of weights is right at both step 2 and step 100, which is why a weighting you tuned early in a run can be wrong by the end of it.

Direction: the arrows fight

Two gradients can point against each other (cosine < 0), so the average update contains a piece that actively raises one task's loss. Task gradients fight on the large majority of training steps, and a whisper turned up is still pointing the wrong way.

The direction panel starts at 0 degrees with the arrows in full agreement. Press Play and stage 4 rotates task 2's gradient: near 100 degrees the cosine turns negative and the badge turns amber, the arrows are fighting. Keep rotating to 180 degrees: the two gradients point opposite ways, the cosine reads -0.99, and the amber sum arrow pushes task 2 uphill, which is exactly what its negative score means. This is the disease no volume knob can fix. Scaling a gradient changes its length, never its direction.

The map for the rest of the series

GradNorm fixes scale and speed: it learns the weights \(w_i\) so every task is equally loud and nobody falls behind. PCGrad and CAGrad fix direction: when task gradients fight, they reshape the update itself. The two families work on different problems, so you can combine them. The comparison page lines them all up side by side on the same landscape.

All of this was geometry: arrows with sizes and directions, added together, sometimes fighting. The conflict is geometry. What are the moving parts?
