Pareto Fronts in Multitask Learning

No single best point, only trade-offs. Meet the Pareto front.

On this page

The betrayal test The failure, with numbers The Pareto lens The shortest blend The catch

The key question of this whole series, and I am asking it early: when is the average gradient a bad deal for one of the tasks?

Plain training walks along the average gradient $g_0$. That is the steepest way down for the average loss, and nothing says it helps every task. The previous page handed you the kit to check: norms, dot products, projections. This page puts that kit to work and draws the map it reveals.

The betrayal test

Picture two tasks sharing one set of knobs. You are about to take one small step in some direction $d$, and you want to know, task by task, whether the step helps or hurts. The answer is one dot product.

Why a dot product? The actual move is $\theta \leftarrow \theta - \alpha d$: you step opposite to $d$, scaled by the step size $\alpha$, which is positive. Task $i$'s loss changes by roughly $-\alpha \langle g_i, d \rangle$: slope times move, knob by knob. Since $\alpha$ is positive, the sign of the dot product is the whole verdict:

$$\langle g_i, d \rangle > 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \text{task } i \text{ improves}$$

Read the symbols. $g_i$ is task $i$'s uphill arrow, its gradient. $d$ is the direction you are considering stepping along. The dot product multiplies the matching entries and adds them up. Positive means $d$ points along task $i$'s uphill arrow, so the actual move $-\alpha d$ goes downhill for task $i$. Negative means the move goes uphill: task $i$ gets worse.

So the betrayal test is one line: whenever some task has $\langle g_i, g_0 \rangle < 0$, the usual update $d = g_0$ makes task $i$ worse. The majority outvotes a minority of one. Keep your eye on this dot product. Every method in this series is really just a different way of picking a $d$ so that no task's score comes out too negative.

The failure, with numbers

Two tasks share two knobs, so every gradient is an arrow with two entries, small enough to draw. These numbers are invented, chosen so we can draw everything. $g_1 = (2, 0)$ points east with length 2. $g_2 = (-1, 0.5)$ points west and slightly north, length about 1.1.

Plain training follows the average: $d = g_0 = \frac{1}{2}(g_1 + g_2) = (0.5, 0.25)$. Notice it leans task 1's way: task 1's arrow is longer, so it pulls the average toward itself.

Now the referee scores the step, one computation per line:

  1. Task 1's score: $\langle g_1, d \rangle = 2(0.5) + 0(0.25) = 1.00$. Positive: the step helps task 1.
  2. Task 2's score: $\langle g_2, d \rangle = (-1)(0.5) + (0.5)(0.25) = -0.5 + 0.125 = -0.375$. Negative: the very same step pushes task 2's loss up.

That is the entire conflict problem in one line of arithmetic: the loud task outvotes the quiet one. You can dial these exact vectors into the playground page later in this series and watch the same betrayal happen live.

The Pareto lens: many ways to be "optimal"

With one loss, comparing two models is easy: lower wins. With $K$ losses, model A can beat model B on task 1 while losing on task 2. Neither is "better," full stop. So "optimal" needs a new meaning.

  1. Pareto-dominated: some other point is at least as good on every task and strictly better on one. There is a free upgrade available, so why stay?
  2. Pareto-optimal: nothing dominates it. You cannot improve any task without hurting another. All the free upgrades are used up. The set of all such points is the Pareto set.
  3. Pareto-stationary: some blend of the task gradients cancels out to zero there. Every Pareto-optimal point is Pareto-stationary, just as every single-task minimum has $\nabla L = 0$.

Stationary deserves the equation treatment, because it is the target every method on the next pages is actually aiming at:

$$\min_{w \in \Delta} \left\| \sum_i w_i g_i \right\| = 0$$
  1. $w$ is the blend weights, one number per task.
  2. $\Delta$ (Delta) is the simplex: every way to split 100% among the tasks, weights at least zero and summing to one.
  3. $\sum_i w_i g_i$ is the blend arrow, the weighted mix of the task gradients.
  4. $\|\cdot\|$ is the length of that blend arrow.
  5. The equation says: at a Pareto-stationary point, there is some legal blend whose arrow has length exactly zero. The pulls cancel.

Here is the catch that motivates the rest of this page: the Pareto set is not one point. It is a whole curve of trade-offs.

The shortest legal blend

So how do you actually land on the frontier? The classical answer is MGDA (Sener and Koltun, "Multi-Task Learning as Multi-Objective Optimization," NeurIPS 2018, arXiv:1810.04650): blend the task gradients into the shortest possible combination and step along it. It is proven to reach some Pareto-stationary point.

MGDA's paper

Ozan Sener, Vladlen Koltun

Multi-Task Learning as Multi-Objective Optimization.

NeurIPS 2018 · arXiv:1810.04650

The authors' starting point: multi-task learning is inherently a multi-objective problem, because tasks conflict and force a trade-off. The standard compromise, a weighted linear combination of per-task losses, is only valid when the tasks do not compete, which is rarely true. Their move was to aim for Pareto-optimal solutions directly. The textbook MGDA (Désidéri, 2012) provably reaches a Pareto-stationary point, but it needs explicit per-task gradients and does not scale to deep nets. Their contribution is a Frank-Wolfe optimizer with an upper bound computable in a single backward pass, so the overhead is negligible, and they prove it still yields a Pareto-optimal solution under realistic assumptions.

New toy, new vectors: the tool below uses $g_1 = (2, 0)$ and $g_2 = (-1, 1.6)$. Every legal blend is $w \cdot g_1 + (1-w) \cdot g_2$ for some $w$ between 0 and 1, and its tip rides the dashed line between the two arrowheads. MGDA asks: which blend is the shortest? The answer has a closed form:

$$w^* = \frac{\langle g_2, g_2 - g_1 \rangle}{\|g_1 - g_2\|^2}, \quad \text{clamped to } [0, 1]$$

Read it aloud with the tool's numbers, one computation per line:

  1. $g_2 - g_1 = (-1 - 2, 1.6 - 0) = (-3, 1.6)$.
  2. $\langle g_2, g_2 - g_1 \rangle = (-1)(-3) + (1.6)(1.6) = 3 + 2.56 = 5.56$.
  3. $g_1 - g_2 = (3, -1.6)$.
  4. $\|g_1 - g_2\|^2 = 3^2 + (-1.6)^2 = 9 + 2.56 = 11.56$.
  5. $w^* = 5.56 / 11.56 = 0.481$ (to three decimals), which sits inside $[0, 1]$, so no clamping.
  6. The blend itself: $(3(0.481) - 1,\; 1.6(1 - 0.481)) = (0.443, 0.830)$, length about 0.94.
  7. And the scores at that blend: $\langle \text{blend}, g_1 \rangle \approx 0.89$ and $\langle \text{blend}, g_2 \rangle \approx 0.89$. Both positive. No task is hurt.

How to play
  • The point: Every blend of the two task gradients is one point on the dashed line between the arrowheads, and MGDA's step is the shortest one. This tool makes the shortest blend something you can watch shrink and bottom out, instead of taking it on faith from the formula.
  • What you'll see: Play sweeps the blend weight w from 0 to 1 in three stages, about 2.6 seconds each. A START pill marks the rest state. Stage 1: the gold blend arrow shortens as its tip rides the dashed line from g2 toward g1. Stage 2: the arrow stops at the gold diamond, where a gold ring lights up: MGDA's min-norm point at w approx 0.48, where the length bottoms out near 0.94 and both task scores read about +0.89. Stage 3: past the diamond the arrow lengthens again toward g1.
  • Try this: try w at 0.00: the blend is all of g2, and task 1's score reads -2.00. Slide slowly toward 0.48 and watch the length readout fall while both scores turn positive together. Then slide to 1.00: the blend is all of g1, and task 2's score reads -2.00.
  • Reset: the Reset button next to Play stops the sweep and restores the start: the slider back to 0.00, the blend back to the full g2 arrow, and the stage text back to Start here.

That bottoming out is the one-line takeaway: every blend lands on the dashed line between the arrowheads (the simplex), and the shortest one is exactly the step MGDA takes. Notice something the tool makes visible: at the min-norm point the two scores are equal, about 0.89 each. A blend cannot sit below both of its ingredients at once, so "search over blends" is really "protect the worst task," only cheaper.

The catch: which point does MGDA pick?

MGDA is proven to reach some Pareto-stationary point. But "some" hides a problem: which point you reach is decided by where you started, not by anything you chose. The average loss, the thing you actually wanted to minimize, is left to luck.

Think of the Pareto set as a menu of all the fair compromises. MGDA and PCGrad only promise to seat you somewhere on the menu; the kitchen picks the dish. Minimizing the average loss is ordering a specific dish. You want the dish you ordered, plus a guarantee that the kitchen is actually cooking it.

Closing that gap is exactly what CAGrad does.

One honest caveat before you go: Pareto-stationary is a weaker promise than Pareto-optimal. A blend can cancel to zero at a point that is only a standoff, not a true optimum, the same way $\nabla L = 0$ holds at a single-task saddle. MGDA promises stationarity, not the dish you ordered.

So the frontier is real, and there is a principled way to land on it. But none of this turns the loud task down: task 1's arrow is still twice as long as task 2's, and it still outvotes the quiet one in every average. Knowing the frontier exists is only half the job.
