This page builds on RL Basics and Aligning Language Models with RL. The terms below are the ones this page keeps using.

  • An agent is the learner that picks the moves. A state is the agent’s situation right now; a move, or action, is one choice the agent can make in a state; a reward is the number the world hands back after each move, good or bad. (RL Basics)
  • A token is a word or a piece of a word; a language model writes its reply one token at a time. (Aligning Language Models with RL)
  • Here the agent is the language model. Here the state is the prompt plus the tokens written so far, and the move is the next token. Here the reward is one number for the whole reply, given when the reply ends.
  • RL, short for reinforcement learning, is learning which moves to make from rewards rather than from labelled answers. (RL Basics)
  • A policy is the agent’s rule for choosing a move in each state; it may always pick the same move or pick by chance. The policy is the language model being trained to write replies. (RL Basics, Aligning Language Models with RL)

1People are the slow, costly part

The same pipeline, a different labeler

What. Picture people reading two summaries of one post and picking the better one. Each pick costs a person’s time, so collecting enough picks is slow and costly.

How. Keep everything else the same and swap only the picker: a strong language model reads the two replies and says which is better.

Why. When it works, labels arrive on demand at about a tenth of the cost of people’s labels, and training runs as it did with people’s picks.

Constitutional AI has a language model check harmful replies against a written list of rules. It swapped people out only for harm labels. Can an AI write every kind of label?

The running example is Reddit TL;DR, a set of Reddit posts with a short summary written underneath. A model writes two summaries of one post, and the task is to say which summary is better.

The cat laptop post and its two summaries follow the labeler from its first pick to its last failure.

Figure 1 shows the running example for RLAIF, reinforcement learning from AI feedback: a Reddit post about a cat knocking a laptop off a table, with two summaries underneath. The top box holds the post, and below it sit Summary 1, which says what happened and what the owner asks, and Summary 2, which only says someone has a cat and a laptop, with Summary 1 ticked better. Take the post itself: the summary that says what happened and what the owner asks is the better one, a label an AI can write.

A reward model is a language model with a one-number output, trained on people’s comparisons so the reply they preferred scores higher. (Reward Models from Human Preferences)

RLHF, reinforcement learning from human feedback, trains a language model to earn a score from such a network, trained on people’s comparisons of two replies.

2Ask a strong language model which is better

The AI labeler

What. Picture a strong language model handed a post, two summaries, and instructions to say which summary is better. It was not trained for this job; it answers from what it already knows.

How. It reads the post and both summaries, then answers “1” or “2”. The chance it answers “1” is the label.

Why. When it works, the better summary gets the higher chance, so the chance itself is a label the reward model can learn from.

2.1The labeler favors the first slot

Position bias

What. Picture the labeler shown the same two summaries twice, once each way round. A labeler with position bias leans toward the same slot across both orders, whatever the two replies say.

How. Show each pair both ways and average the two chances, so the lean toward the first slot cancels.

Why. When it works, the averaged label follows the summary, not the slot, and the better summary wins in either order.

Figure 2 shows the RLHF and RLAIF pipelines side by side, the same method with a different labeler. The left column runs through a person and the right column through an AI labeler. Take the cat laptop pair: a person would read both summaries and pick one, and the AI labeler does the same job by answering “1” or “2”.

3Labels from probabilities

3.1One label from both orders

The soft label

What. Picture the labeler answering “1” or “2” not with a hard pick but with a chance for each choice. That chance is the soft label: a number between zero and one that says how strongly the labeler leans.

How. Ask the pair both ways round, take the chance for Summary 1 from each order, and average the two.

Why. When it works, a pair the labeler finds close moves the two scores apart only a little, and a clear pair moves them far apart.

Here the reward model is trained with a cross-entropy loss on the softmax of its scores: softmax turns scores into chances that add to one, and the loss penalizes chances that miss the soft label, so the scores match the soft label instead of a hard yes or no (Reward Models).

3.2Skipping the reward model

Direct RLAIF

What. Picture the labeler rating each fresh summary the policy writes, on the spot, instead of first training a separate scorer. The rating is the reward itself.

How. Ask the labeler for a rating from 1 to 10 and take its expected rating, each rating weighted by how likely the labeler is to give it.

Why. When it works, the scores stay fresh as the policy’s summaries drift, because no trained scorer sits between the labeler and the reward.

SFT, supervised fine-tuning, trains the model on replies people wrote with the ordinary next-token loss; RL usually starts from that model. (Aligning Language Models with RL)

For summaries, the SFT model is trained on Reddit posts and the summaries people wrote.

An expected value is the average over everything that can happen by chance, weighted by how likely each outcome is. (Probability and Bayes Rule, RL Basics)

The labeler’s expected rating, once normalized, is the reward. The scorer it skips is the reward model trained on those soft labels.

The reference model is a frozen copy of the SFT model, a copy that never changes during training. (Aligning Language Models with RL)

KL measures how different two probability distributions are, zero when they match; the KL penalty charges the policy for drifting away from the reference model. The paper uses \(\beta\) 0.05. Raising \(\beta\) holds the policy closer to the reference model, and lowering \(\beta\) lets the reward pull it further away. \(\beta\) also scales the reward down by one minus \(\beta\). (Aligning Language Models with RL)

Equation 2. The direct RLAIF score before normalization. A reward model takes a second training run, and its scores go stale as the policy’s summaries drift. Take the cat laptop pair: the labeler rates each fresh summary on the spot, and that rating is the score.

The labeler puts little chance on the highest ratings and most of its chance on the middle ratings, so the sum is pulled toward the middle and the score sits below the score of a summary that says what the owner asks.

The RLAIF paper normalizes that score to run from minus one to one.

3.3How often the AI labeler agrees with people

AI labeler alignment

What. Picture checking the labeler against people: take pairs people already labeled and see how often the labeler picks the same summary. That share is AI labeler alignment.

How. Count how often the labeler picks the same summary people picked, over the labelled pairs.

Why. When it works, a high share means the labeler’s picks line up with people’s, and a low share means its labels are not worth training on.

The labeler’s prompt can carry a few worked example labels, called few-shot examples, or none at all, called zero examples, meaning no worked examples are given.

The downsampled preference set is the labelled pairs the paper kept from the training splits, the data splits used for training, cut down to three to four thousand pairs per task. The labeler is checked on those pairs.

Equation 3. Agreement with people. An AI labeler is only useful if its picks line up with people’s picks, so the labeler is checked on pairs people already labeled.

Each pair gives 1 when the picks match and 0 when they differ, and averaging the markers gives the share where the picks match. So the whole equation says this: agreement is that share of labelled pairs.
Example. Take the cat laptop pair: the person and the labeler both pick Summary 1, so the marker is 1.

Adding the markers over all the pairs and dividing by the number of pairs gives the share where the two picks agree.

The paper calls this share \(z_{\mathrm{acc}}\): the share of the labelled pairs where the labeler and the person pick the same summary.

4From both-order labels to an RL update

Figure 3 shows the whole pipeline as one chain. The labeler writes its reasoning first, called chain of thought, then its chances become a soft label. The order swaps, and the two soft labels average into one. A reward model trains on those labels. The SFT model writes fresh summaries for the reward model to score. Each fresh summary is one reply, one episode. The return, all the reward still to come, pushes each token. The value model guesses the score the finished reply usually gets and starts from the SFT model.

The RL step, for both the RLHF and the RLAIF policies, is REINFORCE with a baseline, not proximal policy optimization, which makes small updates so one batch cannot wreck the policy. The value model guesses the score at each token, and the update pushes each token up or down by how much the final reward beat that guess.

The RL run used batches of 128 replies, a learning rate of 1e-5, 8 passes over the data, sampling at temperature 0.9, and \(\beta\) 0.05. Higher temperature means more varied replies, lower temperature steadier ones. Higher learning rates move the policy faster but can overshoot; lower ones move it slower. Larger batches steady the update; more passes reuse the data. Training holds the policy, the value model and the reward model in memory at once; direct RLAIF swaps the reward model for the labeler.

PaLM 2 is the family of large language models used for labelling here: L is the large size, S is small, and XS is extra small. A checkpoint is a saved copy of a model. The run behind this page worked on three tasks: Reddit TL;DR summaries, helpful dialogue, where the model answers a person’s question, and harmless dialogue, where the safer reply wins. Its policy is PaLM 2 XS, and the policy and value model start from the SFT model. Its reward models start from PaLM 2 XS checkpoints, except the reward model for summaries, which starts from the SFT model. The main labels come from PaLM 2 L, every pair asked in both orders and averaged, and the direct RLAIF labeler is a PaLM 2 XS further trained to follow instructions.

5Both-order labels and direct scores

The code below reads the cat laptop post and its two summaries: the "1" vs "2" probabilities, the both-order label, and the direct reward.

The labeler function takes the prompt and returns the AI labeler’s next-token logits, the model’s raw scores for each next token before they are turned into probabilities; \(id_1\) and \(id_2\) are the token ids of “1” and “2”.

import torch
# [1] read "1" vs "2"
def labeler_probs(logits,
    id_1, id_2):
    pair = torch.stack([
        logits[id_1],
        logits[id_2]])
    return torch.softmax(pair, 0)
# [2] the both-order soft label
def both_orders_label(labeler,
    post, s1, s2, id_1, id_2):
    a = labeler_probs(
        labeler(post, s1, s2),
        id_1, id_2)[0]  # first
    b = labeler_probs(
        labeler(post, s2, s1),
        id_1, id_2)[1]  # second
    return 0.5 * (a + b)
# [3] the direct RLAIF reward
def d_rlaif_reward(rating_probs):
    r_ai = (torch.arange(1, 11)
            * rating_probs).sum()
    return 2 * (r_ai - 1) / 9 - 1
  1. 1Reads the “1” vs “2” probabilities.
  2. 2The both-order soft label card: both orders read and averaged.
  3. 3The direct RLAIF score card: the expected rating, scaled to run from minus one to one.

6How labeler size and order change agreement

Figure 4 shows one labeler judging sixty simulated pairs. Each dot is one simulated pair, placed by which summary the labeler leans toward, and the bar shows the share where the labeler agrees with people; a dashed trail marks a pick that flipped when the order swapped. Take one flipped pick: the labeler leans one way with Summary 1 first and the other way when the order swaps.

The bar grows when the labeler gets larger or when both orders are averaged. Take the large labeler with both orders averaged: the bar fills to about four fifths, while the extra small labeler with one order fills little more than half. The paper measured a similar climb: large 78.0, small 73.8, extra small 62.7 in percent agreement.

Each dot now sits at its averaged lean, the dashed trails are gone because no pick flips, and the bar fills further than in one order. Take a pair that flipped before: its dot now sits between its two one-order places, and no trail marks it.

7The AI labeler’s settings

Two settings shape the labels: the labeler’s size, whether it reasons before it picks.

The AI labeler plugs into the loop in two places: up front, it labels pairs to train the reward model, or during RL, it rates each reply the policy writes.

8When an AI labeler is good enough

The run that answers this question trained the same three tasks twice, once with human labels and once with AI labels, the same way otherwise, and compared the finished models by asking people which replies they preferred. Separately, the labeler’s agreement with people, called z_acc, was measured on the labelled pairs of the downsampled preference set.

On summaries the AI-labelled model won 71 in a hundred over SFT, against 73 for the human-labelled one, and on helpful dialogue 63 against 64. Head to head, about even at 50 on summaries and 52 on helpful dialogue. On harmless dialogue it did better: 88 in every 100 replies judged harmless, against 76 for the human-labelled model and 64 for SFT, a gap too large to be chance, which the paper calls statistically significant. Judging a reply is just asking the model a question, so the same model that writes replies can judge them. On summaries, RLAIF with a labeler the size of the policy was preferred 68 times in a hundred over the SFT model; on helpful dialogue, direct RLAIF with the model labelling its own training was preferred 66 times in a hundred. Direct RLAIF beat same-size RLAIF on summaries, 74 in a hundred over SFT and 60 head to head. The AI reward model agreed with people 74.2 times in a hundred, against 79.3 for the human-labelled one, yet the finished models matched.

  • You have a strong language model but few labels. The labeler writes labels on demand at about a tenth of the cost of human labels, but it must be strong: small labelers agree with people much less, as the size slider shows.
  • The labeler can read both candidates. The label comes from comparing two replies side by side, so a task where the labeler cannot see both replies at once does not fit this shape.
  • The reward model is trained once up front and stays fixed during RL, so labels go stale as the policy’s replies drift. Fresh labels from the labeler, or direct rating during RL, keep them current.

Those three points fit the cat laptop post: a strong model but few labels can label its summaries, and the labeler reads both summaries.

Comparing at equal length means judging replies of the same length. The paper found real failures: the RLHF-trained policy’s summary said the author was 20, though the post never says so, and the AI-labelled summaries sometimes ran on with repeated phrases.

10Learning from AI-labelled pairs without RL

Do we even need a reward model and an RL loop to learn from these pairs?

The cat laptop pair is a preference pair: one post, two summaries, a label saying which reply is better. Direct preference optimization (DPO) turns one pair into an update by raising the chance of the preferred reply and lowering the chance of the other, with no reward model and no RL loop.

Group relative policy optimization (GRPO) works on groups of answers, and DeepSeek-R1 replaces the labeler with a program where answers can be checked. GRPO writes a group of answers to one prompt, scores each one, and pushes the policy toward the answers above the group average. DeepSeek-R1 is the reasoning model trained that way: it answers maths and code questions, and a program checks each answer, so no labeler scores it.
